Mount Maunganui Reef — Amalgamating Design and the Constraints of Construction

Shaw Mead, Kerry Black and Andrew Moores ASR Marine Consulting and Research Ltd, PO Box 67, Raglan, New Zealand

Abstract

Mount Maunganui Reef, on the north eastern coast of New Zealand, has been built from sand-filled geotextile containers. The reef was specifically designed to enhance the surfing amenity at Mount Maunganui Beach.

Computer modelling was utilized to develop a reef design for the local wave climate. The final computer design resulted in a ‘Delta’ reef that would shoal incoming waves into a sharp breaking peak, or ‘A-frame’, and then peel fast and hollow for over 50 m in each direction, producing intense breaking surfing waves for competent surfers.

While the iterative computer design approach produced a reef shape for the site that optimized the typical surfing wave climate (i.e. 1-1.5 m straight-crested waves with 8-10 sec period), the reality of replicating this exact shape on the seabed was constrained by the limits of construction materials and methodology. In the first instance, scaled physical modelling was used to amalgamate the final computer design with the construction units, i.e. geotextile containers of up to 60 m long by 7.2 m wide and 3.2 m high (container volumes of up to 900 m*). The second stage was to ensure that the geotextile containers could be accurately positioned on the seabed in the configuration developed during the physical modelling. A novel method was developed to deploy multiple geotextile containers with sub-meter accuracy and was implemented successfully. As a result, the Mount Maunganui reef has consistently produced high-quality surfing waves, even when prior to completion. The characteristics of the waves breaking on the Mount Maunganui reef have proven the reef design and verified the results of both the physical and numerical modelling. This paper describes some of the novel design and construction processes that were applied to construct the Mount Maunganui surfing reef, and the results of the surfing waves that the reef as produced.

WBEND (Wave BENDing) and Bousinnesq model 3DD, from the 3DD Suite of numerical models (Black, 2001) were utilised for reef design and functional/impact assessment, both for resource consent application (Mead et al., 1998) and detailed design (Black and Mead, 2005).

The model WBEND (Black and Rosenberg, 1992) has been applied to a broad range of physical environments and has been calibrated and shown to be accurate on many occasions. The model has also been successfully validated and used as one of the primary design tools for the multi-purpose reef designed for the Gold Coast (Black et al., 1998), validated against surfing reef measurements (e.g. Mead and Black, 2001la, b), laboratory data (Black and Rosenberg, 1992) and in open coast conditions (Black ef al., 1998). In addition, significant modifications have recently been applied to model WBEND to simulate diffraction over complex bathymetric features (Black, unpub).

In 2004, final reef design work was undertaken, and due to the directionally variable wave climate at the Mount, a ‘Delta’ type reef was designed that would break waves from a single peak (an A-frame) and peel them in each direction for 50-65 m (Figure 2).

A database of over 40 mostly world-class surfing breaks was used to determine the peel angles and reef gradients in the Mount Reef design (Mead, 2000). The final reef design is symmetrical about the central axis and consists of 2 zones (Figure 2). There is a leading offshore “focus zone” which is low amplitude (with

Once finalised, the detailed computer reef design was used as the basis for physical modelling in order to amalgamate the design with the restrictions of construction materials and methods.

3 Physical Modelling

Physical modelling was undertaken in ASR’s 8.5 m x 4.5 m wave basin, with maximum water depths up to 0.6 m. Waves are generated from a water-filled compartment spanning the width of the pool. A heavy steel plunger is raised and lowered using an electric winch to form the waves. The generator is operated using a control box which can switch between manual and automatic and allows for the adjustment of timing, both the starting and holding of the generator at preset levels. This allows adjustments to the wave height and period. Wave heights up to 0.20 m can be easily generated.

The first simulations were undertaken using a 1:20 scale model of the computer-designed Mount Reef (Figure 3).

The results of waves breaking on the 1:20 scale computer-designed reef (Figure 2) were very encouraging, with left and right hand waves peeling down either side of the reef with fast and hollow characteristics. However, due to the short width of the crest, the wave barreled and broke for only a short distance (~30 m when scaled up to full-size), the reef had ended and the wave had “fallen off’ the end of the reef (Black et al. 2005). Modifications were undertaken to increase the width of the curving ‘wedge’ component at the back of the reef, which resulted in intense waves breaking the full-length of reef crest (Figure 4).

While the physical modelling provided very good evidence that the Mount Reef design would provide the quality of waves that it had been designed for, in reality, this shape could not be replicated with the materials and methods of the proposed construction — sand-filled mega-containers. Thus, the next stage of physical modelling considered using plastic tubing to replicate the shape of sand-filled mega-containers.

Plumbing tubing could be bought of the shelf to replicate a variety of mega-container sizes at a scale of 1:21. Tubing was taped together to mimic the 2:1 ratio

4 Construction

The Mount Reef was designed primary to provide high-quality surfing waves during clean _ swell conditions (with prevailing south west winds, wave conditions on the north eastern New Zealand coast are often small and clean (due to the offshore winds).

Previous multi-purpose reefs incorporating surfing amenity have utilised split-hull dredge and GPS to position the vessel and drop mega-containers to the seabed (e.g. Jackson, 2001). However, while this is a very cost effective method of multi-purpose reef construction, it is very difficult method for accurate placement the mega-containers. Gaps and humps between mega-containers disrupt the wave quality, especially at smaller wave heights, such as common at the Tay Street site in Mount Maunganui. The led to the development of the R.A.D. (Rapid Accurate Deployment) method of reef construction for the Mount Reef, which allows for the amalgamation of the constraints of construction with design details and tolerances (Black et al., 2005).

The RAD method is essential for minimisation of costs and risks. First, the reef is put together onshore and consists of the following components:

The Mount Reef was built in 2 separate halves, with 12 mega-containers on each half, ranging in size from 50 m long x 7.2 m wide x 3.2 m high (when filled) to 30 x 1.5 m wide x 1.0 m high (when filled). The twelve mega-containers were secured to _ the customised webbing, with a geomat underneath (to prevent differential settling), and then craned onto a barge (Figure 9).

Prior to deployment, manta-anchors were put into the seabed at precisely surveyed (i.e. an RTK system was used). The leader lines of the webbing holding the mega-containers together could then be threaded through the large eyelets in the heads of the mantaanchors so that the reef section could be winched into place.

Once a clear weather window of at least 4 days was identified, the barge with the first half of the Mount reef on board was towed around to the reef site (Figure 10). The leader lines of the webbing were passed through the manta-anchors and the entire half of the reef (approximately 80 x 30 m) was winched to the seabed. The novel RAD method of reef deployment was completed in 3.5 hours without any unforeseen problems and filling of the containers commenced.

The mega-containers were filled in situ, with commercial divers inserting a custom-made fitting into filling ports and then an 8/6 Warman submersible pump extracting sand from the nearby seabed and pumping some 70-80 m into the containers. A longneck digger sitting on a barge was used to both power and move the pump.

Several other components of both physical and numerical modelling have been verified with the actual results of waves breaking on the Mount reef during various phases of construction.

Continued research has included bi-monthly surveys of the reef, which have been used to calibrate sediment transport modelling (due to be published in a Scientific Journal later this year — Black et al., in prep), while the results of the waves breaking on the reef and the sand bars forming on the beach and around it have resulted in a large amount of numerical and physical modelling being validated and strongly confirms the design processes and research findings that have previously been published (e.g. Black and Mead, 2005, Moores et al, 2005; Mead and Black, 2001a, b).

6 Summary

It has been over 10 years since the Mount Reef was first considered, with the construction process now nearing completion. The detailed numerical modelling design was transformed through the used of scaled physical modelling in order to develop a layout for the sand-filled mega-containers that the reef is built of.

The physical modelling amalgamated the constraints of construction methods and material with the detailed computer design. While not yet complete, waves breaking on the Mount Reef during favourable swell conditions have validated a large amount of numerical and physical modelling and strongly confirms the
