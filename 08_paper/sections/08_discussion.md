# 8 Discussion

This section discusses what the computations and the evidence of Sections 3 to 7 show. The analysis started from eight working hypotheses (Section 1.3); their status is tracked separately in Appendix F, and the discussion below rests on the findings themselves.

## 8.1 What controls the surf

**The ramp, not the crest depth, sets the breaker.** The crest depth decides which waves break on a reef: at mean sea level a wave needs $H_0$ of 0.3 to 1.3 m to break on it, and above 1.6 to 5.2 m it breaks seaward of it (Section 4.2). How the wave breaks is set by the ramp that leads up to the crest. Across the eight modelled reefs with a known outcome, the ramp length ranks with the outcome at Spearman $\rho$ = 0.93 (permutation $p$ = 0.005), the mean seaward slope at 0.77 ($p$ = 0.039) and the median surf similarity at the break at −0.77 ($p$ = 0.039), whereas the crest depth does not ($\rho$ = 0.39, $p$ = 0.36). Under the same 1 m, 10 s wave, Palm Beach and Boscombe break at the same height and depth, but the 89 m ramp at 1:17 gives a plunging breaker ($\xi_b$ = 0.56, $Y$ = 2.05) and the 13 m rise at 1:3.1 a collapsing one ($\xi_b$ = 3.00, $Y$ = 1.05; Figure 3). The two built reefs that work, Palm Beach and Albany, both have long, gentle ramps (89 m at 1:17 and 99 m at 1:19.8) and no collapsing cases, while the steep reefs collapse in most cases (Bunbury and Boscombe in all, Mount Maunganui in 91 %; Table 2). For design, this turns the familiar question "how deep should the crest be?" into two: the crest depth for the wave heights to be caught, and a ramp long and gentle enough, 1:16 to 1:20, to make them plunge rather than collapse (Section 7.5).

**The profile must be read where the wave is ridden.** The Albany result depends on where the profile is cut. The automatic cut through the shallowest point crossed the −1 m take-off plateau, where the reef rises 6 m in 22 m (1:4), and put the best wave in the sample among the collapsing reefs; the cut through the long −2 m ridge along which the wave is ridden gives a 99 m ramp and a plunging, "extreme" wave (median $Y$ = 1.75), consistent with the reports of a hollow wave for intermediate-to-advanced surfers [@mercer2025]. The same applies to Cables and Borth (Section 2.4; Figure E1 (Appendix E)). A one-dimensional indicator is meaningful only on the wave path, and on the oblique and refracted swell of a real reef that path is itself uncertain.

**The depth after the break matters too.** The ramp shapes the wave up to the break; the bed shoreward of the break decides whether the wave keeps its shape. Surfers who rode Pratte's reported that the wave "hit the shallow reef quickly, then instantly went into deep water again" and would "mush out and disappear", and that the reef "was shaped like a V instead of a triangle" (`\cite{danharmon2026}`{=latex}; grade D). A narrow V leaves deep water between and behind its arms, whereas Albany's ridge stays nearly flat for about 80 m inside its outer face. Behind a break the wave decays towards a stable height of about 0.4 times the depth and stops breaking where the water deepens [@dally1985]; run on three shore-normal lines across each reef, this decay keeps the wave breaking over the reef for a median of 35 m at Albany and 23 m at Palm Beach against 5 m at Pratte's and 7 m at Bunbury, and the length ranks with the outcome ($\rho$ = 0.77, $p$ = 0.037; Section 4.3, Table 3). On a peeling reef the wave also breaks progressively along the arm, so the ride is better measured along the crest: Palm Beach's oblique crest keeps the wave peeling for a median of 84 m and Albany's ridge for up to 155 m, against 11.5 m at Pratte's and 9 m at Bunbury. This crest-line length alone does not rank the outcomes ($\rho$ = 0.62, $p$ = 0.15), because Boscombe and Narrowneck, which work rarely, also have long shallow ridges: a long arm is needed but the ramp must also give a surfable breaker. The calculation is one-dimensional and idealised, but the testimony, the geometry and the decay model agree: a surf reef should stay shallow for some distance shoreward of the break, with a filled, triangular planform rather than two narrow arms.

**Size enables the ramp.** Volume ranks with outcome ($\rho$ = 0.72 for the stated volume of ten built reefs, 0.93 for the eight models; Figure 6): the three reefs that do not work are 4,900 m³ or smaller, and both built reefs that work 25,000 m³ or larger. The mechanism suggested by the profiles is that volume buys ramp length (ramp length $\rho$ = 0.93, against 0.62 for relief, $p$ = 0.15): on a 4 to 5 m bed a 1:16 to 1:20 ramp needs 15,000 to 25,000 m³ per 100 m of reef. Volume is necessary rather than sufficient: Narrowneck, the largest by stated fill, works only rarely, and Burkitts works at 300 m³ because the natural platform supplies the relief.

**Peel angle and skill.** Refraction over the reef arms turns designed arm angles of 45 to 81 degrees into peel angles of 16 to 62 degrees at the break, and small waves, which break in shallow water, refract most (Figure 5). Every reef therefore serves intermediate to professional surfers (refracted Hutt ratings 3 to 9, mostly 5 to 9), and only one large-wave case at Borth reaches the angle of a learner's wave. Spilling breakers, the other beginner condition, are 0 to 29 % of the on-reef cases; a dedicated beginner reef would need a ramp of about 1:30 and 35,000 to 49,000 m³ per 100 m (Section 4.4). No built reef was intended as, or reported to be, a beginner wave.

**Tide and wave height.** Even given a suitable swell, the reefs are surfable for 0 to 0.70 of the lunar month (Palm Beach 0.70, Albany 0.62, Cables 0.55, Borth 0.19, the others 0.16 or less), because the crest is in the right depth only over part of the tide. Under the Israeli tide (spring range 0.40 m) the average does not rise (mean change +0.01), but the outcome becomes a function of wave height alone: for a given swell the reef works all day or not at all (Figure 7). Short periods soften the break: the surging share is 0.21 at 6 s against 0.41 at 10 to 12 s, which favours the short-period seas of the Israeli coast.

## 8.2 Sediment response and the nourishment confound

Standard predictors expect a salient behind every reef [@black2001; @ranasinghe2006b], but where surveys allow a test the observed response is much smaller: at Boscombe +3.4 m against 84 m predicted, a ratio of 0.02 to 0.06, and at Palm Beach 2 m or less against 114 m (Section 5.3). The reasons are physical and practical. Surfing crests 1 to 2 m below MSL transmit 41 to 80 % of the wave height, and short reefs of 12 to 132 m are bypassed by diffraction; and beach management moves whole frontages by more than any reef signal (Boscombe +72 m³/m in 2006-07). On the Gold Coast the reef effect cannot be separated from continuous nourishment (Section 5.4), so the claimed widening is not evidence of protection by the reef. The defensible role of a surf reef in coastal management is that of a control point within a nourishment scheme, adding amenity, not that of stand-alone protection (Section 5.6). At the same time, the reefs change the bed close to themselves, through settlement of 0.5 to 2 m, local scour and burial [@blacka2013, p. 72], which removes relief and degrades the wave.

## 8.3 Durability by material

Six of the seven geotextile or inflatable reefs suffered major damage or loss within a few years, and the seventh, Narrowneck, survives through renewal of its containers; none of the four rock reefs failed (Fisher exact $p$ = 0.015; Section 6.1). The stability estimates explain the pattern: single sand-filled tubes slide at $H_s$ of 0.34 to 1.65 m, heights that depth-limited seas reach inside every surf-reef crest, and a sealed base lets small tubes lift under breaking waves, whereas rock of 1 to 2 t resists the same waves by the Hudson formula (Section 6.3). The tearing mechanism itself is not modelled, and the reported causes are point damage, undersizing and construction faults. The comparison is confounded, because the rock reefs are the newer and better-funded projects, but the direction is consistent between the record and the calculations. Rock is not free of problems: Albany's core rock moved in a storm during construction and 18 rocks were repositioned after injuries [@sheil2026].

## 8.4 Overall rating of the built reefs

To answer the question that motivates the review, which reefs have delivered what they promised, this review rated each reef on three criteria: (A) surf quality, the quality and consistency of the wave reported or computed; (B) safety and longevity, the structural record of the material and any hazard to users; and (C) sediment and storm protection, any measured effect on the beach or on storm damage. Each criterion is scored from 1 (very poor) to 5 (very good), with 3 for mixed or neutral evidence, including "no measurable effect"; ? means that no usable evidence exists, and * that a signal exists but cannot be attributed to the reef. The reefs are ranked by A, with B as tie-break; C does not affect the rank, because it is mostly unknown. The scores are this review's assessment, based on the evidence cited in Sections 3 to 6 and Appendix C, graded A (measured), B (engineering report or thesis), C (journalism or surfer media) and D (forum or single anecdote) (Table 7).

Albany is the only reef that clearly delivers a high-quality wave, but with safety problems: reported injuries led to the repositioning of 18 rocks within a year of opening [@sheil2026]. The two other reefs classed as working do so only in a narrow set of conditions: Palm Beach needs an easterly or southerly swell above $H_s$ 0.8 m with $T_p$ above 7 s and a suitable tide [@prenzler2023], and Burkitts gives about 30 surf days a year by a researcher's estimate [@raisedwaterndb]. On most days, therefore, neither offers a better wave than the surrounding coast, although Palm Beach gives longer rides than the natural surf zone when it works [@hunt2022]. Those longer rides are measured in specific swell conditions; surfer testimony that singles out a "solid" session on the reef as notable [@thesurf2022], and the scarcity of surf footage from it, suggest that such days are occasional. Hard rock reefs (Cables, Burkitts, Palm Beach, Borth) score best on B, with no structural failure; geotextile and inflatable reefs score worst on B, with six of the seven at the minimum. Criterion C is unknown or confounded for every reef: no score above 3 survives attribution, and the only negative evidence (Mount Maunganui) comes from a press report. The rating thus repeats the central result of this review: a surf reef that works is large, built of rock and given a long ramp, and its benefit is surfing rather than beach protection.

## 8.5 Limitations and what would change the conclusions

The sample is small (8 to 13 reefs), and the outcome classes are this review's readings of sources that are mostly by the designers; every association is descriptive and confounded, because the large reefs are also the recent, better-funded rock reefs. No reef has a published wave climate at the structure, so the surfable fractions are shares of the time with a suitable swell, not days per year, and the reported surfable days are not comparable (Section 3.6). The geometry is idealised for six of the nine models, and the Albany surface is fitted to a design rule (low confidence). The profile results depend on where the cut is taken: for Albany, Cables and Borth the cut was moved to the surfed part of the reef, and for Borth, where no source locates the break, its position is a judgement (Figure E1, Appendix E). The incidence angle is assumed for five of eight reefs, and the wave model is one-dimensional linear shoaling with $\gamma$ = 0.78, normal incidence and no focusing; it stops at the break and so does not test the post-break depth. The @mead2001b fit is extrapolated for 32 to 100 % of the on-reef cases at every reef except Palm Beach. Three key sources [@hutt2001; @mead2001a; @black2001] were used only as reproduced by others. The Haifa analysis rests on a synthetic profile and on the author's unpublished field observations (Appendix G).

The conclusions would change with (1) measured surfable-day counts tied to a wave record at any reef; (2) a small reef that works, or a large rock reef with a long ramp whose wave fails; (3) a controlled before/after survey showing a reef-made salient at a site without nourishment; (4) a geotextile reef that survives a decade in the breaking zone without renewal; and (5) Haifa breaking depths that confirm the early breaking observed in the author's field observations (Appendix G; $H_s/d$ of about 0.35 to 0.40), which would move the recommended crest from 0.6-1.0 m to 1.2-1.5 m below MSL.

## 8.6 Implications for design

The findings point to one design rule set. A surf reef should be large enough to carry a long, gentle ramp (1:16 to 1:20 for an advanced to intermediate wave; never steeper than 1:12), should stay shallow for some distance shoreward of the break, with a filled planform rather than narrow arms, and should be built of rock. Its crest depth should be chosen for the local wave heights and the local tide, with a margin for settlement and burial, and its success should be judged by surfing, with any coastal benefit tested by before/after surveys with controls and not assumed. For Haifa, the small tide removes the tidal window and leaves wave height as the control, while the mobile sand makes burial the main risk (Section 7).
