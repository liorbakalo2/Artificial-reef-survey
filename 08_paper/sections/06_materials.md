# 6 Materials and durability

Surf reefs have been built from geotextile sand containers (GSCs, also called geotubes or bags), a sand-filled rubber bladder (the Airwave), quarried rock and concrete. This section compares their record, the wave loads in the breaking zone, and their ecology and cost.

## 6.1 Failure record

The author's insight (c) holds that soft structures do not survive long in the breaking zone. Table 4 summarises the fate of the reefs by material: six of the seven geotextile or inflatable reefs suffered major damage or loss within a few years, and the seventh, Narrowneck, survives only through continuous maintenance; none of the four rock reefs failed (Fisher exact test, $p$ = 0.015; derived in this review). The comparison is confounded, because the rock reefs are newer and better funded. The designer of several of these reefs now calls the sandbags "an evolutionary deadend" (Reid and Sumner, 2021) and writes that "the fatal flaw of the bags was their propensity for failure" (Mead, as quoted in Geosynthetics Magazine, 2023).

The GSC failures follow a common sequence: point damage from anchors, propellers, knives, debris or construction faults, then loss of fill and change of shape, because "as soon as even a single sand-filled container broke and lost sand, the whole structure changed shape due to the fluid nature of sand" (Mead, as quoted in Geosynthetics Magazine, 2023). At Boscombe the damage to one large container led to the loss of two, 12.5 % of the reef's volume, and the reef was judged "unlikely" to survive its proposed 25-year life "without multiple repairs" (Rendle, 2016, pp. 98, 121). The manufacturers' design life of about 20 years for outer-layer containers (Geofabrics, 2013, as cited in Blacka et al., 2013, p. 35) contrasts with one to eight years before major loss in the field.

The Airwave tore along a glued seam near completion in December 2019 (ABC News, 2019); the Hypalon rubber could not be welded (Bunbury Mail, 2022). Its founder states that the final design was filled with sand instead of air, which did not respond well to the waves, that the reef made perfect waves until a seam opened a couple of days after installation, and that the next version will be concrete (T. Bottegal, Airwave founder, pers. comm., 8 Oct 2026). The timing differs from the press record and the wave quality is unverified; both accounts agree that the seam, not the rubber, failed.

## 6.2 Wave loading in the breaking zone

Bar and Drimer (2023) computed the loads on a submerged, impermeable geotube with a numerical wave flume and defined a sliding index $SI = F_y/(W' - F_z)$ (horizontal force $F_y$, uplift $F_z$, submerged weight $W'$) and a variant $SI_c$ that adds the uplift of the dynamic pressure on the contact area. They found that "the most critical state, regarding the sliding stability of the Geotube, occurs, typically just before breaking" (p. 1), when an up-wave horizontal load coincides with uplift (p. 8), and advised assuming that the dynamic pressure penetrates under the tube (p. 10). They name "too-small size Geotubes" and "sand coverage" as the common causes of failure (p. 2). The tube is a rigid body in the model, which gives forces but not fabric stress or tearing. A later paper by the same author on tube tearing, mentioned to this review, could not be located; the analysis rests on Bar and Drimer (2023).

## 6.3 Stability calculations

This review estimated the loads by integrating the linear incident-wave pressure over a rigid tube section, calibrated against values read from Bar and Drimer (2023), and bounded the uplift ratio $U = \max F_z/W'$ between a sealed base (upper bound) and a base reached by the wave pressure ($U_c$, lower bound) (Appendix A). Under depth-limited breakers ($H_b = 0.78\,h$), tubes 1.0 to 1.65 m high reach $U = 1$ at base depths of 2.3 to 3.9 m and tubes 2.2 to 3.2 m high at 5.3 to 8.0 m (Figure 11a): the sealed-base uplift scales as $U \approx H/(2c\,\Delta\,D\cosh kh)$ ($D$ tube height, $\Delta$ relative submerged density, $c \approx 0.87$), so small tubes lift first. If the pressure reaches the base, $U_c \leq 0.22$ and the calibrated $SI_c$ of 0.37 to 0.56 makes sliding marginal against a friction coefficient of about 0.5 (0.48 measured bag on bag by Corvaro et al., 2022). The single-tube criterion of van Steeg et al. (2011), $H_s/(\Delta\sqrt{BD}\,(f\cos a + \sin a)) \leq 0.65$, gives critical heights of 0.34 to 1.65 m for the built reefs' tubes, which depth-limited seas ($H_s \approx 0.55\,h$) reach at base depths of 0.6 to 3.0 m, inside every surf-reef crest. By the Hudson formula with $K_D$ = 2 (US Army Coastal Engineering Research Center, 1984, as tabulated by Verhagen, 2012), rock of 1 to 2 t resists the same waves; the Albany armour ($D_{50}$ 0.94 m) is stable to $H_s$ 2.1 to 2.5 m but its core rock only to 1.1 to 1.3 m, consistent with the storm movement of exposed core rock during construction. A tube at the break sees 7,200 to 14,400 load cycles a day.

For the author's insight (e), that most wave power acts at the break and lifts the tube up and then down, which is why most tubes tear, the load part is supported: the critical state occurs just before breaking with simultaneous uplift (Bar and Drimer, 2023), and small or sealed tubes are lifted by ordinary breakers. The tearing part is not modelled by any source found, and "most tubes tear" is not supported, since the reported causes are point damage, undersizing, burial and construction faults (Table 4). Repeated lifting and flapping may fatigue fabric and seams ("the initial cause of failure is masked by the damage suffered during cyclic flapping and deflation of the container"; Jackson et al., 2012, p. 7), but this is a hypothesis that needs fabric and seam tests. Insight (c), that soft structures do not survive long in the breaking zone, is supported by both the record and the stability numbers.

## 6.4 Ecology and cost

Insight (c) also holds that geotubes support marine life and are the cheapest structure. Colonisation of geotextile is documented (seaweed on Korean geotubes after one year; Oh and Shin, 2006a), and the designer chose containers partly as a "good substrate for marine organisms" (Geosynthetics Magazine, 2023), whereas at Ashkelon a surfers' group stated that "nothing develops near the geotubes" (Rinat, 2019). Rock and concrete are colonised too, and bio-enhanced concrete is reported by its maker to more than double invertebrate species richness on a Haifa port breakwater (ECOncrete, n.d.; company data). No comparative study was found, so the ecological advantage of geotubes is a new, untested observation.

On capital cost the containers are cheaper: A$56 per m³ at Narrowneck against A$457 for the Cables rock (Blacka et al., 2013, Table 4.4, 2013 AUD), although the small or incomplete GSC reefs cost A$274 to A$545 per m³. Over the life of the structure the advantage shrinks: renewal (Narrowneck), removal (Pratte's, about $551,000, slightly more than its construction; Blacka et al., 2013, p. 119), plastic debris (Rendle, 2016, p. 122) and a short field life. "Cheapest" is supported for construction cost only; for a reef in the breaking zone, durability decides the choice (Section 7).
