# 6 Materials and durability

Surf reefs have been built from geotextile sand containers (GSCs, also called geotubes or bags), a sand-filled rubber bladder (the Airwave), quarried rock and concrete. This section compares their record, the wave loads in the breaking zone, and their ecology and cost.

## 6.1 Failure record

Hypothesis (c; Appendix F) holds that soft structures do not survive long in the breaking zone. Table 5 summarises the fate of the reefs by material: six of the seven geotextile or inflatable reefs suffered major damage or loss within a few years, and the seventh, Narrowneck, survives only through continuous maintenance; none of the four rock reefs failed (Fisher exact test, $p$ = 0.015; derived in this review). The comparison is confounded, because the rock reefs are newer and better funded. The designer of several of these reefs now calls the sandbags "an evolutionary deadend" [@reid2021] and writes that "the fatal flaw of the bags was their propensity for failure" (Mead, as quoted in `\cite{geosyntheticsmagazine2023}`{=latex}).

The GSC failures follow a common sequence: point damage from anchors, propellers, knives, debris or construction faults, then loss of fill and change of shape, because "as soon as even a single sand-filled container broke and lost sand, the whole structure changed shape due to the fluid nature of sand" (Mead, as quoted in `\cite{geosyntheticsmagazine2023}`{=latex}). At Boscombe the damage to one large container led to the loss of two, 12.5 % of the reef's volume, and the reef was judged "unlikely" to survive its proposed 25-year life "without multiple repairs" [@rendle2016, pp. 98, 121]. The manufacturers' design life of about 20 years for outer-layer containers (`\cite{geofabrics2013}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite[p. 35]{blacka2013}`{=latex}) contrasts with one to eight years before major loss in the field.

The Airwave tore along a glued seam near completion in December 2019 [@abcnews2019]; the Hypalon rubber could not be welded [@bunburymail2022]. Its founder states that the final design was filled with sand instead of air, which did not respond well to the waves, that the reef made perfect waves until a seam opened a couple of days after installation, and that the next version will be concrete (T. Bottegal, Airwave founder, pers. comm., 8 Oct 2026). The timing differs from the press record and the wave quality is unverified; both accounts agree that the seam, not the rubber, failed.

## 6.2 Wave loading in the breaking zone

@bar2023 computed the loads on a submerged, impermeable geotube with a numerical wave flume and defined a sliding index $SI = F_y/(W' - F_z)$ (horizontal force $F_y$, uplift $F_z$, submerged weight $W'$) and a variant $SI_c$ that adds the uplift of the dynamic pressure on the contact area. They found that "the most critical state, regarding the sliding stability of the Geotube, occurs, typically just before breaking" (p. 1), when an up-wave horizontal load coincides with uplift (p. 8), and advised assuming that the dynamic pressure penetrates under the tube (p. 10). They name "too-small size Geotubes" and "sand coverage" as the common causes of failure (p. 2). They describe the section of a partly filled tube after Bezuijen and Vastenburg: a flat underside, sides close to quarter circles and a semi-elliptical top, for filling ratios of 0.60 to 0.85 in practice and 0.8 in their validation (pp. 4, 6). The tube is a rigid body in the model, which gives forces but not fabric stress or tearing. A later paper by the same authors on tube tearing, reported in correspondence, could not be located; the analysis rests on @bar2023.

## 6.3 Stability calculations

This review estimated the loads by integrating the linear incident-wave pressure over a rigid tube section, calibrated against values read from @bar2023. The section is the partly filled shape they describe, for a fabric circumference taken from the reported tube diameter or fitted to the reported filled width and height; FA = 0.8 (their validation value) is the default, 0.6 and 0.7 test the sensitivity and 1.0 (a full circle) is a reference. The construction gives their validation tube a height of 4.0 m against the printed 3.8 m. The vertical-load index $U = \max F_z/W'$ is bounded between a sealed base (no wave pressure under the tube; upper bound) and a base reached by the wave pressure ($U_c$, lower bound). $U$ is read as the size of the alternating vertical load on the fabric and seams relative to the weight of the tube, not as a prediction of lift-off: no Israeli tube is known to have been lifted, whereas many tore, and interlocking with neighbouring tubes, sand around and over the base, and pressure reaching under the tube all reduce the net uplift. Under depth-limited breakers ($H_b = 0.78\,h$) at FA 0.8, $U$ reaches 1 at base depths of 2.6 to 3.6 m for tubes 0.8 to 1.0 m high and 6.4 to 8.8 m for tubes 1.8 to 2.3 m high, and stays below 1 within 9 m for tubes 2.6 to 3.3 m high (Figure 11a). The sealed-base value scales as $U \approx H L_b/(2\Delta A\cosh kh)$ ($L_b$ flat base length, $A$ section area, $\Delta$ relative submerged density), so small tubes are loaded most. Filling matters: at the same circumference a tube filled to FA 0.6 is lower and wider, with a longer flat base and less sand, so its $U$ is 1.5 to 1.8 times that at FA 0.8 (1.2 to 1.4 times at FA 0.7), and $U$ = 1 moves to base depths of 2.0 to 6.6 m for all tubes; a full circle has almost no flat base and $U \leq 0.11$. The flatter shape lowers the sliding index only slightly (lowest calibrated $SI_c$ 0.29 at FA 0.6, 0.34 at FA 0.8, 0.38 at FA 1.0, for the most severe period). If the pressure reaches the base, $U_c \leq 0.15$ at FA 0.8 (0.23 at FA 0.6) and the calibrated $SI_c$ of 0.34 to 0.56 makes sliding marginal against a friction coefficient of about 0.5 (0.48 measured bag on bag by `\cite{corvaro2022}`{=latex}). The single-tube criterion of @vansteeg2011, $H_s/(\Delta\sqrt{BD}\,(f\cos a + \sin a)) \leq 0.65$, gives critical heights of 0.38 to 1.65 m for the built reefs' tubes at FA 0.8 (0.33 to 1.40 m at FA 0.6), which depth-limited seas ($H_s \approx 0.55\,h$) reach at base depths of 0.7 to 3.0 m, inside every surf-reef crest. By the Hudson formula with $K_D$ = 2 (`\cite{cerc1984}`{=latex}, as tabulated by `\cite{verhagen2012}`{=latex}), rock of 1 to 2 t resists the same waves; the Albany armour ($D_{50}$ 0.94 m) is stable to $H_s$ 2.4 to 4.3 m on the faces of its surf cut (2.4 m on the steepest, 1:3.5; the gentler values are extrapolated) but its core rock only to 1.2 to 2.2 m, and depth-limited breakers exceed the armour value where the bed is deeper than about 3.1 m, consistent with the storm movement of exposed core rock during construction and the repositioning of 18 rocks in 2026 [@sheil2026]. The Cables rock (1.5 and 3 t) is stable to $H_s$ 3.1 to 6.0 m and the Borth rock (4.5 to 8 t) to 3.3 to 6.1 m. A tube at the break sees 7,200 to 14,400 load cycles a day. For the Israeli tubes (theoretical diameter 5 and 3 m; 6.4 x 2.9 and 3.8 x 1.8 m at FA 0.8), a storm of $H_s$ 3.3 m gives $U$ up to 0.69 and 1.13 and one of 5 m up to 1.02 and 1.69, against at most 0.40 in an $H_s$ 1 m sea (Figure 11b); at FA 0.6 the storm values rise to 1.2 to 3.1.

For hypothesis (e), that most wave power acts at the break and that the break tears the tubes through repeated up-and-down loading rather than lifting them, the load part is supported: the critical state occurs just before breaking with simultaneous uplift [@bar2023], and at the break the vertical load on small, flat or sealed-base tubes alternates by an amount close to or above their submerged weight, several thousand times a day. Lift-off itself has not been reported for Israeli tubes, which is consistent with the reductions listed above. The tearing part is not modelled by any source found, and "most tubes tear" is not supported, since the reported causes are point damage, undersizing, burial and construction faults (Table 5). Repeated lifting and flapping may fatigue fabric and seams ("the initial cause of failure is masked by the damage suffered during cyclic flapping and deflation of the container"; `\cite[p. 7]{jackson2012}`{=latex}), but this is a hypothesis that needs fabric and seam tests. Hypothesis (c; Appendix F), that soft structures do not survive long in the breaking zone, is supported by both the record and the stability numbers.

## 6.4 Ecology and cost

Hypothesis (c) also holds that geotubes support marine life and are the cheapest structure. Colonisation of geotextile is documented (seaweed on Korean geotubes after one year; `\cite{oh2006a}`{=latex}), and the designer chose containers partly as a "good substrate for marine organisms" [@geosyntheticsmagazine2023], whereas at Ashkelon a surfers' group stated that "nothing develops near the geotubes" [@rinat2019]. Rock and concrete are colonised too, and bio-enhanced concrete is reported by its maker to more than double invertebrate species richness on a Haifa port breakwater (`\cite{econcretend}`{=latex}; company data). No comparative study was found, so the ecological advantage of geotubes is a new, untested observation.

On capital cost the containers are cheaper: A$56 per m³ at Narrowneck against A$457 for the Cables rock [@blacka2013, Table 4.4, 2013 AUD], although the small or incomplete GSC reefs cost A$274 to A$545 per m³. Over the life of the structure the advantage shrinks: renewal (Narrowneck), removal (Pratte's, about $551,000, slightly more than its construction; `\cite[p. 119]{blacka2013}`{=latex}), plastic debris [@rendle2016, p. 122] and a short field life. "Cheapest" is supported for construction cost only; for a reef in the breaking zone, durability decides the choice (Section 7).
