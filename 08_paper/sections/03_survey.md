# 3 Survey of built surf reefs

## 3.1 The sample at a glance

Table 1 lists the 13 reefs in order of the start of construction; every value has its source, status and method in Appendix C. Seven reefs are made of geotextile or inflatable fabric filled with sand, five of rock (Burkitts is a natural platform reshaped) and one (Xala) of quarried boulders that is not yet documented. By the classes of this review, three reefs work for surfing (Burkitts, Palm Beach, Albany), three work rarely (Narrowneck, Mount Maunganui, Boscombe), two are mixed or disputed (Cables, Kovalam), three do not work (Pratte's, Opunake, Bunbury) and two are unknown (Borth, Xala). Figure 1 shows the plan shapes and depth profiles of the nine reefs with a traced outline, and Figure 2 the size, crest depth and depth gap of all 13.

## 3.2 Australia

**Cables Reef, Perth (1999).** After a surfers' campaign from 1975, the Western Australian Department of Sport and Recreation financed a reef on the site of a natural break (Raised Water Research, 2019b). It was designed for swells of 0.5-3.5 m, a peel angle of about 45 degrees and a 1:20 ramp (Lyons, 1992; Pattiaratchi, 1999). Granite boulders of 1.5 t (body) and 3.0 t (perimeter) were placed on limestone in 1999 (Raised Water Research, 2019b) for AUD 1.8 M (Blacka et al., 2013). The reef is undamaged: surveyed rock volumes of 4,671 m^3^ (1999) and 5,219 m^3^ (2002) differ by less than the survey error. Its surf record is disputed: the designers report 142 surfable days a year against 5-7 before, counting days with surfers present (Mead & Borrero, 2011), whereas surfers report good waves only in big swells (Blacka et al., 2013, p. 93), and a 2009 critique puts the crest about 1.5 m below the lowest tide, in the swell shadow of Rottnest Island (Pitt, 2009, as cited in Raised Water Research, 2019b).

**Narrowneck Reef, Gold Coast (1999-2000, renewed 2017-18).** The primary aim was to protect the eroding Narrowneck isthmus, with surfing second (Jackson et al., 2007; Blacka et al., 2013). About 400 containers holding about 70,000 m^3^ of sand were laid to a design crest of -1.0 m AHD tested on a 1:50 physical model (Mead & Borrero, 2011; Jackson et al., 2007), for AUD 2.3 M, or AUD 2.8 M with top-ups and maintenance (Blacka et al., 2013, p. 101). Non-uniform placement "detracted from the surfing amenity" (Mead & Borrero, 2011, p. 3); the containers settled by 1-2 m, the 2018 crest is at -2.2 m AHD, and 84 containers were renewed in 2017-18 (Blacka et al., 2013, pp. 97-98; Corbett et al., 2023). The reef "is rarely surfed" because world-class breaks work in the same conditions (EcoShape, n.d.). Its protective effect is disputed (Section 5.4).

**Palm Beach Reef, Gold Coast (2019).** The City of Gold Coast built the reef to protect the beach and to give a right-hand wave (Hunt et al., 2022), on a concept design by DHI (Mortensen et al., 2015). About 60,000 t of quarried boulders (25,000 m^3^) were placed to a crest at -1.5 m MSL for AUD 18.2 M (Appendix C). The reef is stable, and a year of shore-camera tracking found the waves "desirable" when easterly or southerly swells had $H_s$ above 0.8 m and $T_p$ above 7 s, with rides longer than in the natural surf zone (Hunt et al., 2022; Prenzler et al., 2022).

**Bunbury Airwave (2019).** The Airwave, patented by T. Bottegal (Waveco), is a Hypalon bladder 12 m across ballasted with 140-150 t of beach sand, intended to turn a closeout into a peeling wave. The City of Bunbury invested AUD 75,000 (ABC News, 2019); the cost was about USD 350,000-400,000 (Raised Water Research, n.d.-a; sources disagree). The bladder tore along a glued seam during installation in December 2019 and was removed within about three days (ABC News, 2019); according to the founder, the seam opened a couple of days after installation and the wave "worked amazingly" until then (T. Bottegal, Airwave founder, pers. comm., 8 Oct 2026). The two accounts differ on timing and are both given.

**Southern Ocean Surf Reef, Albany (2025).** Bluecoast Consulting Engineers designed the reef from the feasibility study of Royal HaskoningDHV (2015) as a long peeling wave for surfing; the AUD 11.75 M cost was shared by the Australian Government (AUD 4.75 M), the Government of Western Australia (AUD 5 M), the City of Albany (AUD 1.995 M) and Albany Boardriders (City of Albany, n.d.). Rock of three grades (62,800 t, design volume 34,000 m^3^) was placed to a crest of -1.0 m AHD (City of Albany, n.d.). It opened in July 2025 with faces of 0.8-1.3 m and rides of 100-120 m (Raised Water Research, n.d.-d; whether the wave is the "Long Right" of the 2015 option or the left described at opening was not reconciled), but a designer calls it "intermediate-to-advanced on most days" (Mercer, 2025). A storm in May 2025 moved some core rock, and about 18 high rocks were repositioned in February 2026 after injuries (Sheil, 2026).

**Burkitts Reef, Bargara (1997).** G. Redgard, a sugarcane cutter, moved about 300 m^3^ of boulders on a natural basalt platform with a hired excavator, at about AUD 10,000 of his own money and with no engineering design (Howells & O'Neal, 2024; Raised Water Research, n.d.-b). Surfable days rose from "a handful" to about 30 a year, a researcher's estimate (A. Pitt, as reported by Raised Water Research, n.d.-b), and the site has needed no maintenance in nearly three decades (Howells & O'Neal, 2024). No crest level, depth or plan is known.

## 3.3 New Zealand

**Mount Maunganui Reef (2005-2008).** Designed by K. Black and S. Mead as a symmetrical "delta" with a wave-focusing and a wave-breaking zone (Mead & Black, 1999; Mead et al., 2007), it was financed by a community trust at about NZD 1.5 M (SunLive, n.d.). Of 6,500 m^3^ designed, 2,800 m^3^ were built as 24 containers (Mead & Borrero, 2011, p. 3); containers ruptured during filling and the apex deflated to about -2.0 to -2.4 m CD by 2013 (Dahm & Gibberd, 2013). In October 2006 the right-hander broke "in a hollow, tubing manner", but after the crest slumped by about 1 m it "breaks during only a very confined set of conditions" (Mead & Borrero, 2011, p. 3). The reef was removed in 2014 (SunLive, n.d.).

**Opunake Reef (2006-2008, never completed).** Financed by the South Taranaki District Council and a local trust (Reid & Sumner, 2021), it was designed for a right-hand wave of 1.8-2.5 m face, to raise surfable days from about one in eight to one in three (Moores, 2006). Only 27 bags were in place in April 2008, costs approached twice the estimate (Mead & Borrero, 2011, p. 4; NZD 1.7 M, Appendix C), and the structure broke apart; the designers later called bag failure the "fatal flaw" of the technology (Reid & Sumner, 2021).

## 3.4 United Kingdom

**Boscombe Surf Reef (2008-2009).** Bournemouth Borough Council commissioned ASR Ltd, as part of a regeneration scheme, to improve a site with "mediocre" surfing; the reef was designed primarily for surfing (Mead et al., 2010). It cost GBP 3.2 M on a fixed-price contract (Mead & Borrero, 2011; Raised Water Research, n.d.-e) and aimed to double the good surfing days from about 77 to 154 a year (Raised Water Research, n.d.-e). Sand containers (12,800 m^3^) were placed with a crest designed at +0.5 m CD to break small waves at low tide; whether it was built higher is unresolved (Rendle, 2016, p. 70; Mead et al., 2010). The independent evaluation found the wave "steep and frequently plunging" and restricted to more advanced surfers (Davidson, 2010, as cited in Rendle, 2016, pp. 70-71; Section 4.3). The containers failed from March-April 2011 and the reef was closed on 31 March 2011 (Rendle & Davidson, 2012).

**Borth reef (2011-2012).** The reef is part of the Phase 1 coastal defence of Borth, Wales, designed by Royal Haskoning with ASR as sub-consultant, physically modelled by HR Wallingford and funded by a Welsh Government grant and EU convergence funds (Borth Community, n.d.). The whole scheme cost GBP 13 M; about 51,000 t of rock lie in two mounds (30,119 m^3^, derived), of which the northern is shaped as a surf reef (Appendix A.9). The crest is 0.19 m above MSL in a 4.3 m spring range, so it dries at low water. The defences withstood the storms of December 2011 and show no damage in imagery of 2012-2024 (Hansford, 2011; Daily Post, 2013; this review), but no surf record exists, and Blacka et al. (2013) rate the surfing objective "unknown".

## 3.5 United States, India and Mexico

**Pratte's Reef, El Segundo (2000-2001).** The first structure in US waters built for surfing, it mitigated the loss of surf caused by the El Segundo groin (Borrero & Nelsen, 2003) and was financed by Chevron as legal mitigation, with the California Coastal Conservancy and the Surfrider Foundation (Raised Water Research, n.d.-f). It was a V of 110 sand-filled polypropylene bags in Phase I and 90 in Phase II (1,350 m^3^), costing USD 550,000 (Appendix C). Fewer than 25 people surfed it in two years, in about 10 sessions, because of its "small size": 30 m cross-shore against about 150 m at Narrowneck (Borrero & Nelsen, 2003, pp. 13-14). The bags disintegrated within about two years, the crest dropped 1.5 m, and the reef was removed in 2008 and 2010 (Blacka et al., 2013; Fontaine, 2008).

**Kovalam Reef, India (2009-2010).** ASR built India's first multi-purpose reef from 5-6 fanned strips of sand containers (about 4,000 m^3^; INR 75 M) for a left-hander of about 100 m, financed by the post-tsunami rehabilitation programme of the Government of India (Raised Water Research, n.d.-c; Mead & Borrero, 2011). The first season was praised as a wave that "could very well be the best wave an artificial reef has ever created" (Collins, 2010), but later "most of the waves just roll over the reef" (Raised Water Research, n.d.-c); the containers were torn, deflated and sunk by 2013 (E. Rendle, pers. comm., 2013, in Blacka et al., 2013, p. 70). The designers' claim of a beach 10-30 m wider (Mead & Borrero, 2011) has not been independently assessed.

**Xala Reef, Jalisco, Mexico (2025-26; unverified).** Announced as part of a private resort development and built from quarried boulders ("3,000 truckloads"; Romeyn, 2025; Indelicato, 2026), the reef has no published crest level, volume, cost, surf report or monitoring. It carries no weight in the analysis.

## 3.6 What the survey shows

Four patterns emerge, to be tested in Sections 4 to 7. First, size separates the outcomes: the three reefs that do not work are 4,900 m^3^ or less, whereas the two constructed reefs that work are 25,000 m^3^ or more (Burkitts is a reshaped natural platform, not a built volume). Second, six of the seven fabric-filled or inflatable reefs suffered major damage or loss within a few years, whereas none of the four rock reefs failed (Appendix C, Table C4); material is confounded with date and budget. Third, reported surfable days are not comparable: they are a count of days with surfers present (Cables), a design baseline (Boscombe), a share of the year of unstated basis (Albany) or an estimate (Burkitts). Fourth, no reef was built or reported as a beginner wave. Design wave heights and periods are unknown for most reefs, and most performance claims come from the designers (Mead & Borrero, 2011).

## 3.7 Structures that are not surf reefs

Bar and Drimer (2023, p. 2) summarise the built record in one paragraph and borrow the statement that most projects "have failed" from the Raised Water Research website, without a list or a definition of failure. Of the eleven projects they name, three are among the 13 reefs above. Of the other eight, four are geotextile-tube shore-protection breakwaters (Korea, Mexico, the UAE, Indonesia), one is a nature-based breakwater (New York), two are unbuilt design studies (Portugal, Spain), and one is the Ashkelon North cliff-foot geotube scheme in Israel, where several tubes tore in the first winter (State Comptroller of Israel, 2020). None was built for surfing; they are documented in Appendix D.
