# 3 Survey of built surf reefs

## 3.1 The sample at a glance

Table 1 lists the 13 reefs in order of the review's rating rank (Section 8.4; the year of construction is in the table); every value has its source, status and method in Appendix C. Seven reefs are made of geotextile or inflatable fabric filled with sand, five of rock (Burkitts is a natural platform reshaped) and one (Xala) of quarried boulders that is not yet documented. By the classes of this review, three reefs work for surfing (Burkitts, Palm Beach, Albany), three work rarely (Narrowneck, Mount Maunganui, Boscombe), two are mixed or disputed (Cables, Kovalam), three do not work (Pratte's, Opunake, Bunbury) and two are unknown (Borth, Xala). These classes, used in the figures, follow the surf-quality rating (A) of Table 1 and Section 8.4: 'works' is A 3 to 5 with consistent reports, 'works rarely' A 2, 'does not work' A 1, and 'mixed or disputed' marks conflicting reports (Cables A 3, Kovalam A 2). Figure 1 shows the plan shapes and depth profiles of the nine reefs with a traced outline, and Figure 2 the size, crest depth and depth gap of all 13.

**Reading the volume columns.** The reported and the modelled volumes agree within 15 % at Cables (5,500 m³ stated; 4,671 to 5,219 m³ surveyed; 4,683 m³ modelled), Mount Maunganui and Boscombe; Albany's model was fitted to the stated volume and is not an independent check. Four reefs differ. (1) Narrowneck's 70,000 m³ and the 13,495 m³ of the 3D model measure different things. The literature value is the sand pumped into the containers. The model measures only the mound that stands above today's seabed within the container field visible on the aerial of 8 August 2020 (6,239 m²). Three effects separate the two: the containers have settled 1 to 2 m and are partly buried in the sand bed [@jackson2012, p. 6], so much of the fill lies below the modelled seabed; containers spread beyond the visible field, since the City of Gold Coast reef polygon covers 34,565 m² [@cityofndb], over which 60,000 m³ is a mean thickness of about 1.7 m, whereas the visible field alone would need a mean height of almost 10 m in 4 to 6 m of water; and a stack of flattened bags encloses voids, which the envelope counts and the fill does not, a smaller effect in the opposite direction. In plan, the visible Narrowneck mound is therefore of the order of Palm Beach and Albany (Figure 1), but the sand placed is three to five times larger (derived). (2) The Pratte's model covers only the Phase I design layout. (3) At Palm Beach the stated 25,000 m³ and 60,000 t imply 2.4 t/m³, a solid-rock density rather than that of placed rock with voids (1.7 to 1.8 t/m³), so at least one of the two is wrong; the model (22,020 m³) lies below every reported basis. (4) Borth has no reef-only quantity, and Bunbury's bladder volume is not a fill volume.

**Reading the cost columns.** Most reefs cost about USD 250 to 750 per m³ at 2024 prices; the values outside that band, and the spread within it, have identifiable causes. Narrowneck is the cheapest built reef (USD 42 per m³), and unit costs explain why. Sand placed in large volumes by hydraulic dredging and pumping costs roughly USD 5 to 25 per m³ at 2024 prices: all-inclusive rates for placed sand in Australia are AUD 5 to 50 per m³, NSW projects of about 60,000 m³ cost AUD 10 to 13 per m³, and international averages are USD 4 to 7 per m³ at 2010 prices [@carley2017, Section 6.1]. About USD 42 per m³ for sand dredged nearby and pumped into containers dropped from a split-hull barge, with no quarried rock, is therefore plausible. Economies of scale explain the other end of the range: mobilisation, design, consent and monitoring are largely fixed costs, spread over a large volume at Narrowneck and over small volumes at the small reefs (below). The price year is 1999-2000, and a consumer price index need not follow the cost of marine construction. Per m³ of the exposed mound it would be about USD 220. The 2017-18 renewal, excluded from the table, added 84 containers, about 12,300 m³ at about 147 m³ each, for about AUD 2 M [@theinertia2017], or about USD 150 per m³ at 2024 prices (derived): more per m³ than the original build, because the fixed costs fell on a small addition. Burkitts (USD 50 per m³) is two days of excavator hire on a natural boulder platform, not a construction project. The small sand-bag reefs are the most expensive per m³ (Pratte's 742, Kovalam 590, Boscombe 569, Mount Maunganui 549): design, consent, mobilisation and monitoring are largely fixed costs spread over small volumes; Pratte's was placed in two campaigns, Mount Maunganui's budget was for a design more than twice the built volume, and Boscombe's cost rose from an original GBP 0.6 M to GBP 3.2 M during the project [@rendle2016, pp. 65-67]. The two modern rock reefs differ by a factor of 2.5: Albany USD 246 per m³ (AUD 382) against Palm Beach USD 621 (AUD 728; about AUD 700 by @internationalcoastal2025). The sources do not explain the gap; Albany's rock came from a quarry opened near the site, and the Palm Beach volume is uncertain (USD 470 per m³ if the register's 33,000 m³ holds). Borth (the whole defence scheme) and Bunbury (a manufactured bladder of about 100 m³) are not comparable and are bracketed. Total cost does not follow outcome: the best wave (Albany) cost about half as much as Palm Beach.

## 3.2 Australia

**Cables Reef, Perth (1999).** After a surfers' campaign from 1975, the Western Australian Department of Sport and Recreation financed a reef on the site of a natural break [@raisedwater2019b]. It was designed for swells of 0.5-3.5 m, a peel angle of about 45 degrees and a 1:20 ramp [@lyons1992; @pattiaratchi1999]. Granite boulders of 1.5 t (body) and 3.0 t (perimeter) were placed on limestone in 1999 [@raisedwater2019b] for AUD 1.8 M [@blacka2013]. The reef is undamaged: surveyed rock volumes of 4,671 m^3^ (1999) and 5,219 m^3^ (2002) differ by less than the survey error. Its surf record is disputed: the designers report 142 surfable days a year against 5-7 before, counting days with surfers present [@mead2011], whereas surfers report good waves only in big swells [@blacka2013, p. 93], and a 2009 critique puts the crest about 1.5 m below the lowest tide, in the swell shadow of Rottnest Island (`\cite{pitt2009}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite{raisedwater2019b}`{=latex}).

**Narrowneck Reef, Gold Coast (1999-2000, renewed 2017-18).** The primary aim was to protect the eroding Narrowneck isthmus, with surfing second [@jackson2007b; @blacka2013]. About 400 containers holding about 70,000 m^3^ of sand were laid to a design crest of -1.0 m AHD tested on a 1:50 physical model [@mead2011; @jackson2007b], for AUD 2.3 M, or AUD 2.8 M with top-ups and maintenance [@blacka2013, p. 101]; the 2017-18 renewal cost about AUD 2 M more [@theinertia2017] (Appendix C). Non-uniform placement "detracted from the surfing amenity" [@mead2011, p. 3]; the containers settled by 1-2 m, the 2018 crest is at -2.2 m AHD, and 84 containers were renewed in 2017-18 [@blacka2013, pp. 97-98; @corbett2023]. The reef "is rarely surfed" because world-class breaks work in the same conditions [@ecoshapend]. Its protective effect is disputed (Section 5.4).

**Palm Beach Reef, Gold Coast (2019).** The City of Gold Coast built the reef to protect the beach and to give a right-hand wave [@hunt2022], on a concept design by DHI [@mortensen2015]. About 60,000 t of quarried boulders (stated as 25,000 m^3^; Table 1, note) were placed to a crest at -1.5 m MSL for AUD 18.2 M (Appendix C). The reef is stable, and a year of shore-camera tracking found the waves "desirable" when easterly or southerly swells had $H_s$ above 0.8 m and $T_p$ above 7 s, with rides longer than in the natural surf zone [@hunt2022; @prenzler2023].

**Bunbury Airwave (2019).** The Airwave, patented by T. Bottegal (Waveco), is a Hypalon bladder 12 m across ballasted with 140-150 t of beach sand, intended to turn a closeout into a peeling wave. The City of Bunbury invested AUD 75,000 [@abcnews2019]; the cost was about USD 350,000-400,000 (`\cite{raisedwaternda}`{=latex}; sources disagree). The bladder tore along a glued seam during installation in December 2019 and was removed within about three days [@abcnews2019]; according to the founder, the seam opened a couple of days after installation and the wave "worked amazingly" until then (T. Bottegal, Airwave founder, pers. comm., 8 Oct 2026). The two accounts differ on timing and are both given.

**Southern Ocean Surf Reef, Albany (2025).** Bluecoast Consulting Engineers designed the reef from the feasibility study of @royalhaskoningdhv2015 as a long peeling wave for surfing; the reported final cost of AUD 13 M [@elezovich2026] was shared by the Australian Government (AUD 5 M), the Government of Western Australia (AUD 4.75 M; AUD 4.5 M in the City's figures), the City of Albany (AUD 1.995 M) and Albany Boardriders, against AUD 11.75 M of funds committed before construction [@cityofnd; @governmentof2025]. Rock of three grades (62,800 t, design volume 34,000 m^3^) was placed to a crest of -1.0 m AHD [@cityofnd]. It opened in July 2025 with faces of 0.8-1.3 m and rides of 100-120 m (`\cite{raisedwaterndd}`{=latex}; whether the wave is the "Long Right" of the 2015 option or the left described at opening was not reconciled), but a designer calls it "intermediate-to-advanced on most days" [@mercer2025]. A storm in May 2025 moved some core rock, and about 18 high rocks were repositioned in February 2026 after injuries [@sheil2026].

**Burkitts Reef, Bargara (1997).** G. Redgard, a sugarcane cutter, moved about 300 m^3^ of boulders on a natural basalt platform with a hired excavator, at about AUD 10,000 of his own money and with no engineering design [@howells2024; @raisedwaterndb]. Surfable days rose from "a handful" to about 30 a year, a researcher's estimate (A. Pitt, as reported by `\cite{raisedwaterndb}`{=latex}), and the site has needed no maintenance in nearly three decades [@howells2024]. No crest level, depth or plan is known.

## 3.3 New Zealand

**Mount Maunganui Reef (2005-2008).** Designed by K. Black and S. Mead as a symmetrical "delta" with a wave-focusing and a wave-breaking zone [@mead1999; @mead2007], it was financed by a community trust at about NZD 1.5 M [@sunlivend]. Of 6,500 m^3^ designed, 2,800 m^3^ were built as 24 containers [@mead2011, p. 3]; containers ruptured during filling and the apex deflated to about -2.0 to -2.4 m CD by 2013 [@dahm2013]. In October 2006 the right-hander broke "in a hollow, tubing manner", but after the crest slumped by about 1 m it "breaks during only a very confined set of conditions" [@mead2011, p. 3]. The reef was removed in 2014 [@sunlivend].

**Opunake Reef (2006-2008, never completed).** Financed by the South Taranaki District Council and a local trust [@reid2021], it was designed for a right-hand wave of 1.8-2.5 m face, to raise surfable days from about one in eight to one in three [@moores2006]. Only 27 bags were in place in April 2008, costs approached twice the estimate (`\cite[p. 4]{mead2011}`{=latex}; at least NZD 1.7 M, Appendix C), and the structure broke apart; the designers later called bag failure the "fatal flaw" of the technology [@reid2021].

## 3.4 United Kingdom

**Boscombe Surf Reef (2008-2009).** Bournemouth Borough Council commissioned ASR Ltd, as part of a regeneration scheme, to improve a site with "mediocre" surfing; the reef was designed primarily for surfing [@mead2010]. It cost GBP 3.2 M on a fixed-price contract [@mead2011; @raisedwaternde] and aimed to double the good surfing days from about 77 to 154 a year [@raisedwaternde]. Sand containers (12,800 m^3^) were placed with a crest designed at +0.5 m CD to break small waves at low tide; whether it was built higher is unresolved [@rendle2016, p. 70; @mead2010]. The independent evaluation found the wave "steep and frequently plunging" and restricted to more advanced surfers (`\cite{davidson2010}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite[pp. 70-71]{rendle2016}`{=latex}; Section 4.3). The containers failed from March-April 2011 and the reef was closed on 31 March 2011 [@rendle2012].

**Borth reef (2011-2012).** The reef is part of the Phase 1 coastal defence of Borth, Wales, designed by Royal Haskoning with ASR as sub-consultant, physically modelled by HR Wallingford and funded by a Welsh Government grant and EU convergence funds [@borthcommunitynd]. The whole scheme cost GBP 13 M; about 51,000 t of rock lie in two mounds (30,119 m^3^, derived), of which the northern is shaped as a surf reef (Appendix A.9). The crest is 0.19 m above MSL in a 4.3 m spring range, so it dries at low water. The defences withstood the storms of December 2011 and show no damage in imagery of 2012-2024 (`\cite{hansford2011}`{=latex}; `\cite{dailypost2013}`{=latex}; this review), but no surf record exists, and @blacka2013 rate the surfing objective "unknown".

## 3.5 United States, India and Mexico

**Pratte's Reef, El Segundo (2000-2001).** The first structure in US waters built for surfing, it mitigated the loss of surf caused by the El Segundo groin [@borrero2003] and was financed by Chevron as legal mitigation, with the California Coastal Conservancy and the Surfrider Foundation [@raisedwaterndf]. It was a V of 110 sand-filled polypropylene bags in Phase I and 90 in Phase II (1,350 m^3^), costing USD 550,000 (Appendix C). Fewer than 25 people surfed it in two years, in about 10 sessions, because of its "small size": 30 m cross-shore against about 150 m at Narrowneck [@borrero2003, pp. 13-14]. Anecdotal witness accounts (evidence grade D: viewer comments on the YouTube video *The $500k Surf Reef Failure*, `\cite{danharmon2026}`{=latex}, retrieved 19 Oct 2026) agree: a worker at the adjacent power plant "never saw" a surfable wave, others describe it as breaking rarely, "really tide sensitive and wave height sensitive" and "barely surfable", and attribute this partly to shadowing of the southern swells by Catalina Island. The bags disintegrated within about two years, the crest dropped 1.5 m, and the reef was removed in 2008 and 2010 [@blacka2013; @fontaine2008]; one commenter on the same video, by contrast, states that the bags he saw removed "were still intact" [@danharmon2026], so the failure is better documented for the wave than for the fabric.

**Kovalam Reef, India (2009-2010).** ASR built India's first multi-purpose reef from 5-6 fanned strips of sand containers (about 4,000 m^3^; INR 75 M) for a left-hander of about 100 m, financed by the post-tsunami rehabilitation programme of the Government of India [@raisedwaterndc; @mead2011]. The first season was praised as a wave that "could very well be the best wave an artificial reef has ever created" [@collins2010], but later "most of the waves just roll over the reef" [@raisedwaterndc]; the containers were torn, deflated and sunk by 2013 (E. Rendle, pers. comm., 2013, in `\cite[p. 70]{blacka2013}`{=latex}). The designers' claim of a beach 10-30 m wider [@mead2011] has not been independently assessed.

**Xala Reef, Jalisco, Mexico (2025-26; unverified).** Announced as part of a private resort development and built from quarried boulders ("3,000 truckloads"; `\cite{romeyn2025}`{=latex}; `\cite{indelicato2026}`{=latex}), the reef has no published crest level, volume, cost, surf report or monitoring. It carries no weight in the analysis.

## 3.6 What the survey shows

Four patterns emerge, to be tested in Sections 4 to 7. First, size separates the outcomes: the three reefs that do not work are 4,900 m^3^ or less, whereas the two constructed reefs that work are 25,000 m^3^ or more (Burkitts is a reshaped natural platform, not a built volume). Second, six of the seven fabric-filled or inflatable reefs suffered major damage or loss within a few years, whereas none of the four rock reefs failed (Appendix C, Table C4); material is confounded with date and budget. Third, reported surfable days are not comparable: they are a count of days with surfers present (Cables), a design baseline (Boscombe), a share of the year of unstated basis (Albany) or an estimate (Burkitts). Fourth, no reef was built or reported as a beginner wave. Design wave heights and periods are unknown for most reefs, and most performance claims come from the designers [@mead2011].

## 3.7 Structures that are not surf reefs

@bar2023 [p. 2] summarise the built record in one paragraph and borrow the statement that most projects "have failed" from the Raised Water Research website, without a list or a definition of failure. Of the eleven projects they name, three are among the 13 reefs above. Of the other eight, four are geotextile-tube shore-protection breakwaters (Korea, Mexico, the UAE, Indonesia), one is a nature-based breakwater (New York), two are unbuilt design studies (Portugal, Spain), and one is the Ashkelon North cliff-foot geotube scheme in Israel, where several tubes tore in the first winter [@statecomptroller2020]. None was built for surfing; they are documented in Appendix D.
