# 4 Reef effect on waves and surf quality

A surf reef must make a swell break at a chosen place, with a chosen breaker shape, and with a break that peels along the reef at a speed surfers can follow. Section 4.1 summarises the theory; Sections 4.2 to 4.6 apply it to the nine reconstructed reefs (Section 2; Appendix A). Outcomes are scored 3 (works), 2 (works rarely or mixed) and 1 (does not work) from the survey (Appendix C).

## 4.1 Theory: shoaling, refraction, breaking, breaker type and peel

**Shoaling.** In linear (Airy) theory the wave number $k$ follows from $\omega^2 = g k \tanh(kh)$, and conservation of energy flux, without refraction, friction or reflection, gives the shoaling coefficient

$$K_s = \frac{H}{H_0} = \sqrt{\frac{c_{g0}}{c_g}},$$

with $H_0$ the deep-water height and $c_g$ the group velocity at depth $h$ [@dean1991]. Over a reef the depth falls from toe to crest within tens of metres, so $K_s$ rises quickly at the end of the wave's path.

**Refraction and focusing.** By Snell's law, $\sin\theta / c = \text{const}$, oblique crests turn towards the depth contours, and convex contours make rays converge on a shoal [@dean1991]. @mead2001a classified the seabed of 34 world-class breaks into ramp, platform, focus, wedge, ledge, ridge and pinnacle components, each of which pre-conditions the swell or sets the break point or breaker type (as summarised by `\cite[Section 2.6]{blacka2013}`{=latex}); a surf reef is designed to reproduce them.

**Breaking criterion.** A wave breaks when $H_b = \gamma h_b$. The flat-bed limit $\gamma = 0.78$ (`\cite{mccowan1894}`{=latex}, as given by `\cite{weggel1972}`{=latex}) rises on slopes; @weggel1972 gives $\gamma_b$ as a function of bed slope and $H_b/(gT^2)$, and @battjes1974 reports values of about 0.7 to 1.2. The criterion sets *which* waves break on a crest.

**Breaker type.** The surf-similarity (Iribarren) parameter at the break point,

$$\xi_b = \frac{\tan\beta}{\sqrt{H_b / L_0}}, \qquad L_0 = \frac{g T^2}{2\pi},$$

separates spilling ($\xi_b < 0.4$), plunging ($0.4 < \xi_b < 2.0$) and surging or collapsing breakers ($\xi_b > 2.0$) (`\cite{battjes1974}`{=latex}, after `\cite{galvin1968}`{=latex}). It sets *how* a wave breaks. For a 1 m breaker, $\xi_b = 0.4$ corresponds to a slope of 1:31 at $T$ = 10 s and 1:22 at 7 s, and $\xi_b = 2.0$ to 1:6.2 and 1:4.4 (derived in this review).

**Breaking intensity.** @mead2001b related the hollowness of plunging waves, measured by the vortex ratio $Y$ (length to width of the tube), to the seabed gradient along the wave orthogonal, $Y = 0.065\,X + 0.821$ ($R^2 = 0.71$), where $X$ is the gradient "given as the horizontal distance to one vertical unit", i.e. a slope of 1:$X$ (`\cite[Eq. 6.5]{mead2000}`{=latex}, p. 115). Intensity is "extreme" for $Y$ = 1.6 to 1.9, "very high" for 1.91 to 2.2, "high" for 2.21 to 2.5, "medium/high" for 2.51 to 2.8 and "medium" for 2.81 to 3.1; waves with $Y < 1.6$ are likely to collapse and be unsurfable (`\cite[Table 6.2]{mead2000}`{=latex}, p. 121). The fit covers slopes of about 1:9 to 1:40 and scatters widely (`\cite{robertson2014}`{=latex}, found $R^2 = 0.27$ on their field data), so it is used as an indicator only.

**Peel angle and surfer skill.** The peel angle $\alpha$ is the angle between the unbroken crest and the path of the break, which moves along the crest at the peel speed $V_p = c_b/\sin\alpha$, so small angles are fast [@lewis2015, p. 3]. @hutt2001 gave the smallest peel angle that each surfer rating can ride: 90, 70, 60, 55, 50, 40, 29 and 27 degrees for ratings 1 (beginner) to 8 (professional), with no limit for ratings 9 and 10 (as reproduced by `\cite[Table 3]{lewis2015}`{=latex}, and `\cite[Table 1.1]{barlow2013}`{=latex}, which agree). @walker1975 [p. 5] described the 90-degree wave as suited to "a beginning surfer who has not developed skill levels sufficient to negotiate a turn" and suggested 30 degrees "as a minimum for a plunging breaker at 6-foot height": a hydrodynamic minimum, not a skill class. On a reef arm, Snell's law refracts the angle $\phi_0$ between the incident crest and the arm at the toe (depth $h_0$) to $\sin\alpha_b = \sin\phi_0\, c(h_b)/c(h_0)$ at the break (method of this review).

## 4.2 Where waves break: crest depth, depth gap and ramp length

Waves of $H_0$ = 0.5 to 3 m and $T$ = 6 to 14 s were shoaled along the depth profile of each reef (Section 2.4; for Albany, Cables and Borth a surf cut through the part of the reef where the wave is ridden; line positions in Figure E1 (Appendix E)) at low, mean and high water (normal incidence, $\gamma = 0.78$; sensitivity with $\gamma = 0.55$ and the Weggel index). At MSL a wave needs $H_0$ of 0.3 to 1.3 m to break on the reef, and above 1.6 to 5.2 m it breaks seaward of it (Table 2; Borth, whose crest dries, excepted). Over all cases, 34 to 84 % break on the ramp or crest.

One of the author's leads (f; Appendix F) is that the depth gap alone does not decide the wave: a long, gradual ramp from 5 m to 2 m can give a good wave, because most waves do not break at 2 m, whereas a sudden rise to 1 to 1.5 m gives a too-hollow wave. Figure 3 tests this on two reefs with comparable depth gaps (5.49 m at Palm Beach, 4.51 m at Boscombe) under the same 1 m, 10 s wave. Both waves break at the same height and depth ($H_b$ = 1.39 m, $h_b$ = 1.78 m): the crest depth fixes where the wave breaks. But over Palm Beach's 89 m ramp (1:17) the breaker plunges with $\xi_b$ = 0.56 and "very high" intensity ($Y$ = 2.05), while over Boscombe's 13 m ramp (1:3.1) it collapses ($\xi_b$ = 3.00, $Y$ = 1.05, extrapolated).

## 4.3 Breaker type, hollowness and the Boscombe case

Over all cases that break on the reef (Table 2), Palm Beach gives a median $\xi_b$ of 0.49 and $Y$ of 2.01 with no case below $Y$ = 1.6, whereas Bunbury (1:2.7) and Boscombe (1:3) give $Y < 1.6$ in every case and Mount Maunganui (1:9) in 91 %. The median $\xi_b$ ranks with the outcome at $\rho = -0.77$ ($p$ = 0.039): the reefs that work break more gently. Period matters as well as slope: the surging share rises from 0.21 at 6 s to 0.41 at 10 to 12 s, so the same reef gives gentler waves in short-period seas such as Israel's. Hollowness alone is not failure: Mount Maunganui first "broke as designed in a hollow, tubing manner" [@mead2011, p. 3], and Albany, which plunges on its ridge (median $\xi_b$ 0.56, median $Y$ 1.75, "extreme", with no surging case), works but is "intermediate-to-advanced on most days" [@mercer2025].

Boscombe tests the author's lead (d; Appendix F) that its structure rose too steeply and made a wave mostly for bodyboarders. The computed 1:3 flank collapses in every case, Boscombe's typical sea (0.5 m, 8 s) gives $\xi_b$ = 1.60 and $Y$ = 1.30, and even the 2011 survey of the damaged reef (mean ramp 1:12) gives $Y < 1.6$ in 85 % of cases. The independent audit found the wave "steep and frequently plunging", so that "novice surfers and intermediates would not be able to surf the Boscombe Reef" (`\cite{davidson2010}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite[pp. 70-71]{rendle2016}`{=latex}); it required Hutt level 7 or 8 against a design level of 5 or 6 (`\cite[p. 205]{rendle2016}`{=latex}; the designers give 3 to 6, `\cite{mead2010}`{=latex}). Bodyboarders were more satisfied than stand-up surfers, who called it "a bodyboarders wave" [@rendle2016, pp. 178, 204-205], and the designers note that outside the design swell the waves were "frequently more suitable to body-boarding due to the intense plunging nature of the wave breaking" [@mead2010, p. 5]; yet advanced stand-up surfers rode it (`\cite{davidson2010}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite[p. 70]{rendle2016}`{=latex}). The evidence supports "too steep" and "mostly bodyboarders". The crest was set shallow so that the common 0.5 m waves would break at low tide [@rendle2016, p. 71], exactly the condition in which a short ramp collapses the wave, and, exposed at low tide and too deep at high tide, it left a mid-tide window of "1 to 2 hours" [@rendle2016, p. 71].

The depth shoreward of the break matters as well as the ramp seaward of it. A breaking wave loses energy until its height falls to a stable fraction of the local depth, about $H$ = 0.4$h$, and then stops breaking; where the water deepens again after the break, as over the trough behind a bar, the wave drops below that ratio, stops breaking and re-forms as an unbroken wave [@dally1985]. @dally1985 model this as a decay of the energy flux towards that of the stable wave at a rate inversely proportional to the depth (decay coefficient $K$ = 0.15, stable ratio $\Gamma$ = 0.4), solved for a flat shelf and a plane slope and run on a profile with two bars and troughs, where it reproduces the shoaling, breaking and re-forming seen in nature. Applied along each reef profile from the break point shoreward (breaking heights $H_b$ = 1, 1.5 and 2 m at low, mean and high water; Appendix A), the model gives the length over which the reef keeps the wave breaking before the water behind the break deepens or the reef ends (Table 3). Albany, the best wave in the sample, keeps it breaking for a median of 90 m (at most 97 m): its ridge stays nearly flat at −2.0 to −2.4 m AHD for about 80 m inside the steep outer face. Pratte's and Bunbury, the two reefs that do not work, keep it for 9 m and 7.5 m; at Pratte's even a 2 m wave that breaks on the crest has only 6 m of reef left inside the break and goes on breaking, if at all, on the natural bed. Over the eight reefs with a known outcome the median length ranks with the outcome at $\rho$ = 0.77 (permutation $p$ = 0.037), a weaker link than that of the ramp length. Length is not sufficient on its own: Palm Beach, which works, has 27 m, because its crest is a mound with a trough about 4.5 m deep behind it, and the cross-shore line is not the ride, which runs along the reef with the peel (Section 4.4). The cruder test that holds the height at its breaking value (breaking stops where $h > H_b$/0.4) separates nothing for 2 m waves, since the water behind every reef is shallower than 5 m; the decay model is what distinguishes the reefs. Surfers who rode Pratte's describe exactly this: the wave "came from deep water, hit the shallow reef quickly, then instantly went into deep water again. The wave would suck up fast, but before it could actually barrel, it would mush out and disappear", and "the reef was shaped like a V instead of a triangle" (viewer comments, grade D; `\cite{danharmon2026}`{=latex}). Two narrow arms of a V leave deep water between and behind them, whereas a filled triangular plan that stays shallow toward the shore keeps the bed shallow under the breaking wave along the ride. The computation is one-dimensional and idealised: normal incidence, no refraction, set-up or friction, a stable ratio fixed at 0.4 although it varies with the bed slope, idealised model profiles for Pratte's and Bunbury (Section 2.4), and no account of the depth lost to the sides of a V or a narrow arm, which a single cross-shore line cannot see.

## 4.4 Peel angle and surfer skill

The designed arm angles of 45 to 81 degrees refract to 16 to 62 degrees at the break (Figure 5; arm and cut positions in Figure E1 (Appendix E)). Small waves break in shallow water, where refraction is strongest: at $H_0$ = 0.5 m every reef reaches $\alpha_b$ below 27 to 29 degrees (Hutt ratings 8 to 9), and only large waves keep $\alpha_b$ above 50 degrees. Peel speeds are 6.9 to 11.4 m/s. Only one case reaches the 60 to 90 degrees of ratings 1 to 3: Borth at $H_0$ = 3 m (61.5 degrees, rating 3), a large wave at the upper end of Borth's window that breaks at 3.2 m, above the 2.5 m height limit of that rating, on a reef with no surf record; the incidence angle $\phi_0$ is assumed for five of the eight reefs (Appendix A). The observations agree: no reef was built or reported as a beginner wave (Albany "intermediate-to-advanced", `\cite{mercer2025}`{=latex}; Boscombe "an intermediate to expert break", `\cite[p. 273]{rendle2016}`{=latex}). The literature defines the beginner's wave by a large peel angle rather than by a spilling breaker; both are missing here, since spilling breakers are 0 to 29 % of on-reef cases, and a spilling ramp of about 1:30 needs 35,000 to 49,000 m³ per 100 m in Israeli conditions (Section 7).

## 4.5 Reef size and volume

The author's lead (a; Appendix F) is that reefs need a large volume and that small reefs such as Pratte's do not work. Volume ranks with outcome (Figure 6): $\rho$ = 0.72 ($p$ = 0.021, $n$ = 10) for the stated volume of the built reefs and 0.93 ($p$ = 0.005, $n$ = 8) for the model volume. All three reefs that do not work are 4,900 m³ or smaller and both built reefs that work are 25,000 m³ or larger, because volume buys ramp length (ramp length $\rho$ = 0.93 against relief 0.62, $p$ = 0.15): a 1:16 to 1:20 ramp on a 4 to 5 m bed needs 15,000 to 25,000 m³ per 100 m (Section 7). Burkitts, a natural reef reshaped by moving about 300 m³ of rock, works, so volume matters when the reef must create the whole relief; and size is collinear with material and budget, so the association is descriptive. The direction is in the literature: "the single largest limitation to building a successful artificial reef in terms of surfing is the issue of scale" (`\cite{shand2011}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite[p. 2]{mead2011}`{=latex}), and Pratte's failure was attributed to its "small size" [@borrero2003, pp. 13-14]. Volume is not sufficient: Narrowneck, the largest reef, works only rarely [@mead2011, p. 2].

## 4.6 Surfable window and tide

Reef waves work within narrow windows of wave height and water level. Pratte's broke "1-5 times per year" (`\cite{borrero2003}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite[p. 39]{rendle2016}`{=latex}), which local witnesses confirm anecdotally: it "never produced a surfable wave" in one account and was "really tide sensitive and wave height sensitive" in another (viewer comments, grade D; `\cite{danharmon2026}`{=latex}, retrieved 19 Oct 2026); and Palm Beach works for easterly or southerly swells above $H_s$ 0.8 m with peak periods above 7 s [@prenzler2023]; the designers attribute the narrow windows to small volume [@mead2011, pp. 2, 6]. This supports the first part of lead (d), that reef waves are active on few days.

The computation steps the water level through the tidal range and counts a level as surfable when the wave breaks on the reef with $\xi_b < 2$ and $Y \geq 1.6$, weighted by a synthetic spring-neap tide (Appendix A). Given a suitable swell, Palm Beach, Albany and Cables are surfable 0.55 to 0.70 of the time (0.70, 0.62 and 0.55), Borth 0.19, Mount Maunganui and Narrowneck 0.15 to 0.16, Pratte's 0.04, Bunbury 0.01 and Boscombe never (Table 2). Under the Israeli tide (spring range 0.40 m; `\cite{rosen2001}`{=latex}) the average does not rise (mean change +0.01; 66 % of cases change by less than 0.05), but the outcome becomes a function of wave height alone: for a given swell the reef works all day or not at all (Figure 7). The Palm Beach geometry under a 1 m wave rises from 0.69 to 1.00, while Mount Maunganui, which breaks only at low water, falls from 0.41 to 0. A synthetic Haifa-type reef keeps 0.87 to 0.98 of its no-tide surfable time at a 0.2 to 0.4 m range, against 0.64 to 0.68 at 4.3 m. The tidal control is documented: before its renewal Narrowneck broke regularly above an RMS height of about 0.8 m at low tide and 1.2 m at high tide [@corbett2023, p. 668], and the Albany design relied on a "low tidal range" [@cityof2023, p. 2]. Without a wave climate, the fractions are shares of the time with a suitable swell, not days per year.

## 4.7 Synthesis

The crest depth decides which waves break and the length and gradient of the ramp how they break (ramp length $\rho$ = 0.93, mean slope 0.77, median $\xi_b$ −0.77; crest depth 0.39, not significant); every reef refracts its waves to peel angles for advanced surfers; and a small tide makes the surfable time depend on wave height alone (Table 2). The model is one-dimensional with linear shoaling, ignores refraction and focusing across the reef (except for the peel angle) and rests on idealised geometry for six of the nine reefs, so its classes are indicators, not predictions of ride quality.
