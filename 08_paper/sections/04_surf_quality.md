# 4 Reef effect on waves and surf quality

A surf reef must make a swell break at a chosen place, with a chosen breaker shape, and with a break that peels along the reef at a speed surfers can follow. Section 4.1 summarises the theory; Sections 4.2 to 4.6 apply it to the nine reconstructed reefs (Section 2; Appendix A). Outcomes are scored 3 (works), 2 (works rarely or mixed) and 1 (does not work) from the survey (Appendix C).

## 4.1 Theory: shoaling, refraction, breaking, breaker type and peel

**Shoaling.** In linear (Airy) theory the wave number $k$ follows from $\omega^2 = g k \tanh(kh)$, and conservation of energy flux, without refraction, friction or reflection, gives the shoaling coefficient

$$K_s = \frac{H}{H_0} = \sqrt{\frac{c_{g0}}{c_g}},$$

with $H_0$ the deep-water height and $c_g$ the group velocity at depth $h$ (Dean & Dalrymple, 1991). Over a reef the depth falls from toe to crest within tens of metres, so $K_s$ rises quickly at the end of the wave's path.

**Refraction and focusing.** By Snell's law, $\sin\theta / c = \text{const}$, oblique crests turn towards the depth contours, and convex contours make rays converge on a shoal (Dean & Dalrymple, 1991). Mead and Black (2001a) classified the seabed of 34 world-class breaks into ramp, platform, focus, wedge, ledge, ridge and pinnacle components, each of which pre-conditions the swell or sets the break point or breaker type (as summarised by Blacka et al., 2013, Section 2.6); a surf reef is designed to reproduce them.

**Breaking criterion.** A wave breaks when $H_b = \gamma h_b$. The flat-bed limit $\gamma = 0.78$ (McCowan, 1894, as given by Weggel, 1972) rises on slopes; Weggel (1972) gives $\gamma_b$ as a function of bed slope and $H_b/(gT^2)$, and Battjes (1974) reports values of about 0.7 to 1.2. The criterion sets *which* waves break on a crest.

**Breaker type.** The surf-similarity (Iribarren) parameter at the break point,

$$\xi_b = \frac{\tan\beta}{\sqrt{H_b / L_0}}, \qquad L_0 = \frac{g T^2}{2\pi},$$

separates spilling ($\xi_b < 0.4$), plunging ($0.4 < \xi_b < 2.0$) and surging or collapsing breakers ($\xi_b > 2.0$) (Battjes, 1974, after Galvin, 1968). It sets *how* a wave breaks. For a 1 m breaker, $\xi_b = 0.4$ corresponds to a slope of 1:31 at $T$ = 10 s and 1:22 at 7 s, and $\xi_b = 2.0$ to 1:6.2 and 1:4.4 (derived in this review).

**Breaking intensity.** Mead and Black (2001b) related the hollowness of plunging waves, measured by the vortex ratio $Y$ (length to width of the tube), to the seabed gradient along the wave orthogonal, $Y = 0.065\,X + 0.821$ ($R^2 = 0.71$), where $X$ is the gradient "given as the horizontal distance to one vertical unit", i.e. a slope of 1:$X$ (Mead, 2000, Eq. 6.5, p. 115). Intensity is "extreme" for $Y$ = 1.6 to 1.9, "very high" for 1.91 to 2.2, "high" for 2.21 to 2.5, "medium/high" for 2.51 to 2.8 and "medium" for 2.81 to 3.1; waves with $Y < 1.6$ are likely to collapse and be unsurfable (Mead, 2000, Table 6.2, p. 121). The fit covers slopes of about 1:9 to 1:40 and scatters widely (Robertson et al., 2014, found $R^2 = 0.27$ on their field data), so it is used as an indicator only.

**Peel angle and surfer skill.** The peel angle $\alpha$ is the angle between the unbroken crest and the path of the break, which moves along the crest at the peel speed $V_p = c_b/\sin\alpha$, so small angles are fast (Lewis et al., 2015, p. 3). Hutt et al. (2001) gave the smallest peel angle that each surfer rating can ride: 90, 70, 60, 55, 50, 40, 29 and 27 degrees for ratings 1 (beginner) to 8 (professional), with no limit for ratings 9 and 10 (as reproduced by Lewis et al., 2015, Table 3, and Barlow, 2013, Table 1.1, which agree). Walker (1975, p. 5) described the 90-degree wave as suited to "a beginning surfer who has not developed skill levels sufficient to negotiate a turn" and suggested 30 degrees "as a minimum for a plunging breaker at 6-foot height": a hydrodynamic minimum, not a skill class. On a reef arm, Snell's law refracts the angle $\phi_0$ between the incident crest and the arm at the toe (depth $h_0$) to $\sin\alpha_b = \sin\phi_0\, c(h_b)/c(h_0)$ at the break (method of this review).

## 4.2 Where waves break: crest depth, depth gap and ramp length

Waves of $H_0$ = 0.5 to 3 m and $T$ = 6 to 14 s were shoaled along the crest line of each reef at low, mean and high water (normal incidence, $\gamma = 0.78$; sensitivity with $\gamma = 0.55$ and the Weggel index). At MSL a wave needs $H_0$ of 0.3 to 1.3 m to break on the reef, and above 1.6 to 5.2 m it breaks seaward of it (Table 2; Borth, whose crest dries, excepted). Over all cases, 34 to 84 % break on the ramp or crest.

The author's observation (f) is that the depth gap alone does not decide the wave: a long, gradual ramp from 5 m to 2 m can give a good wave, because most waves do not break at 2 m, whereas a sudden rise to 1 to 1.5 m gives a too-hollow wave. Figure 3 tests this on two reefs with comparable depth gaps (5.49 m at Palm Beach, 4.51 m at Boscombe) under the same 1 m, 10 s wave. Both waves break at the same height and depth ($H_b$ = 1.39 m, $h_b$ = 1.78 m): the crest depth fixes where the wave breaks. But over Palm Beach's 89 m ramp (1:17) the breaker plunges with $\xi_b$ = 0.56 and "very high" intensity ($Y$ = 2.05), while over Boscombe's 13 m ramp (1:3.1) it collapses ($\xi_b$ = 3.00, $Y$ = 1.05, extrapolated).

Across the reefs (Figure 4), the depth gap ranks with the outcome (Spearman $\rho$ = 0.93, permutation $p$ = 0.005, $n$ = 8), as does the ramp length ($\rho$ = 0.85, $p$ = 0.015), but not the crest depth ($\rho$ = 0.00). The same 2 to 4 m gap gives a surfable wave over a long ramp and a collapsing wave over an abrupt rise. The ramp is a recognised seabed component (Mead & Black, 2001a, as summarised by Blacka et al., 2013), but "there are no models available off the shelf which directly assess surfability" (Blacka et al., 2013, p. 56), and no source found quantifies the contrast between a gradual ramp and a sudden rise; the quantification is new to this review.

## 4.3 Breaker type, hollowness and the Boscombe case

Over all cases that break on the reef (Table 2), Palm Beach gives a median $\xi_b$ of 0.49 and $Y$ of 2.01 with no case below $Y$ = 1.6, whereas Bunbury (1:2.7) and Boscombe (1:3) give $Y < 1.6$ in every case and Mount Maunganui (1:9) in 91 %. The median $\xi_b$ ranks with the outcome at $\rho = -0.69$ ($p$ = 0.078). Period matters as well as slope: the surging share rises from 0.21 at 6 s to 0.41 at 10 to 12 s, so the same reef gives gentler waves in short-period seas such as Israel's. Hollowness alone is not failure: Mount Maunganui first "broke as designed in a hollow, tubing manner" (Mead & Borrero, 2011, p. 3), and Albany, whose crest line rises at about 1:4, works but is "intermediate-to-advanced on most days" (Mercer, 2025).

Boscombe tests the author's observation (d) that its structure rose too steeply and made a wave only for bodyboarders. The computed 1:3 flank collapses in every case, Boscombe's typical sea (0.5 m, 8 s) gives $\xi_b$ = 1.60 and $Y$ = 1.30, and even the 2011 survey of the damaged reef (mean ramp 1:12) gives $Y < 1.6$ in 85 % of cases. The independent audit found the wave "steep and frequently plunging", so that "novice surfers and intermediates would not be able to surf the Boscombe Reef" (Davidson, 2010, as cited in Rendle, 2016, pp. 70-71); it required Hutt level 7 or 8 against a design level of 5 or 6 (Rendle, 2016, p. 205; the designers give 3 to 6, Mead et al., 2010). Bodyboarders were more satisfied than stand-up surfers, who called it "a bodyboarders wave" (Rendle, 2016, pp. 178, 204-205), and the designers note that outside the design swell the waves were "frequently more suitable to body-boarding due to the intense plunging nature of the wave breaking" (Mead et al., 2010, p. 5); yet advanced stand-up surfers rode it (Davidson, 2010, as cited in Rendle, 2016, p. 70). The evidence supports "too steep" and "mostly bodyboarders", not "only bodyboarders". The crest was set shallow so that the common 0.5 m waves would break at low tide (Rendle, 2016, p. 71), exactly the condition in which a short ramp collapses the wave, and, exposed at low tide and too deep at high tide, it left a mid-tide window of "1 to 2 hours" (Rendle, 2016, p. 71).

## 4.4 Peel angle and surfer skill

The designed arm angles of 45 to 81 degrees refract to 16 to 62 degrees at the break (Figure 5). Small waves break in shallow water, where refraction is strongest: at $H_0$ = 0.5 m every reef reaches $\alpha_b$ below 27 to 29 degrees (Hutt ratings 8 to 9), and only large waves keep $\alpha_b$ above 50 degrees. Peel speeds are 6.9 to 11.4 m/s. No case within the Hutt height ranges reaches the 60 to 90 degrees of ratings 1 to 3; the incidence angle $\phi_0$ is assumed for five of the eight reefs (Appendix A). The observations agree: no reef was built or reported as a beginner wave (Albany "intermediate-to-advanced", Mercer, 2025; Boscombe "an intermediate to expert break", Rendle, 2016, p. 273). The literature defines the beginner's wave by a large peel angle rather than by a spilling breaker; both are missing here, since spilling breakers are 0 to 29 % of on-reef cases, and a spilling ramp of about 1:30 needs 35,000 to 49,000 m³ per 100 m in Israeli conditions (Section 7).

## 4.5 Reef size and volume

The author's observation (a) is that reefs need a large volume and that small reefs such as Pratte's do not work. Volume ranks with outcome (Figure 6): $\rho$ = 0.72 ($p$ = 0.021, $n$ = 10) for the stated volume of the built reefs and 0.93 ($p$ = 0.005, $n$ = 8) for the model volume. All three reefs that do not work are 4,900 m³ or smaller and both built reefs that work are 25,000 m³ or larger, because volume buys relief and ramp length: a 1:16 to 1:20 ramp on a 4 to 5 m bed needs 15,000 to 25,000 m³ per 100 m (Section 7). Burkitts, a natural reef reshaped by moving about 300 m³ of rock, works, so volume matters when the reef must create the whole relief; and size is collinear with material and budget, so the association is descriptive. The direction is in the literature: "the single largest limitation to building a successful artificial reef in terms of surfing is the issue of scale" (Shand, 2011, as cited in Mead & Borrero, 2011, p. 2), and Pratte's failure was attributed to its "small size" (Borrero & Nelsen, 2003, pp. 13-14). Volume is not sufficient: Narrowneck, the largest reef, works only rarely (Mead & Borrero, 2011, p. 2).

## 4.6 Surfable window and tide

Reef waves work within narrow windows of wave height and water level. Pratte's broke "1-5 times per year" (Borrero & Nelsen, 2003, as cited in Rendle, 2016, p. 39), and Palm Beach works for easterly or southerly swells above $H_s$ 0.8 m with peak periods above 7 s (Prenzler et al., 2022); the designers attribute the narrow windows to small volume (Mead & Borrero, 2011, pp. 2, 6). This supports the first part of observation (d), that reef waves are active on few days.

The computation steps the water level through the tidal range and counts a level as surfable when the wave breaks on the reef with $\xi_b < 2$ and $Y \geq 1.6$, weighted by a synthetic spring-neap tide (Appendix A). Given a suitable swell, Palm Beach, Cables and Albany are surfable 0.59 to 0.70 of the time, Borth 0.27, Mount Maunganui and Narrowneck 0.15 to 0.16, Pratte's 0.04, and Boscombe and Bunbury never (Table 2). Under the Israeli tide (spring range 0.40 m; Rosen, 2001) the average does not rise (mean change +0.01; 75 % of cases unchanged), but the outcome becomes a function of wave height alone: for a given swell the reef works all day or not at all (Figure 7). The Palm Beach geometry under a 1 m wave rises from 0.69 to 1.00, while Mount Maunganui, which breaks only at low water, falls from 0.41 to 0. A synthetic Haifa-type reef keeps 0.87 to 0.98 of its no-tide surfable time at a 0.2 to 0.4 m range, against 0.64 to 0.68 at 4.3 m. The tidal control is documented: before its renewal Narrowneck broke regularly above an RMS height of about 0.8 m at low tide and 1.2 m at high tide (Corbett et al., 2023, p. 668), and the Albany design relied on a "low tidal range" (City of Albany, 2023, p. 2). Without a wave climate, the fractions are shares of the time with a suitable swell, not days per year.

## 4.7 Synthesis

The crest depth decides which waves break and the ramp gradient how they break; every reef refracts its waves to peel angles for advanced surfers; and a small tide makes the surfable time depend on wave height alone (Table 2). The model is one-dimensional with linear shoaling, ignores refraction and focusing across the reef (except for the peel angle) and rests on idealised geometry for six of the nine reefs, so its classes are indicators, not predictions of ride quality.
