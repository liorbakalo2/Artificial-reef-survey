# 7 Implications for the Israeli coast and Haifa

This section applies Sections 4 to 6 to the Israeli Mediterranean coast and to Haifa, where a surf reef is being considered.

## 7.1 Tide, water level and waves

The semidiurnal tide has a range of about 0.4 m at springs and 0.15 m at neaps along the whole coast [@rosen2001; @spanier2022], smaller than at any built reef (1.1 to 4.3 m; Section 1.3). Weather and season move the water level more: the 1-year low and high levels are $-0.38$ and +0.64 m, and the 100-year high is +1.10 m [@rosen2001, Table 1]. The most common $H_s$ is 0.5 to 1.0 m; summer is calm, winter brings about five storms a year above $H_s$ 3.5 m at Haifa (6 % of the records above 2 m and 1.4 % between 3.0 and 4.5 m off Haifa, 1994-2004) [@bitan2020, p. 3], and 70 % of waves come from west to west-north-west [@spanier2022; @bitan2020]; storm waves above 5 m have periods up to 15 s [@carmel1985]. Haifa Bay is sheltered: the nearshore wave exceeded 12 hours a year is 3.0 to 3.3 m against 5.1 to 5.8 m on the open coast, and the closure depth 4.7 to 5.2 m [@bitan2020]. Field observations by the author (about ten weeks, July to October 2025; Appendix G) put the smallest sea that was surfable, borderline or nearly surfable at $H_s$ 0.40 to 0.45 m ($H_{max}$ 0.50 to 0.55 m) at periods of 6.5 to 7.5 s from 284 to 294 degrees at the Shikmona buoy; height alone does not separate the classes, since a short period needs more height (a borderline sea at 4 s had $H_s$ 0.57 m, and a sea of $H_s$ 0.42 m at 5.1 s was not surfable). On days with $H_s$ 0.45 to 0.60 m the waves broke at a water depth of 1.30 to 1.50 m at the peak (1.15 m for the smallest surfable shoulder, 1.70 m for the largest sets), and in borderline seas the take-off was in about 1 m of water, falling to about 0.5 m towards the end of the ride (Appendix G, Table G3). Israeli surf is thus below the Palm Beach window ($H_s$ above 0.8 m, $T_p$ above 7 s; `\cite{prenzler2023}`{=latex}) and close to Boscombe's design range ($H_{10}$ = 1 ± 0.5 m, $T$ = 7 ± 2 s; `\cite{mead2010}`{=latex}), where a shallow crest built for small waves gave a collapsing wave (Section 4.3); the short periods help, because they soften the breaker on a given ramp.

## 7.2 A small tide: consistency, not more surf

Insight (h-A) holds that the small tide would make a reef wave more consistent and narrow the range of loads. The first part is supported (Section 4.6, Figure 7): a synthetic Haifa-type reef keeps 0.87 to 0.98 of its no-tide surfable time, and the break depends on wave height alone, although the average surfable time of the built geometries does not rise. The second part is only partly supported. The tidal range of the depth-limited crest wave is 0.31 m in Israel against 0.9 to 3.4 m at the built reefs, but damaging loads come from storms: the storm and seasonal water-level range (1.02 m) is 2.5 times the spring tide, Haifa Bay storm seas of $H_s$ 3.0 to 3.3 m exceed the @vansteeg2011 limit for single tubes by a factor of 2 to 5, and a sealed-base 6.5 x 2.75 m tube is lifted at 7 to 9 m depth (Figure 11). The material, not the tide, decides durability.

## 7.3 Israeli experience with geotextile tubes

The geotubes that tore in Israel were at Ashkelon North; no geotube was built at Netanya, which is protected by rock breakwaters (`\cite[p. 71]{statecomptroller2020}`{=latex}; `\citeyear[p. 48]{statecomptroller2024}`{=latex}). At Ashkelon North 48 submerged geotubes were laid in five clusters between July 2017 and September 2019 for about NIS 11 million, as temporary cliff protection [@statecomptroller2020, pp. 71, 83]. Several "were damaged and torn shortly after being laid in the sea, after the winter storms, and had to be repaired" (`\cite[p. 71]{statecomptroller2020}`{=latex}; translated), and in early 2019 severe damage of unknown cause halted the work [@rinat2019]. Twelve tubes were replaced in 2021, maintenance meant "sewing tears and collecting loose sheets", and the protection of one section later collapsed (`\cite[pp. 29, 48, 53]{statecomptroller2024}`{=latex}; translated). The official causes are "unprofessional laying, non-optimal filling, external damage for example from propeller blades, anchors or divers' knives" (`\cite[p. 9]{statecomptroller2024}`{=latex}; translated), and the agency estimates the life of the unarmoured sleeve used at months to a few years (p. 57). A senior Israeli coastal engineer argues that these failures do not prove geotubes unsuitable, since they can be strengthened and Haifa Bay waves are lower than those of the open coast (N. Drimer, as quoted in `\cite[p. 23]{zalul2025}`{=latex}; an expert opinion). The Israeli record agrees with the international one: the fabric is the weak point.

## 7.4 Sand mobility, burial and the sand-trap idea

The net drift runs northward, from about 400,000 m³ a year at Ziqim to 80,000 ± 20,000 m³ a year at the entrance to Haifa Bay [@spanier2022]; the Haifa Port breakwater trapped an average of 66,000 m³ a year from 1929 to 2004 [@zviely2009, p. 95]. Gross transport is far larger: by the CERC formula [@usace2002], 1 m breakers at 10 degrees move about 2,700 m³ a day, the net annual drift of the southern Carmel coast (60,000 to 72,000 m³; `\cite{zviely2007}`{=latex}) in about 22 days (derived; order of magnitude). A reef crest and toe within the 5 m closure depth lie in the active zone. Field observations by the author at the Haifa shore (Appendix G) indicate a seasonal bed-level change of about 1 m, and Google Earth satellite imagery of the coast shows more sand accumulating north of the breakwaters; this is an observation, not a survey, and has not been published, but the seasonal shoreline swing of up to ±20 m [@zviely2009] on slopes of 1:70 to 1:30 implies 0.3 to 1.3 m (derived). As long as the crest stays above the sand, a seasonal rise of the surrounding bed does not change the break, which is set by the crest and the upper ramp; the wave conditions and crest depths of Section 7.5 therefore hold through the seasons. The risk is to the structure: sand can cover the toe and the lower ramp, reach the crest of a low reef in the high-sand seasons, and, with scour and settlement (geotextile reefs settled by 1 to 2 m; Section 5.5), bury it over time. Burial is therefore a durability and maintenance risk, to be met by a crest kept well above the seasonal high bed and by monitoring, rather than a shift in surf quality; hypothesis (h-B; Appendix F) anticipated it.

A sand-trapping approach is proposed: to trap sand in the high-sand seasons (October and May, from field observations; Appendix G) and to build the reef on it under a geotextile sheet. As a sand source the idea is not supported: at the observed salient ratio a reef holds about 10 to 2,400 m³, less than 4 % of one year's net drift, and even the Black and Andrews upper bound (21,000 to 102,000 m³) is a one-time fill, not a supply (derived). As a construction saving it is plausible: building on a bed 1 m higher under a 70 x 100 m footprint saves about 7,000 m³, half the fill of a 1:12 reef section (derived). No precedent was found and the sheet would face the uplift of Section 6.3, so the idea remains a proposal that needs a pilot.

## 7.5 Design envelope for Haifa and next steps

Without Haifa bathymetry, the envelope was screened on a synthetic profile (1:50 bed, crest 10 m wide, 200 m offshore) with the methods of Section 4, the Israeli tide and periods of 6 to 8 s (Figure 12; Table 6). The cells of Figure 12 are computed for Haifa waves and the Israeli tide only; the built reefs are placed in it by their geometry (crest depth and ramp slope) for comparison and are not evaluated under their own wave climates. In the model ($\gamma$ = 0.78) the depth at which a wave breaks does not depend on the crest: at 7 s it is about 0.9, 1.0, 1.3, 1.6, 2.2 and 2.8 m for $H_0$ of 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 m (derived). A wave is shaped by the reef only if this depth exceeds the crest depth, so the crest depth sets the smallest wave the reef can use: $H_0$ 0.5 m for a crest 0.6 to 0.8 m below MSL, 0.6 m at 1.0 m, 0.8 m at 1.2 m, 1.0 m at 1.5 m and 1.5 m at 2.0 m. Larger waves break farther seaward on the ramp whatever the crest depth, and the largest winter seas ($H_s$ 3.0 to 4.5 m, 1.4 % of the time off Haifa [@bitan2020, p. 3]) break in about 4 to 6 m of water, near or seaward of the toe, where the reef no longer shapes them. What a shallow crest changes for the larger waves is the water left over the rock: the broken waves cross the crest as bores, a falling surfer meets the rock sooner, and the load on the crest armour grows as the submergence falls. The crest depth is therefore bounded on both sides. On the deep side, the most common seas ($H_s$ 0.5 to 1.0 m) must break on the reef, which by the model requires a crest no deeper than about 1.0 to 1.2 m; a crest at 1.5 m would use only $H_0$ of 1.0 m or more. On the shallow side, the end of the ride needs about 0.5 m of water (Appendix G), and the water level falls to $-0.38$ m about once a year [@rosen2001, Table 1], so the crest should lie at least about 0.9 m below MSL; a crest at 0.6 m would leave about 0.2 m of water at such lows. The recommended crest is therefore 1.0 to 1.2 m below MSL: by the model it breaks every wave from $H_0$ 0.6 to 0.8 m upward (a surfable fraction of 0.76 to 0.91 over $H_0$ 0.6 to 1.5 m under the Israeli tide), which favours the common surfable winter swells, while the smallest summer seas ($H_0$ about 0.5 m) would need a crest of about 0.8 m at the cost of that margin. Ramps of 1:8 collapse the wave, 1:12 to 1:16 give an expert wave and 1:20 a mix of advanced and spilling waves. The largest uncertainty is the breaking criterion. The field observations by the author (Section 7.1; Appendix G, Table G3) show waves of $H_s$ 0.45 to 0.60 m ($H_{max}$ 0.6 to 0.8 m) at the buoy breaking at 1.30 to 1.50 m depth, about 0.2 to 0.3 m deeper than the model breaks the same waves when $H_{max}$ is taken as $H_0$. These single-observer readings are indicative only and do not change the envelope; they suggest that the model errs on the shallow side, so that a crest at 1.0 to 1.2 m would probably also catch the smallest surfable summer seas.

Before design, the following are needed: (1) multibeam bathymetry of the site and seasonal bed surveys to test the 1 m observation; (2) a west-north-west wave climate giving the days with $H_0 \geq 0.6$ m; (3) a two-dimensional wave model or flume test including refraction and focusing; (4) calibration of the breaking criterion against field breaking depths; (5) seam and fabric tests if any geotextile is retained; and (6) before/after monitoring with controls. A Haifa reef designed this way would combine the features of the reefs that work (a large rock structure with a long, gentle ramp) with the advantage of a small tide, while accepting that it serves intermediate to advanced surfers and acts as a control point for the beach rather than as stand-alone protection.
