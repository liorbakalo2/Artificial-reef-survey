# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Purpose of surf reefs

A surf reef is a submerged structure of rock or sand-filled geotextile containers, placed in or just outside the surf zone so that incoming waves break over it in a controlled way and produce a rideable wave. Three purposes are attached to such structures [@mead2011]. The first is surf amenity: a reef can create a break where the natural one is poor, or restore one lost to coastal works. The second is coastal protection: a submerged structure dissipates wave energy and, in theory, causes sand to accumulate in its lee as a salient or tombolo; @blacka2013 review such reefs as a "softer" alternative to seawalls with negligible visual intrusion. The third is ecological enhancement: hard, stable substrate supports more species than mobile sand, and colonisation of surf reefs begins within days [@mead2011]. Reefs that combine the three are called multi-purpose reefs (MPRs).

The record is mixed. Of 32 submerged constructed reefs reviewed for New South Wales, 29 were intended for coastal protection, and the resulting shoreline often differed significantly from the design prediction; eight were built to improve surfing, and approximately half of these were considered at least partially successful; most structures settled or scoured, so that the crest level differed from the design [@blacka2013, p. ii].

## 1.2 History in brief

Artificial reefs for habitat date back to 2000 BC on the west coast of India (`\cite{kurian1995}`{=latex}, as cited in `\cite{mead2011}`{=latex}). A reef combining coastal protection, ecology and surfing was first proposed in a 1969 article in Surfer Magazine, and the first methods to quantify a surfing break were developed by J. R. Walker in the 1970s and 1980s [@mead2011]. The first reef counted in this review, Burkitts Reef at Bargara, Australia, is a natural platform reshaped by hand in 1997 [@howells2024]; the first purpose-designed structure, Cables Reef in Perth, was built in 1999 [@mead2011]. Between 1999 and 2010 seven reefs were built in five countries, most from sand-filled geotextile containers [@mead2011]. From 2011 the projects shifted to quarried rock, and an inflatable, sand-ballasted reef (the Airwave) failed on installation in 2019 (Section 3).

## 1.3 Motivation and research questions

This review addresses two questions: what effect a reef has on the waves and on surf quality for surfers of different levels, with insight that is computational where possible and grounded in coastal theory; and what effect it has on sand transport, including the combination of a surf reef with beach stabilisation. Published evaluations are mostly by the designers [for example @mead2011] or cover single sites. The main independent synthesis [@blacka2013] concentrates on New South Wales and precedes the rock reefs of 2019 and 2025. No source found tabulates all built surf reefs with traceable geometry, cost, material and outcome, and none relates their geometry to surf quality.

The practical motivation is a planned surf reef at Haifa, on the Mediterranean coast of Israel, where conditions differ from those of the built reefs in three ways. The tide is very small, about 0.4 m at spring tide [@rosen2001], against 1.1 to 4.3 m at the reefs surveyed here (for the three south-west Australian reefs this is the predicted annual range, not a spring range; Section 3). Waves are mostly small, with significant heights $H_s$ of 0.5 to 1.0 m the most common class [@spanier2022; @bitan2020]. Sand moves strongly with the seasons; field observations by the author (Appendix G) indicate a seasonal bed-level change of the order of 1 m at Haifa (Section 7). The analysis was guided by eight working hypotheses, drawn from field experience and the literature, labelled (a) to (h): (a) reef volume, (b) sand transport, (c) soft materials, (d) the number of surfable days, (e) the failure mechanism of tubes, (f) depth gap and ramp length, (g) surfer level and (h) the Israeli setting. They served only as questions to investigate; the findings of Sections 3 to 7 are discussed in Section 8 on their own terms, and Appendix F records for each hypothesis whether the evidence supports it and whether it is consistent with published work or new.

The research questions are:

1. Which surf reefs have been built, of what size, material and cost, and how did they perform and last (Section 3)?
2. How does a reef change the waves, and what surf quality results for surfers of different levels, as set by depth gap, ramp length, breaker type, peel angle and tide (Section 4)?
3. What effect have surf reefs had on sand transport and the shoreline, and can a surf reef be combined with beach stabilisation where beaches are nourished (Section 5)?
4. Which materials survive in the breaking zone, and why do soft structures fail (Section 6)?
5. What do the answers imply for a reef at Haifa (Section 7)?

Section 2 gives the methods, Section 8 the discussion and Section 9 the conclusions. Appendix A documents the method for each reef, Appendix B shows the three-dimensional reconstructions, Appendix C holds the full data table, Appendix D covers the other structures named by @bar2023 and the Israeli geotube projects, Figure E1 (Appendix E) shows where the depth profiles and peel-angle lines were taken on each reef, and Appendix F lists the working hypotheses and their status.
