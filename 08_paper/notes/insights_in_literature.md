## 0. How to read this file

- **Verdict codes.** ALREADY IN LITERATURE = the core claim is stated or supported by a source we can cite. PARTLY = some part is supported, another part is not found, or sources disagree. NEW (not found) = no source found in the sources searched. CONTRADICTED = a source says otherwise.
- **Page numbers.** For local PDFs, "p.N" is the PDF page (the page index from the file, not the printed page number). Printed numbers may differ, for example in the Rendle thesis (printed p.70 is near PDF p.74).
- **Source status.** [READ] = text read by this agent in the local PDF or the open web copy. [ABSTRACT] = only an abstract or a search-result snippet was seen. [SECONDARY] = quoted in another source; original not opened. [NOTE] = taken from a literature note in 08_paper/notes/ or 08_paper/lit/; sources cited there were not re-read here.
- **Quotes.** Quotes are copied from extracted text. Where the PDF text layer is OCR-like, the quote is trimmed to a clean sentence. Nothing is paraphrased inside quotation marks.
- **Gaps.** The Mount Maunganui PDF in papers/ (6 pages, 1,476 characters) is a scan and was not OCR'd. Two Albany-related RWR web PDFs and the New Zealand Geographic PDF have almost no text layer; their OCR intake texts were checked instead (05_qa/papers_intake/albany_rwr_pdfs_ocr.md, nzgeo_moores_2006_artificial_surf_reefs_ocr.md). ## 2. Insight (a): Reefs need a lot of volume. Small reefs such as Pratte's Reef simply do not work.

**Insight in one line.** Reef volume is the main limit on a surf reef; small structures fail to produce consistent surfing waves.

**Verdict: ALREADY IN LITERATURE** (the direction is well supported; the size threshold is not established, and one small reef is claimed to work).

**Supporting sources**
- [MB11] PDF p.3, on Pratte's Reef: "Unfortunately, this project was doomed from the outset due to the scale problem described above." Same page: "the smallest ever MPR (1,350 m3) was built very close to the shore and did not provide any surfing amenity." [READ]
- [MB11] PDF p.2, reporting Shand (2011): "He concludes that the single largest limitation to building a successful artificial reef in terms of surfing is the issue of scale." [READ, secondhand for Shand]
- [MB11] PDF p.2: "MPR structures must be much larger in volume than most built to date to not be dependent on the joint occurrence of a small range of wave and tide conditions." [READ]
- [MB11] PDF p.6 (lessons): "Small structures result in a reduced amenity due to varying wave period, and varying tide levels [2]." [READ]
- [BLA13] PDF p.40 (on MPR feasibility): "...the large required reef volume and limited longshore extent required to produce surfable waves also makes these structures inefficient..." [READ; sentence trimmed at the end]
- [RND16] PDF p.41, Table 2.1 (current ASR projects by size of sand-filled containers, adapted from Jackson and Corbett 2007): Boscombe 13,000 and Kovalam 4,300 as printed (unit given in the table as SFC; other columns not used here). [READ, table, not quoted]

**Contradicting or qualifying sources**
- [MB11] PDF p.2 on Cable Station: the 5,000 m3 reef "Pattiaratchi [13] reported an increase from 5-7 surfable days at this site prior to reef construction to 142 surfable days following construction." This is a small-reef counterexample, but the same page calls Cable Station a "fickle surfing break" and says the site is sheltered (small wave climate, narrow swell window). [READ; the 142-day figure is secondary, from [PAT03]]
- [RND16] PDF p.42 (Cable Station): "Pitt (2010) states that Cable Reef, in its current form, has failed to meet the design brief" (the thesis cites Pitt 2010 as the opposing view). [READ]
- [MB11] PDF p.2 on Narrowneck: "Although Narrowneck Reef is the largest yet built (some 70,000 m3 – [8]), volume is still an issue with respect to creating a new surfing break." [READ] This says volume alone is not enough.

**How the paper should phrase it.** "Consistent with Mead and Borrero (2011) and Shand (2011), reef volume is widely identified as the main constraint on surfing performance; the smallest reef (Pratte's, about 1,350 m3) gave no surfing amenity, but a 5,000 m3 reef (Cable Station) is reported to have produced 142 surfable days in one study, so the size threshold itself remains unresolved."

## 3. Insight (b): Little measurable effect on sand transport. Gold Coast claims protection, but the shoreline shows no change. Evaluate the sediment question at sites WITHOUT nourishment.

**Insight in one line.** Surf reefs have little measurable shoreline effect where there is no nourishment; Gold Coast claims are hard to attribute because sand is nourished continuously.

**Verdict: ALREADY IN LITERATURE** for non-nourished sites (Boscombe, Mount Maunganui, Pratte's, and the review-level finding). **PARTLY** for the Gold Coast (Narrowneck), where the literature disagrees.

**Supporting sources**
- [BLA13] PDF p.6 (executive summary ES.3), a review of 32 submerged or reef structures: "Approximately half of the 'protection' structures had no significant accretionary impact on shoreline alignment compared to the predicted morphological response;" [READ] This is the strongest single support.
- [BLA13] PDF p.115 (Mount Maunganui, appendix A.3.4, after Weppe et al.): "Any effects of the reef on shoreline position are subtle and within the natural variation of shoreline position; No noticeable salient developed;" [READ]
- [BLA13] PDF p.129 (Pratte's, appendix figure A.17 context): "After several years of beach and bathymetry monitoring the reef was reported to have no discernible impact on either the surrounding bathymetry or the beach." [READ]
- [BLA13] PDF p.82 (Boscombe, citing Rendle and Davidson 2012): "...there has been no noticeable long-term impact of the reef on the shoreline (0 m contour) alignment (Rendle and Davidson, 2012)." [READ]
- [RND16] PDF p.130 (Boscombe): "The remainder 11,100 m3 remained on the beach, spread in the area between Boscombe Pier and the first groyne." This is a non-reef explanation for the salient the designer reported (ASR Ltd claim, PDF p.90: "the development of a large salient in the lee of the reef"). [READ]
- [RT06] abstract (secondary; not verified here): the review found shoreline erosion in the lee of most submerged structures. Treat as ABSTRACT. [SECONDARY]

**Contradicting or qualifying sources**
- [RND16] PDF p.43 (Narrowneck, citing Turner 2006): "...the reef has proven been effective in stabilising the beach and a salient is generally present (Turner, 2006)." [READ] This is the opposing view at Narrowneck.
- [BLA13] PDF p.110 (Narrowneck): "This is simply an artefact of the somewhat arbitrary location of the reference line." The review says the claimed 20-50 m widening is a misreading of the monitoring report. [READ] This supports Lior's scepticism on the Gold Coast.
- [COR23] PDF p.1: "...the reef has been subject to significant seabed changes which has resulted in burial of sections of the original structure as well as the damage and subsequent loss of individual GSCs." [READ] This shows bed change near the reef, not a shoreline claim.
- [BLA13] PDF p.106: the Narrowneck beach has "a net littoral drift of approximately 500,000 m3 per year to the north" (the review's setting for sediment transport at that site). [READ]
- Gold Coast nourishment and the City's claim of offshore sand gains: see [NOTE] 08_paper/notes/sediment.md section 4 and 3.10. Not re-read here.

**How the paper should phrase it.** "Consistent with Blacka et al. (2013, p.6), about half of 29 coastal-protection reefs reviewed had no significant accretionary impact; at non-nourished sites (Mount Maunganui, Pratte's, Boscombe) monitoring found no noticeable shoreline effect. At Narrowneck the literature disagrees (Turner 2006, cited by Rendle 2016, against the re-reading by Blacka et al. 2013), and the sand supply there is continuous, so the Gold Coast cannot be used to attribute shoreline change to the reef."

## 4. Insight (c): Soft structures (geotextile tubes/containers) do not survive in the breaking zone for long. Geotubes support marine life and are the cheapest structure.

**Insight in one line.** Geotextile tubes and containers are damaged or lost in the breaking zone within a short time; they are the cheapest option and support marine life.

**Verdict: PARTLY.** Loss and burial of containers in the breaking zone are documented. The "not long" claim is not quantified in the sources. The "cheapest" and "marine life" parts are not found in the local sources.

**Supporting sources**
- [COR23] PDF p.1, Narrowneck: "After almost 20 years since construction, the reef has been subject to significant seabed changes which has resulted in burial of sections of the original structure as well as the damage and subsequent loss of individual GSCs." Same page: this "reduced its performance as a coastal protection structure and reduced surfing amenity." [READ]
- [RND16] PDF pp.42-43, Narrowneck: "Seabed fluctuations and damaged containers lowered the ASR..." and a second installment of 90 bags was added in April 2001 "after seabed fluctuations caused bags to be lost during the winter and, scour and accretion at the toe of the reef." [READ]
- [MB11] PDF p.3, Mount Maunganui: "during construction, one of the largest containers was damaged beyond repair and was removed" and "it was some 9 months before replacement containers were put into the 60 m long x 6 m wide gap in the reef". [READ]
- [RND16] PDF p.272 (general lessons): "In the event that the ASR fails to deliver or begins to disintegrate at the end of its design lifespan, as has happened with two of the ASR projects to date (e.g. Pratte's Reef, an ASR in California and Mt. Managui ASR in New Zealand)." [READ]
- [SON12] abstract (lab, GSC structures): "A new failure mode of GSCs was identified through the model experiments and named as 'uplift and drift'." [ABSTRACT]
- [BLA13] PDF p.44 (placement, citing Evans and Ranasinghe 2001): "Splitting/bursting of geotextile tubes due to fall velocity and touch down impact" is a placement risk. [READ]

**Contradicting or qualifying sources**
- [RND16] PDF p.30, ASR Ltd (2006) claims, not independent: "torn tubes only lose sediment within a small area either side of the tear and so caused no serious damage to the structure" and "When correctly installed, failures are rare." [READ]
- [BLA13] PDF p.47: "outer layer containers now have a design life of approximately 20 years (Geofabrics, 2013). Improvements to the geotextile bag materials such as vandal deterrent layers and improved ultra violet (UV) light resistance have contributed to achieving this life." [READ]
- [BLA13] PDF p.47: "The expected design life of geotextile materials has been difficult to quantify." [READ] This supports the "not quantified" point.
- [RND16] PDF p.30: a material spec with a "25-year manufacturer's guarantee" (Noosa ASR). [READ]
- Marine life: [RND16] PDF p.40 gives reef biomass context only in general terms; no source found on geotube-specific colonisation in the local set. Israeli ecology: [NOTE] 08_paper/notes/israel.md section 5.6 (not re-read). **Verdict for this sub-claim: NEW (not found).**
- Cost: [BLA13] PDF p.83 reports cost rates per cubic metre for ASR/MPR, but makes no comparison with other structures. **Verdict for "cheapest": NEW (not found).**

**How the paper should phrase it.** "Geotextile containers in the breaking zone have been lost or buried within about 20 years (Corbett et al. 2023; Rendle 2016), and damage during construction is reported (Mead and Borrero 2011). The manufacturer-based design life of about 20 years for outer-layer containers (Blacka et al. 2013, citing Geofabrics 2013) is not independently checked. Claims that geotubes are the cheapest structure, or that they support marine life, are not supported by the sources reviewed so far."

**Insight in one line.** Reef waves are surfable on few days only; the Boscombe wave was too steep and only bodyboarders could ride it.

**Verdict: PARTLY.** "Few days and specific conditions" is in the literature. The "bodyboarders only" detail at Boscombe is contradicted by the one source that tested the wave with surfers. The "too steep" part is supported.

**Supporting sources**
- [MB11] PDF p.2 (Cable Station): "...the common perception is that Cable Station does not regularly produce high-quality surfing waves" and the site is sheltered with a "narrow swell window" (also see section 2). [READ]
- [MB11] PDF p.2: the reef gives "a fickle surfing break that does not consistently produce [good waves]" (the sentence is cut in the extract at the word "produce"). [READ, trimmed]
- [RND16] PDF p.42 (Pratte's, after Borrero and Nelson 2003): "Prattes Reef did not perform to expectations, there few reports of wave breaking at the reef only 1-5 times per year." [READ; OCR text, trimmed]
- [BLA13] PDF p.105 (one appendix reef): "...the number of good surfable days per year is small." The review's general statement on p.40 is that "the surfing amenity produced by those that were completed has generally failed to live up to public expectation (Shand, 2011)." [READ]
- [COR23] PDF p.4: "Ride lengths and peel angles are expected to be variable in response to changes in adjacent sand bars that affect the bathymetry surrounding the reef." [READ] This is a model statement, but it fits the "specific conditions" point.
- [RND16] PDF p.74 (Boscombe, Davidson 2010, after the design audit): "the wave is too challenging in comparison to the design criteria, meaning novice surfers and intermediates would not be able to surf the Boscombe Reef" and "the wave is steep and frequently plunging". [READ]
- [RND16] PDF p.74: "Since the reef crest is fully exposed at low tide, no surfing is possible at this state of the tide. At high tide, given the wave climate, surfing is not possible due to the depth of water above the..." [READ, trimmed]

**Contradicting or qualifying sources**
- **Bodyboarders only: not found.** The thesis gives the same Davidson (2010) audit as "restricted to more advanced level surfers" (PDF pp.73-74), not bodyboarders only. The Opunake design (TRC09 PDF p.9) was intended "to produce a fast hollow wave, which would be suitable for surfers and body boarders". **Verdict for the bodyboarder sub-claim: CONTRADICTED in its specific form** (the source says advanced surfers, not bodyboarders only).

At Boscombe the independent audit found the wave steep, plunging and restricted to advanced surfers (Davidson 2010, cited in Rendle 2016), which contradicts the narrower claim that only bodyboarders could ride it."

## 6. Insight (e): Mechanism of tube failure. Most wave power acts at the break; it lifts the geotextile tube up and then down, which is why most tubes tear.

**Insight in one line.** Wave power peaks at the break; the tube is lifted and dropped, and most tubes tear as a result.

**Verdict: PARTLY.** The load part (peak at the break, and uplift and suction on the tube) is supported by [BD23]. The tearing part is not modelled in [BD23] and not found elsewhere. The claim that "most tubes tear" is not supported; the literature names undersizing, sand coverage, seabed change and construction damage as causes.

**Supporting sources**
- [BD23] PDF p.1 (abstract): "the most critical state, regarding the sliding stability of the Geotube, occurs, typically just before breaking." [READ]
- [BD23] PDF p.8: "A negative (to the up-wave direction) horizontal load occurs simultaneously with a positive (uplift) vertical load." [READ]
- [BD23] PDF p.10: "...it is safer to assume that the dynamic pressure penetrates between the seabed and the bottom of the structure, and in the critical situation contributes to the uplifting of the Geotube." [READ]
- [SON12] abstract: "uplift and drift" is a new failure mode of GSCs in lab tests. [ABSTRACT]

**Contradicting or qualifying sources**
- [BD23] PDF p.2: "Common reasons for failure are related to too-small size Geotubes installed or the loss of effectiveness due to sand coverage [18,20]." [READ] This names size and burial, not tearing, as the common failure causes.
- Tearing is not in [BD23]: keyword search of the full text gives 0 hits for "tear" and 0 for "fabric". The model treats the bottom as a rigid boundary (Appendix A, PDF p.14; note [NOTE] drimer_force_model.md 4.8). **The tearing part has no source in the set.**
- [RND16] PDF p.30 (ASR Ltd 2006 claim): "When correctly installed, failures are rare." [READ] Company statement; contradicts "most tubes tear" if taken as independent.
- [COR23] PDF p.1: the Narrowneck damage is linked to seabed change and burial, not to a stated tearing mechanism. [READ]

**How the paper should phrase it.** "The Bar and Drimer (2023) model shows that the critical sliding load on a geotube occurs just before breaking, with a negative horizontal load and a positive uplift load (pp.1, 8, 10). That model does not treat fabric tearing. Most reported failures are attributed to undersizing and sand coverage (Bar and Drimer 2023, p.2). A tearing mechanism needs a separate source (fabric strength and seam tests), which we have not found."

## 7. Insight (f): Depth gap and length: a long, gradual ramp can give a good wave; a sudden rise to 1-1.5 m gives a too-hollow wave. Quantify with wave height (breaker index, Iribarren number, Mead & Black breaking intensity).

**Insight in one line.** A gradual seabed ramp gives a good wave; a sudden shallow rise gives a too-hollow wave; quantify with breaker indices.

**Verdict: PARTLY.** The role of seabed features in break type is in the literature. No source quantifies the "long gradual ramp versus sudden rise" effect. Breaker indices exist, but the Mead and Black breaking-intensity formula is not usable as reproduced (LITERATURE.md item 4).

**Supporting sources**
- [BLA13] PDF p.38 (on Mead and Black 2001): the seabed features (ramp, platform, focus, wedge, ledge, ridge, pinnacle): "Each of these components influence either the pre-conditioning, break point or break type." [READ]
- [RND16] PDF p.37: "The peel angle of the wave (between the breaking crest and the shoreline) is optimised and the form of the wave is steeper and plunging in form, as opposed to a spilling breaker type." [READ]
- [WAL75] PDF p.5: "The greater the height, the more skill that is required for the surfer to ride the wave" (OCR text; "to" appears as "Co"). [READ]
- [RND16] PDF p.43 (Narrowneck): "Narrowneck does not break as steeply as other natural world class reef breaks in the nearby area under the same or similar oceanographic conditions." [READ]
- [BLA13] PDF p.43 (Table 2.3, rock armour sizes for reef depths around 2 m): shows the cost of placing a shallow reef, not its wave quality. [READ, table]
- Breaker indices: Weggel (1972) slope-dependent breaker index and Battjes (1974) surf similarity are verified in [NOTE] 08_paper/lit/LITERATURE.md items 3.4-3.5, with P-level sources read there.

**Contradicting or qualifying sources**
- [MB11] PDF p.3, Mount Maunganui: the reef "produced very good surfing waves which broke as designed in a hollow, tubing manner". [READ] A hollow wave was rated good here, so "too hollow" is not automatically bad.
- [RND16] PDF p.74 (Davidson 2010, Boscombe): "the wave is steep and frequently plunging, a ride length is possible of 20 m and 40 m to the left and right" — the steepness problem is about ride length as well as hollowness. [READ, trimmed]
- Mead and Black breaking-intensity formula: not usable as reproduced; see [NOTE] LITERATURE.md item 4 (vortex ratio check gives about 0.82, far below the reported field values).
- [BLA13] PDF p.68: "There are no models available off the shelf which directly assess surfability." [READ] Quantification is at an early research stage.

**How the paper should phrase it.** "Break type and hollowness depend on seabed features and depth (Mead and Black 2001, as summarised in Blacka et al. 2013, p.38). The literature does not quantify the effect of a long, gradual ramp against a sudden shallow rise; breaker-index methods (Weggel 1972; Battjes 1974) can be used to test it, but no surfability model is established (Blacka et al. 2013, p.68)."

## 8. Insight (g): Surfer level. Beginners need a very slowly breaking (spilling) wave, which reefs do not create; once standing, difficulty depends on the peel angle (Hutt, Black & Mead 2001). About 30 degrees is for good surfers making high-level turns; smaller angles are faster and harder.

**Insight in one line.** Peel angle sets how fast a surfer must travel; beginners need large peel angles and experienced surfers can handle small ones; 30 degrees is a high-skill value.

**Verdict: ALREADY IN LITERATURE**, with one wording correction: Walker's 30 degrees is a minimum peel angle for a 6-ft plunging wave, not a skill level.

**Supporting sources**
- [WAL75] PDF p.5: "This type of wave is most suitable for a beginning surfer who has not developed skill levels sufficient to negotiate a turn." (The sentence refers to waves with a 90-degree peel angle; the text is OCR, with "suit;able" as an artefact.) [READ]
- [WAL75] PDF p.5: "A peel angle of 30 is suggested as a minimum for a plunging breaker at 6-foot height, For waves of greater height, the minimum peel angle is increased." [READ]
- [WAL75] PDF p.5: "A peel angle of 0 degrees indicates that the wave breaks simultaneously along the entire crest" (the passage goes on to call this condition a "close out"). [READ, trimmed]
- [HBM01] Table 3, as reproduced in Lewis et al. (2015), p.15: peel-angle limit 90 deg for level 1 (beginner), 70 for level 2, 60, 55, 50, 40 for level 6, **29 for level 7** (top amateur) and **27 for level 8** (professional). [SECONDARY, read in LITERATURE.md item 1; not checked against the original]
- [BLA13] PDF p.38: "These wave breaking characteristics were related to surfers' skill level (Walker, 1974; Hutt et al., 2001)." [READ]
- [NOTE] LITERATURE.md item 4.1: V_s = c / sin(alpha) (peel-rate speed) is a derived relation; small peel angles are fast. [NOTE, derived]

**Contradicting or qualifying sources**
- Walker's 30 degrees [WAL75] p.5 is a minimum for a 6-ft plunging breaker, and the minimum rises with wave height. The "30 is for good surfers" link comes from Hutt's table (level 7 = 29 deg), which is only secondary here. The paper should not say "30 degrees is a skill level" without the Hutt table in hand.
- Beginner spilling waves: the sources describe beginners as needing a large peel angle (straight-in wave), not a spilling type. Lior's "spilling" word is a different axis from peel angle. Rendle 2016 PDF p.37 says spilling is "not as thrilling for the rider". [READ]
- Breaker types for beginners: the Albany design aims at "beginner to intermediate abilities" with "plunging to spilling" waves ([ALB23] PDF p.1-2). [READ]

**How the paper should phrase it.** "Peel angle sets the surfer's speed across the wave; beginners need large angles, approaching 90 degrees (Walker 1975, p.5; Hutt et al. 2001, Table 3 as reproduced by Lewis et al. 2015), and the Hutt et al. table gives about 29 degrees for the highest amateur level. Walker's 30-degree figure is a minimum for a 6-ft plunging wave, which rises with height."

## 9. Insight (h): Israel. (A) The tide range is much smaller, so the reef wave would be more consistent and the range of wave power smaller; the Netanya/Ashkelon tubes still tore. (B) Strong sand transport at Haifa, about 1 m seasonal bed change; the reef can sink; high-sand seasons (October, May) could trap sand cheaply under a geotextile sheet.

**Insight in one line.** A small tide makes a reef wave more consistent; tubes tore in Israel, so the geotextile may be at fault; Haifa has about 1 m seasonal bed change, which can bury a reef but can also trap sand.

**Verdict: PARTLY.** The tide-range point is supported in principle by the reef literature (tide controls breaking), but the Israeli tide range is only one of several controls. The geotube failure is confirmed for Ashkelon North (not Netanya) by [NOTE]. The Haifa bed-change, sand-trap and seasonal-month claims are not found in the literature.

**Sub-claims**

**(h-A1) Small tide gives a more consistent reef wave.** Verdict: PARTLY.
- [COR23] PDF p.6 (Narrowneck, before and after renewal): waves broke regularly "when the RMS wave height was greater than ~0.8 m at low tide and ~1.2 m at high tide". After renewal, "greater than ~0.5 m at low tide and 0.8 m at high tide." [READ] Tide sets the threshold strongly; the notes give a Narrowneck spring range of 1.31 m (08_paper/notes/israel.md section 2; not re-checked here).
- [COR23] PDF p.4: "During average wave conditions at low tide, frequency of wave breaking was increased by ~30%." [READ]
- [ALB23] PDF p.2: the design assumes a "Low tidal range - maximises the duration that swell will break on the reef structure." [READ, design claim]
- [RND16] PDF p.69: the Boscombe design claim: "The reef will work through a much larger tidal range than the sandbars used now." [READ, design claim]
- [RND16] PDF p.74: as built, the crest is exposed at low tide and too deep at high tide for surfing (see section 5). [READ]
- [MB11] PDF p.6: "varying tide levels" reduce amenity for small structures (section 2). [READ]
- Israeli tide (Haifa): spring range about 0.4 m, neap about 0.15 m; 1-year water-level variability 1.02 m (storm and season), 100-year high 1.10 m. [NOTE] 08_paper/notes/israel.md section 2 (Rosen 2001, Table 1; Spanier and Zviely 2023). The note says storm variability dominates the load range, not the astronomical tide.

**(h-A2) Netanya/Ashkelon tubes tore, so the geotextile may have been the problem.** Verdict: PARTLY.
- [NOTE] 08_paper/notes/israel.md section 5.1: the torn tubes were at Ashkelon North, not Netanya; "Netanya: evidence that no geotube was built or contracted" (israel_geotubes_netanya.md section 5). **The Netanya part of Lior's note is contradicted by the notes.**
- Literature support for "the geotextile is the weak point": [COR23] PDF p.1, [MB11] PDF p.3, [RND16] PDF p.272 (see section 4). Cause in Israel: unproven (israel.md section 7).

**(h-B1) About 1 m seasonal bed-level change near Haifa.** Verdict: NEW (not found in the literature). [NOTE] israel.md section 4.3: no published seasonal profile or bed-level series for Haifa was found; a derived estimate gives 0.3-1.3 m. [READ-level notes: Zviely et al. 2009 p.95, trapped sand about 66,000 m3/yr on average.]

**(h-B2) High-sand seasons are October and May.** Verdict: NEW. [NOTE] israel.md section 4.4: "No source gives months." Bitan and Zviely (2020, p.13) recommend starting nourishment at the end of winter (April-May); the GeoMare opinion (2024, p.6) places natural feeding in transition seasons.

**(h-B3) Trap sand in high seasons, cover with geotextile, build on it.** Verdict: NEW. [NOTE] israel.md section 7: "No precedent found; port breakwater trapped about the full drift but is a much larger structure" (Zviely et al. 2009, p.95). No local source covers trapping sand under a geotextile.

**How the paper should phrase it.** "A small tidal range should make a reef wave more consistent through the day: the Narrowneck breaking thresholds shift by about 0.3-0.4 m between low and high water (Corbett et al. 2023, p.6), and the Boscombe design relied on a larger tidal range (Rendle 2016, p.69). The Haifa tide is small (about 0.4 m spring range; Rosen 2001 via the notes), but storm-driven level variability (about 1 m over a year) is larger, so the tide is not the main control. Tube damage is reported at Ashkelon North; the causes are not established. Haifa's bed-level change, sand-trapping and season claims have no published source yet."
