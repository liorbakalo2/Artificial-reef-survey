## 0. Bottom line (for the paper's section 7)

1. **Every geotextile-container surf reef in the survey lost shape, crest height or bags in the surf zone; none of the rock reefs did.** Geotextile: Boscombe, Narrowneck (maintained since 2001, renewed 2017-18), Pratte's (removed), Mount Maunganui (partly removed 2014), Opunake (never completed), Kovalam (non-functional). Rock: Cables (since 1999), Borth, Burkitts, Palm Beach, Albany (opened 2025, rocks moved by storm and by divers). The designer himself, Shaw Mead, now says "the sandbags were an evolutionary deadend" and "the fatal flaw of the bags was their propensity for failure" (sections 3 and 5).
2. **The failure mechanism named most often is point damage followed by shape change**: "as soon as even a single sand-filled container broke and lost sand, the whole structure changed shape due to the fluid nature of sand" (Mead, 2023). Named causes: anchors (Narrowneck), propeller strike or vandalism (Boscombe, contested), debris strikes (Kovalam), over-filling and ruptures during construction (Mount Maunganui), hydraulic reworking of the fill ("pillowing", Boscombe), UV and abrasion of the fabric (Pratte's: torn by hand after about 8 years), knives and spears (Narrowneck).
3. **At Boscombe one bag was 12.5% of the reef.** Two T4 containers of 746.7 m3 each (Rendle) made up 1,493 m3 of 11,900 m3 (12.5%); the designers' claim that "torn tubes only lose sediment within a small area either side of the tear" was falsified (Rendle p.121).
4. **Depth when failing was shallow in most cases**: crest submergence 0.4-0.5 m (Mount Maunganui, Boscombe), 0.9 m (Pratte's after phase 2), 1.0 m (Narrowneck design; built 1.5 m) and about 1 m at low tide (Airwave). The bags that fail first are crest bags in the break zone (Boscombe upper dissipating section) or bags in deeper water exposed to anchors (Narrowneck). This is the observation Lior's insight (e) and (f) relies on; the wave-force mechanism is in `08_paper/notes/drimer_force_model.md` (not repeated here).
5. **Life of geotextile in the surf zone**: the industry design-life figure is "approximately 20 years" for outer-layer containers, possibly 40 years with marine growth (Blacka 2013, p.35, citing Geofabrics 2013 and ASR 2008b, both interested sources). Field record: 1 to 8 years before major loss (Boscombe 2 winters, Pratte's 8 years to hand-tearable fabric, Kovalam first monsoon, Fiji Maqai "from almost day one"), and the renewal of Narrowneck after about 17 years. At Narrowneck in 2017 the designers found "no significant advances" in available geotextile (Corbett 2023).
6. **Rock costs more per m3 and lasts**: Blacka (2013, Table 4.4, 2013 AUD) gives Cables (rock) A$457/m3, Pratte's A$504, Mount Maunganui A$545, Kovalam A$351, Boscombe A$384, Opunake A$274, Narrowneck A$56 [S via intake]; ICM: "building any kind of structure out of rock is significantly more expensive, but it's also significantly more durable and stable" (conference talk) and Palm Beach about AU$700/m3 versus about AU$30/m3 at Narrowneck [card]. Note the geotextile per-m3 prices are not comparable because the geotextile reefs were incomplete or later removed.

## 2. Boscombe in detail (the best-documented failure; Rendle thesis)

All [P], **REND** printed pages.
- Visible change after the first winter (2009-10): "the Boscombe ASR visibly changed shape ... sediment moved in the containers through the hydraulic action of the waves ... a misshaped appearance ... referred to as a 'pillowing' effect. ... Sediment has been displaced from seaward extent of the geotextile containers towards the landward extent, reducing the elevation of the outer edge ... and further elevating the landward section" (p.94, PDF 97).
- Major damage after the second winter, 2011, to "one of the largest size containers known as a T4"; "It is contested as to whether this was caused by geotextile container weakness, accidental damage from a boat propeller, or through deliberate vandalism." A second T4 "was damaged due to the lack of support from the lost neighboring container, and therefore removed in 2012" (p.94). Crest changes 0.25 m up to 0.5 m down across profiles; gap 4 m, then 8-10 m; 2 m crest depression in April 2011; the council closed the reef in April 2011 because the gap "enhanced turbulence over the reef crest, endangering surfers and swimmers" (p.96).
- Volume: T4 units 5.9 m wide x 2.3 m high x 65.5 m long, 746.7 m3 each (her Table 5.3); total lost 1,493.4 m3 (text: 1,492.4) = 12.5% of 11,900 m3 [P, p.98]. "not all filled to the capacity specified ... one or more of the containers are missing from the original designs" (Carpenter 2013, pers. comm., quoted p.98). Causes of under-filling given by ASR: wrong grain size supplied, mixed grain size that had to be sieved, bad weather, "final rush to complete" (p.98).
- Crest: "The final crest height of the Boscombe ASR was 0.5 m higher than the final design height, this is a fundamental design flaw" (abstract, PDF 6; p.122); "Additional hydraulic stress was caused in the larger upper bags by the exposure to extended surface interaction due to the ASR's increased crest elevation (+0.5 m)" (p.122, PDF 125). Missing smaller side bags "would have supported the larger bags" (p.122).
- Repairs: "tears that were poorly repaired and patches that came loose after a few storms"; "Two apparent tears in the SFCs with temporary tie-wrap stitching were located" (Richards 2009, quoted p.120, PDF 123). Fill ports needing proper sealing (p.122).
- Designers' claims tested: "when correctly installed, failures are rare" (ASR Ltd 2006) and "torn tubes only lose sediment within a small area either side of the tear" (p.121): "Unfortunately, this has not proved to be accurate. Damage to one container equated to a large volume loss of 12.5% of the entire volume" (p.121, PDF 124). Postulated life of 25 years: "it is unlikely the ASR at Boscombe will survive for the proposed lifespan (25 years) without multiple repairs" (p.121). "The geotextile material is not as resistant to mechanical damage as hard coastal construction materials (rock or concrete)" (p.121, citing Black et al. 2006). It "has been proven to be more resilient in a covered or submerged environment" (sand dune stabilisation, groynes in low-energy systems, deeper installations) (p.121).
- Deliberate damage hypothesis: "it has also been suggested that the reef was intentionally damaged by fishermen, those that had been displaced by the structure and the no-go-zone (this is a verbal account ...)" (p.121); unconfirmed.
- Debris: material still washing ashore on 26 December 2017 [card: boscombe-surf-reef, Newsroom caption; also NR21 photo caption "Debris believed to be from the Boscombe artificial reef washes up on Boxing Day 2017" [P]]. Environmental concern: "arguably introduced plastic and microplastics waste into the marine environment" (p.122).
- Independent list of typical geotextile reef problems (from Mead & Borrero 2011 and Rendle & Davidson 2012): "ruptured containers during construction; sediment inundation during filling; unsealed filling ports; hydraulic reshaping of fill material by waves; damage by vessel impact; potential damage by wave impact" [P, Blacka p.72, PDF 84].
- Insurance: council obtained a £306,531 settlement in 2013; ASR liquidated Sept 2012 [card:boscombe-surf-reef, ref R1 Wikipedia; tertiary, not re-read].

## 3. Narrowneck in detail

- Construction: "just over 400 Terrafix geotextile 'mega-containers', each measuring 20 m in length, 3-4.5 m in diameter ... and weighing 160-300 tonnes", split-hull hopper dredge, GPS positioned; ~50 bags added 2001-2007; 90-100 containers per month at peak [P, Blacka A.2.5, p.97, PDF 109]. 
- Storm record and fabric changes: stable "in waves up to 8-10 m" (Black 2001; maximum, not significant height); by 2002 nine storms with Hs > 5 m "as well as other impacts such as boat anchors, spears, knives, surfboards"; composite geotextiles were therefore used for later bags [P, Blacka p.98, PDF 110].
- 2011 survey: "some 10 containers have been compromised" (isopach, 3 years to 2008), burial of seaward containers, "a number of containers missing or damaged" [P, JAC12 abstract and p.4-6]. Cause: "The reef is a very popular fishing area and there has previously been clear evidence of instances where damage has been the result of: propeller damage, anchor damage, vandalism and spears from spear guns"; "the initial cause of failure is masked by the damage suffered during cyclic flapping and deflation of the container" [P, JAC12 p.7]. Containers placed 2002-2006 in the maintenance table (T1, T2, T4) show eight "not evident" or "failed" in 2011 (JAC12 Table 1, p.7). Smaller, partly filled containers "tended to develop 'ears' ... more vulnerable to flapping"; deeper-water containers "more vulnerable to anchor damage" (p.8).
- 2018-2023: "maintenance requirements are largely driven by individual GSC damage from anchors and knives ... a review of commercially available products and correspondence with manufacturers did not identify any significant advances in this regard. As a result, the renewal works necessarily adopted products developed during the original construction" [P, COR23 p.665, PDF 3]. 84 containers added in 6 months; "burial of sections of the original structure as well as the damage and subsequent loss of individual GSCs" lowered the effective crest [P, COR23 p.663, PDF 1].
- ICM managing director: "it became too good of an ecological hot spot ... the reef gets covered in fishermen on calm days and fishermen drop their anchors and they can actually damage these containers" [P, conference talk transcript ~00:52:56-00:53:17; speaker not confirmed; `Initial info from Lior/transcript- goldcoast ...txt`].
- Mead (email to Geosynthetics Magazine author, 2023): "The containers failed due to a number of reasons including anchors (Gold Coast), debris strikes (Kovalam), during construction with over-filling (Mount Reef), propellor strike (Boscombe), etc., etc." [P, GM23]. He also names Narrowneck as "sandbags maintained" among the survivors (NR21).
- Crest settlement and later deepening: see `sediment.md` section 4; Blacka (p.77-78) says the crest was not topped up after settling for safety [S via intake].

## 4. Other geotextile reefs, short entries

- **Pratte's** [P, Blacka A.11]: "Woven polypropylene bags with nylon lifting handles used for initial construction ... Woven polyester bags used for second stage" (p.116); after about 8 years, "Torn geotextile fabric and nylon lifting straps indicated damaged geocontainers; Degraded state of the polypropylene bags, such that the material could be torn by hand" (p.117). Removal cost "approximately $551,000 ($1,000 more than initial construction)" and the removal rate was 6.3 bags/day (p.119). Mead & Borrero (p.3): "removed in 2010, costing more than twice the US$300,000 it cost to build" (a different reading of the same budget).
- **Mount Maunganui** [P, MB11 p.3]: "From the onset of construction, the project was plagued by delays and cost over-runs, as well as ... ruptured SFC's, unsealed ports leading to loss of sand, and plant failure"; "one of the largest containers was damaged beyond repair and was removed ... some 9 months before replacement containers were put into the 60 m long x 6 m wide gap ... an adjacent container slumped into the gap resulting in a ~1 m lower crest height at the offshore end"; "only 2,800 m3 of the 6,500 m3 design volume currently in place". Independent report summarised by Rendle (citing Bay of Plenty Times 2014): "under-filling of the containers", "removal of one of the major containers when it was damaged, and other damage and sand leakage over time"; staged removal of the largest containers "at a cost of about NZ$60,000" [S, Rendle p.41, PDF 44; the newspaper not read]. Another card entry gives NZ$87,000 [card R2; unresolved].
- **Opunake**: 27 bags in place by April 2008 with "fine-tuning" to follow in 2009/10 [P, TRC09 p.4]; consent conditions required fill sand "similar to that of existing sands in Opunake Bay" (TRC09 p.6, Condition 7, PDF 12). Mead: "the fatal flaw" [card R5, via GM23-type article; see section 5].
- **Kovalam** [P, Rendle p.41-42, PDF 44-45]: "As parts of the reef washed ashore and loose bags are caught in fishers nets questions were raised"; the figure shows the clean-up after the first monsoon, September 2010. Designers' monsoon-season shoreline results are in `sediment.md`.
- **Bunbury Airwave**: the failure mode of a soft inflatable/sand-filled structure.
  - Independent record: the bladder was about 90% complete and not fully anchored when "an unexpectedly large, deep offshore swell" drove undertow through the line-up and tore a glued seam; removed within about three days [card:bunbury-airwave; refs R1 ABC News 2019-12-16/17, R5 Raised Water Research 2019-12-16; not re-read]. Cause of the weak seam: "Hypalon rubber, which could not be welded and so had to be glued" [card, ref R8 Bunbury Mail 2022-03-22, story 7667604; not re-read]. Fill: 140 t of washed beach sand plus air; base 12 m; height 1.6-2 m; 30-45 m offshore in about 1 m of water at low tide [card; refs R8, R9 Tracks Magazine 2019-11-15].
  - **Author's observation / personal communication (Lior Bakalo)**: Zoom interview of Lior with Troy, founder of Airwave (surname not given in the notes; presumably Troy Bottegal of Waveco), 8 October 2026 (the file's date is as recorded in the card; it is in the future relative to today's date 2026-10-15 only by notation, not confirmed): the final design used **sand instead of air** because air "did not respond well with the waves"; **a seam opened a couple of days after installation**; until then it "worked amazingly, perfect waves"; the next version is **concrete** [card:bunbury-airwave R25; unrecorded, no transcript]. This disagrees with the independent record on timing (tear during installation) and on whether installation was complete; both are kept. Waveco's current Bunbury-facing project is a granite reef for erosion protection [card R20, Waveco website 2025-09-10; not re-read]. Depth when it failed: about 1 m at low tide (~1.5 m to 2 m of structure height in 1 m of water means the crest was effectively at or above low-tide level) [card sizes; ASSUMPTION for the "effectively at the surface" reading].
- **Maqai / Qamea, Fiji (not one of our 13; useful evidence)**: geo-container breakwater built 2012 with 105 m3 of pumped beach sand; Mead: "The offshore ends of the two larger containers required maintenance from almost day one since they were constantly in the surf zone through the full range of the tide ... It was not a maintenance issue, it was a durability issue with the containers ... It was expected that the geo-fabric containers would not last indefinitely"; Cyclone Winston (Feb 2016) made it "blow apart" [P, NR21].

## 5. What designers and critics say, in their words

- Mead (to Newsroom, email): "the sandbags were an evolutionary deadend, seemed to have potential to begin with ... they are still useful for large volumes if the shape is not important." [P, NR21]. World Wave Project web text (Mead's venture): "The majority of artificial surfing reefs were made of sand filled geotextile containers due to the perception that they are easily removed if the reef project fails, but that misconception has been disproven." [P, quoted in NR21]. World Wave Project to Newsroom: "the structural failure of each of these sites is related to the use of geotextile containers, incomplete construction and/or a lack of maintenance" [P, NR21].
- Mead (email, 2023): sand containers were chosen as "a more cost-effective construction method, a lower carbon footprint ..., good substrate for marine organisms ..., 'safer' than rock or concrete and easier to remove"; "However, the fatal flaw of the bags was their propensity for failure"; "I undertook a critical review of the performance of sand-filled containers for MPR construction approximately a decade ago, which led to the change to traditional rock construction methods of the most recent MPRs built" [P, GM23].
- Mead (to Newsroom): "while the structures have failed (except for Cable Station (limestone), Borth (rock), Palm Beach (rock) and Narrowneck (sandbags maintained)), all have created surfing waves that have replicated the designs" [P, NR21]. This is an interested-party claim about wave performance; see `surf_outcomes.md` for the independent record.
- ICM: rock is "significantly more expensive, but ... significantly more durable and stable"; on a geotube reef, falls onto the soft surface are "not a bad place to land" [P, conference talk ~00:54:16, and in Lior's notes `01_source_notes/transcript_goldcoast_reef_company.md`].
- Blacka (2013): "While geotextile structures are promoted as having the ability to be used as a 'trial' that can be easily removed, such removal comes at considerable expense if not specifically considered during the design phase. Removal can also provide an environmental risk if the geotextile is torn during the reef removal process. However, there is little doubt that removal of geotextile structures is easier than rock structures" (p.72); "The expected design life of geotextile materials has been difficult to quantify ... modern staple fibre non-woven geotextiles ... outer layer containers now have a design life of approximately 20 years (Geofabrics, 2013) ... vandal deterrent layers and improved ultra violet (UV) light resistance ... possibly as long as 40 years (ASR, 2008b), which is comparable to the 50 year design life for most rock structures" (p.35); "design life ... from approximately 20 years (geotextile structures) to 50 years (concrete and rock structures)" (p.71) [P, Blacka p.35, 71, 72; PDF 47, 83, 84].
- Rendle (p.119-121): heavy needle-punched non-wovens, protective armour layer, improved seams and multi-celled containers have extended life; "The use of geotextile materials in a shallow and exposed area such as this should be carefully considered given the major SFC failure seen at Boscombe"; successful uses are in dunes, groynes in low energy, submerged installations (Mediterranean Italy, Yucatan, UAE) [P, p.120-121]. Pratte's lesson since: a geomat was placed under later bags [S, Rendle p.39, PDF 42].
