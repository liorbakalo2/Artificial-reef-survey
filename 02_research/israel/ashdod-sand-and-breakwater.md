## Motivation and who pushed for it

**Port breakwaters (1960s–2005):** built to give Israel a modern deep-water cargo port on an otherwise harborless stretch of coast; later "Jubilee Port" expansion (approved 1995, opened 2005, ≈3 billion NIS) added four container terminals [R6].

**Floating breakwater (2019–2023):** The idea traces to at least August 2019, when Ashdod's Tourism Department, under councilmember **Gershon Feldman**, explored an "ecological breakwater" — technology from "an Israeli company with international recognition" (the source's wording; re-checked 2026-09-24 and it does not say "patented," so that characterization is softened here) said to reduce wave energy while still allowing sand migration, aimed at bathers rather than surfers, developed with input from the government's Coastal Protection Committee [R9]. By October 2021 the Ashdod Urban Tourism Company's board had unanimously approved building a **temporary, seasonal floating breakwater** (~100 m long, installed for bathing season and removed in winter, modeled on a similar Rishon LeZion structure), championed by **Deputy Mayor Shimon Katzenelson** [R10]. By January 2023 the project (jointly scoped with Rishon LeZion, feasibility led by marine engineer **Alex Fayerman** and Tourism Company CEO **Orit Almozlino Raiz**) was headed to a council vote for a feasibility-study budget of ≈6 million NIS [R11]. Lior's own notes (from a conference-speaker photo) independently flag **Sharon Marck (שרון מרק)**, an Ashdod city-council member, as the person credited with blocking the plan, sourced to kan-ashdod.co.il/news/122249 — a page automated fetch could not retrieve (403); Lior can read it by hand (see Blocked sources) [R1][R2].

## Design as planned

A **temporary, removable floating breakwater** roughly 100 m long, to be installed at the start of the bathing season and dismantled at the end of it, sited at specific northern beach sections — explicitly *not* at surfing beaches — requiring Ministry of Environmental Protection approval [R10][R11]. It was framed as a first-of-its-kind pilot for Israel, developed jointly with Rishon LeZion [R14]. An earlier (2019) alternative concept was a **fixed "ecological" breakwater** using proprietary technology from an Israeli company meant to preserve natural sand migration (not confirmed as literally patented — see Motivation section), unlike Ashkelon/Netanya/Tel Aviv's older fixed breakwaters, which were said to block sand drift and harm marine ecosystems [R9]. Separately, a **permanently fixed floating breakwater** option was tested and found unsuitable for winter Mediterranean sea conditions, per reporting from that period [R12].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

**Nothing was built.** The January 2023 city-council item — a request to approve budget for a feasibility study, not final construction approval — was removed from the agenda by Mayor Yechiel Lasri after (a) a planned surfer protest at city hall was announced and (b) councilmember Sharon Marck publicly campaigned against the ≈6 million NIS cost, the absence of Israeli technical precedent, and the lack of prior public consultation [R11][R12][R13][R14]. Deputy Mayor Katzenelson, the plan's main champion, responded "if they don't want it — we don't need it" [R14]. As of the sources found (through 2023), the item has not resurfaced; no later confirmation of cancellation-vs-shelving was found this pass.

## Outcome (surfing quality / coastal protection / ecology / longevity / damage / removal, with measurements)

- **No floating breakwater exists** — surfing conditions at Ashdod's breaks (Hakshtot, Ashdod-Hshover/Port) are unaffected by it [R15][R16].
- **Port-caused sand redistribution is real and measured.** A 1996 study (Golik, Rosen, Golan, Shoshany, DiCastro & Harari, presented at ICCE) found the port trapped ~4.5 million m³ of sediment on its south side over the **34 years from 1958 (pre-construction baseline) to 1992** — **corrected 2026-09-24**: the dossier's first draft said "35 years (1961–1996)," which the paper's own abstract text does not support (its bathymetric comparisons run through 1995, but the accretion/study period it states is 1958–1992) — with beach accretion reaching >100 m near the main breakwater, measured out to 2.5 km south of it; ~2.2 million m³ (≈49% — close to, but not quite, half) of the trapped volume accumulated 1985–1995, more than half of that from the severe 1992 storms alone; the authors also found more than half of the total sediment volume still bypasses the port northward naturally [R23].
- **(pending — source blocked, see Blocked sources)** A 2025 academic reconstruction (MDPI, *Journal of Marine Science and Engineering*, "Six Decades of Morphodynamic Evolution Around Ashdod Port") using surveys from 1957–2025 reportedly states: ~4.50×10⁶ m³ accumulated in the ~2.5 km sector south of the main breakwater during 1965–1995, with a comparable sand **deficit developing north of the port**; after Ashdod Marina was built (1995–1997) accretion spread across more of the southern sectors while erosion continued and extended further downdrift (north); an **artificial northward sand transfer of ~1.457×10⁶ m³ since 2000**, with the downdrift (northern) sand deficit persisting. Re-checked 2026-09-24: the paper is still 403-blocked to automated fetch (obtained only via a search-engine snippet, never the full text), and its specific numbers are not independently corroborated by any other source fetched — note they also describe a different time window (1965–1995) than Golik et al.'s 1958–1992 below, so the two should not be treated as cross-confirming each other [R24].
- **Ongoing mitigation:** ~180,000 m³/year of sand is moved mechanically from south of the port breakwater to beaches north of it, an active, recurring programme [R6][R8]. Sand sourced near the Ashdod Port breakwater has also been used to nourish **Bat Yam's beaches** (further north/downdrift), part of a cycle the municipality describes as running for roughly 40 years, most recently under a "Rock Beach Sand Nourishment Project" begun 2021, run by the Government Company for Protection of Mediterranean Sea Cliffs under Ministry of Environmental Protection oversight [R17].
- **Detached breakwaters** proposed for Lido/Mey-Ami beaches (attorney Tzachi Abu's 2015 proposal, modeled on Ashkelon and Tel Aviv) were never built [R19][R23].
- **Tetrapod public-art project (2018):** ~40+ leftover 3 m, >12-ton concrete tetrapods from marina construction two decades earlier were repainted by 9 selected artists and placed at beach entrances as art installations — a cosmetic reuse, not a coastal-protection intervention [R19][R20].

## Why it worked / why it failed

The **port's sand-trapping effect "worked" as unintended coastal engineering**: it widened beaches south of the breakwater by several dozen to over 100 m while starving beaches to the north, a textbook updrift-accretion/downdrift-erosion pattern consistent across three independent sources spanning 1996–2025 [R6][R23][R24]. It is being partially offset, not solved, by continuous artificial sand bypass (~180,000 m³/yr recurring, plus a cumulative ~1.46 million m³ moved since 2000) [R6][R8][R24].

The **floating breakwater failed politically, before any technical failure could occur.** Contributing factors, per the sources: (1) an organized, vocal surfing community (surfers, a city-council member, and an association chair) framed it as an existential threat to surfing and swimmer safety, citing rip-current risk from fixed breakwaters elsewhere (pointing to a 2021 statistic — surfers reported twice as many bathing-season drownings near Tel Aviv's breakwater as on all southern beaches combined) [R10][R11]; (2) an opposition councilmember (Sharon Marck) turned the ≈6 million NIS cost and the lack of Israeli precedent into a public-accountability issue [R11][R12]; (3) the mayor chose to avoid a threatened protest at city hall rather than defend the project, pulling it from the agenda outright [R14]; (4) even on pure engineering grounds, an earlier fixed/floating version had reportedly failed testing for Mediterranean winter sea states [R12].

## Unexpected results

- Leftover construction tetrapods — normally an eyesore/disposal problem — became a celebrated public-art trail instead of being buried or dumped, a low-cost reuse nobody had planned for when the marina was built [R19][R20].
- A city 40 km south of Tel Aviv (Bat Yam) has depended on **Ashdod Port's own trapped sand** to renourish its beaches for roughly four decades, i.e. the "problem" sand accumulating at Ashdod's port has become a supply source for erosion relief elsewhere on the coast [R17].
- The same surfer-mobilization pattern that killed the Tel Aviv geotube-breakwater proposal in 2019 (per Lior's notes) repeated itself in Ashdod in 2021–2023, suggesting a now-recurring Israeli-coastal-politics dynamic: organized surfers can and do block local-government coastal structures they see as threatening surf/safety, largely independent of the structure's actual engineering merit [R1][R10][R11].

## What could have been done better (authors'/experts' view vs surfers'/public view)

- **Officials'/engineers' view:** Deputy Mayor Katzenelson and Tourism Company CEO Orit Almozlino Raiz framed the item as merely a feasibility study, not construction, and said the project would go through Ministry of Environmental Protection approval and community engagement with surfers before any building — implying the process, not the concept, needed more patience and a clearer distinction between "study" and "build" in public communication [R10][R11].
- **Surfers'/public view:** Surfers argued decision-makers lacked maritime expertise, that Israeli sea conditions differ from the European conditions the proposed equipment was designed for, that a fixed/seasonal breakwater would create dangerous currents even if it "stops waves," and that Ashdod's existing natural surf conditions did not justify the risk or cost at all — i.e., they wanted a professional, transparent needs-assessment and public consultation before any structure was proposed, not after [R11].
- **Opposition-councilmember view (Sharon Marck):** questioned whether the underlying problem justified any expenditure, criticized the absence of demonstrated Israeli technical expertise for this exact structure type, and criticized the lack of public consultation prior to bringing it to a vote [R11].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

- **Tide range:** essentially the same micro-tidal regime as the rest of Israel's Mediterranean coast — nearby surf-forecast data for Ashdod's own breaks show only ~0.32–0.36 m between high and low water [R15][R16], consistent with Lior's framing of Israel's small tidal range as a structural-stability advantage for any fixed reef/structure (still needing an authoritative Mediterranean-wide tidal reference per the master list's flagged gap).
- **Wave climate:** same regional swell pattern (west/WNW-dominant swell, best with offshore SE–ESE winds) as documented for Ashdod's breaks, broadly consistent with the Haifa/Shikmona buoy data already in Lior's files [R15][R16].
- **What transfers to Haifa:** (1) the **updrift-accretion/downdrift-erosion pattern** documented at Ashdod (4.5 million m³ trapped south, comparable deficit north, only partly offset by ~180,000 m³/yr bypass) is the same basic sediment-transport mechanism any Haifa reef/structure would need to model before siting it [R6][R23][R24]; (2) the **political-process lesson** is arguably the most transferable finding for Lior's own Haifa reef pitch: a technically modest, temporary, reversible structure (100 m, seasonal, removable) was still blocked purely through surfer mobilization plus one councilmember's public cost/process objections — meaning any Haifa proposal likely needs early, visible surfer buy-in and a transparent cost case to avoid the same fate, independent of its engineering merits [R10][R11][R14].
