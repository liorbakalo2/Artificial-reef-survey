## Motivation and who pushed for it

The marina itself was a private-public tourism/real-estate venture: construction was approved by Interior Minister Arieh Deri in June 1990 and built by a consortium (Isrotel/Yeshers 20%, Nathan Kirsh 30%, Moti Zisser 50%) chaired by Shuki Forer, aimed at creating boat berths, hotels, a shipyard, restaurants and commercial space [R3][R4]. Sand management was not the marina's purpose — the three detached breakwaters immediately north of it were added specifically to contain the erosion the marina itself was expected to cause downdrift [R2].

Four decades later, the push to do something further about the resulting beach loss and cliff erosion has come from two very different directions that are now in open conflict:
- The **City of Herzliya / national coastal-protection apparatus**, driven by the physical danger of collapsing chalk (kurkar) cliffs along the coast — this is what produced the 2020/2022 seven-breakwater-plus-nourishment plan [R3][R12][R13].
- **Local surfers, swimmers, environmental NGOs (Zalul, Adam Teva V'Din) and some council members**, who oppose new *emergent* breakwaters and instead push for large-scale sand nourishment as the primary or sole tool [R1][R5][R6].

## Design as planned

- **Original marina (c.1990–1995):** a conventional rock-armored marina with a ≈500 m outer breakwater, built without an accompanying sand bypass or backpassing system and without pre-filling the anticipated erosion downdrift with sand [R2].
- **The 3 detached breakwaters north of the marina:** added to "prevent the anticipated erosion downdrift of the marina, with natural sand accretion in their lee" — i.e., designed to trap sand and hold beach width immediately north of the marina, but explicitly *not* pre-filled with sand at construction [R2].
- **Baird Associates, 1998:** a formal shore-protection/wave-agitation/sedimentation design study for the marina area, referenced by Harris (2007) as the source model whose predictions later diverged from reality [R2].
- **Harris (2007), unbuilt:** proposed two 8–10 m-wide, porous, submerged Reef Ball artificial-reef breakwaters at the north end of the beach (near Apollonia), crest ≤0.5 m below low-tide level, explicitly designed to let sand continue moving alongshore (avoiding tombolo formation) while still creating stabilizing salients, plus beach nourishment to pre-fill the expected salients [R2].
- **2020/2022 plan (name/plan-number pending, see Blocked sources [R14]):** 6 detached breakwaters plus 1 shore-connected groyne ("dorban") along the Akdiya/Sharon/Sidna Ali beaches, paired with artificial sand nourishment, targeting a maintained beach width of 60–130 m, plus a promenade and sports facilities; estimated 1–2 years to build [R13].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

The marina and its 500 m outer breakwater, plus the 3 detached breakwaters north of it, were built and are the structures in place today; Baird's 1998 remedial design study and Harris's 2007 submerged-reef proposal were consultancy studies, not construction — **the Reef Ball/submerged-breakwater alternative was never built** [R2]. The critical gap between plan and execution, per Harris's 2007 review of the existing structures: the three detached breakwaters "were not pre-filled with sand after construction" [R2], and Baird's 1998 model "predicted soil accumulation" in their lee but "under-predicted the degree of erosion" that actually occurred further north — ground evidence showed the structures simply "pushed the area of erosion northward" rather than stopping it [search-engine synthesis of R9, corroborated qualitatively by R2's own critique of the same design]. As of 2024, the newer 7-breakwater/nourishment plan that was approved in principle in 2020–2022 has itself not been built: the municipality suspended it for its own re-examination [R11].

## Outcome (surfing quality / coastal protection / ecology / longevity / damage / removal, with measurements)

- **Erosion extent:** the State Comptroller's 2020 report found the marina and its three detached breakwaters caused the coastline north of them to retreat by up to **≈2 km** [R7].
- **Academic measurements (Klein & Zviely 2001, Applied Geography — full text not directly accessible, cited via search-engine synthesis of the abstract/citation, see Blocked sources):** a **12–15 m** advance of the waterline toward the cliffs in erosion-prone areas up to **2,500 m** north of the marina, and a **35–45%** decrease in sandy-shore area, measured 1990–1997; the zone of influence extended **≈2,000 m** north (downdrift) of the detached breakwaters and **≈1,500 m** south (updrift) of the main marina breakwater; modeled sand accumulation behind the breakwaters was exceeded by real-world accretion by roughly **30,000 m²** [R9].
- **Trapped sand:** an estimated **300,000 m³** of sand from the littoral system had accumulated in the lee of the 3 breakwaters as of 2007 [R2]; Zalul separately estimated **≈200,000 m³** of sand sitting in the marina sediment/between the Akdiya breakwaters as of 2020, with **30,000–50,000 m³/yr** needed for ongoing maintenance dredging [R1].
- **Cliff damage:** erosion of the beach at the base led to undermining and collapse of the coastal chalk cliffs north of the marina; Apollonia and Sidna Ali beaches "disappeared" according to 2012 reporting [R5]; cliff collapses at Herzliya beaches (including Sidna Ali) continued to be reported through the mid-2020s, with at least two collapses reported within one month in recent coverage [R10].
- **Mitigation in practice:** ≈10,000–15,000 m³/yr of sand has been scraped from the breakwater lee and trucked to the northern beaches each spring ("sand backpassing"), a practice already in place by 2007 and recommended by Harris to be expanded [R2].
- **Beach protection standard proposed but not yet achieved:** Zalul's 2020 sand-nourishment-pilot alternative set a target of maintaining a sandy-beach width of at least 30 m for 95% of the bathing season as "success" [R1] — implying that width is *not* currently reliably achieved along the affected beaches.
- **No removal:** none of the original marina, breakwater, or the 3 detached breakwaters have been removed or modified; Harris (2007) explicitly considered and rejected breakwater removal/modification as impractical, since removing them would trigger the severe erosion they were built to prevent [R2].

## Why it worked / why it failed

The 3 detached breakwaters "worked" narrowly: the beaches directly updrift (south of the marina) and directly in the lee of the breakwaters are the widest along this stretch of coast, because the marina and breakwaters do trap sand there [R2]. They failed as a downdrift-erosion fix because (a) they were built without pre-filling the sand volume needed to satisfy their own trapping capacity, so they simply relocated the erosion problem further north instead of preventing it, and (b) as emergent (surface-piercing) structures they fully blocked, rather than merely slowed, longshore sand transport, forming tombolos that cut off sand supply to the beaches beyond them — the textbook downdrift-erosion mechanism of detached breakwaters without accompanying nourishment [R2][R9].

## Unexpected results

- The 1998 Baird design model *underestimated* the severity of downdrift erosion it was meant to predict and manage [R2][R9].
- Actual sand accretion behind the breakwaters exceeded the model's prediction by roughly 30,000 m² [R9].
- What began as a private marina/tourism project has, decades later, become entangled with a life-safety issue (collapsing cliffs) and a separate, underfunded State cliff-protection program: the State Comptroller found that of a 360 million NIS budget earmarked for coastal-cliff protection, only ≈100 million NIS had been used, and in the 2013–2018 window just ≈26 million NIS (8.6% of the plan) was actually spent, with land-based ("dry side") protections — the local authorities' own responsibility — still not implemented in most cliff sections including Herzliya as of the report [R8].
- The Comptroller went further and flagged a structural conflict of interest: new marina construction (which by the Comptroller's own analysis "consumes about one kilometer of beach" per installation, per NGO commentary in the same reporting) is continuing to be approved (6 more marinas planned Tel Aviv–Nahariya by 2040, 3,200–3,500 new berths) even as cliff-collapse risk from existing marinas like Herzliya's remains unresolved — the Comptroller recommended a joint economic/environmental review of whether new marina construction and cliff protection can proceed simultaneously at all [R7].
- The municipality's own 2022-approved fix (7 more breakwaters) has itself stalled: by August 2024 Herzliya had pulled back from the national partnership track used successfully by Netanya and Ashdod, setting up its own advisory committee instead, specifically over fear the new breakwaters would themselves further damage the beaches and marine recreation they were meant to save [R11].

## What could have been done better (authors'/experts' view vs surfers'/public view)

**Experts' view (Harris, 2007 engineering report, and the Baird 1998 study by implication):**
1. The three original breakwaters should have been pre-filled with sand (the ≈300,000 m³ they were expected to trap) at time of construction, to prevent starving the beaches to their north [R2].
2. New structures at the north end should be *submerged*, wide-crested (8–10 m), porous artificial-reef units (e.g. Reef Balls) rather than emergent breakwaters — submerged structures let smaller waves and sand continue over/through them, avoiding tombolo formation, while an 8–10 m width with crest ≤0.5 m below low tide is still enough to attenuate storm waves and stabilize the beach [R2].
3. A permanent beach-profile survey benchmark network (Florida-style, 300 m spacing) should be established; existing surveys were sparse and inconsistent (1980, 1995, 1998, 2000, incomplete coverage each time) [R2].
4. Sand backpassing volume/frequency should be increased [R2].
5. The Apollonia archaeological bluff's erosion is dominated by rainfall/wind attack on the over-steepened upper slope, not by wave action at its base, and needs separate slope-stabilization expertise, not just a beach-erosion fix [R2].

**Public/surfer/environmental view (Zalul, Adam Teva V'Din, resident/surfer campaigners, 2012–2024):**
1. Reject new emergent breakwaters outright; run a monitored, multi-year sand-nourishment pilot instead, funded from marina revenue, municipal beach-development budget and beachfront property-tax surcharges, using sand already trapped in the marina/between the Akdiya breakwaters (~200,000 m³) plus annual maintenance dredging (30,000–50,000 m³/yr) [R1].
2. Concerns specific to breakwaters, not addressed by the "expert" engineering fix: stagnant water and algae blooms in the lee of structures, accumulation of decomposing plastic waste, increased drowning risk from rip currents/vortices near structures, habitat for invasive/nuisance species (e.g. stinging jellyfish), harm to surfing, sailing and diving, and permanent loss of open-sea sightlines/landscape value [R1][R13].
3. Some campaigners (e.g. Anbel Rashef) point to a domestic precedent they consider a failure — sand disappearing after similar concrete promenade/breakwater work at Dado Beach, Haifa — as the reason to distrust the breakwater approach generally [R5].
4. A parallel, adjacent fight (the Apollonia housing development on the cliff-top nature reserve immediately north) is intertwined in local politics with the beach-erosion fight, with residents (e.g. Alon Carmeli) arguing no adequate environmental-impact survey was done for that separate plan [R6].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

- **Tide range:** astronomical tide range at Herzliya is only ≈20–40 cm, with storm surge/wave setup adding more — this is representative of the whole Israeli Mediterranean coast, Haifa included; it is a genuinely micro-tidal environment, so submerged-breakwater crest elevation relative to low tide is a design detail that transfers directly to Haifa [R2].
- **Wave climate:** predominant waves from NW/W/SW with a large western fetch; offshore storm significant wave heights up to ≈9 m have been recorded, with ≈5 m typical during storms; nearshore waves are depth-limited [R2]. This wave climate and directionality is shared with the Haifa coast further north on the same open Mediterranean shoreline.
- **Sediment transport direction:** dominant longshore sand transport along the whole Israeli coast — Herzliya included — runs south to north, with sand ultimately sourced in part from as far away as the Nile Delta [R2]. This is the same regional transport regime that governs the Haifa Bay sediment budget referenced elsewhere in this project's research.
- **Direct transferability:** Herzliya is the clearest, best-documented Israeli case of the generic mechanism any protruding structure (marina, breakwater, artificial reef) on this coast must reckon with — trapping sand on its updrift (south) side while starving its downdrift (north) side unless it is either submerged/porous or pre-filled with sand at construction. Harris's 2007 recommendation — wide (8–10 m), porous, submerged artificial-reef units with crest near/below low-tide level, deployed with pre-fill nourishment — is architecturally the same category of solution (a submerged/near-surface artificial reef) proposed for a Haifa surf-reef project, and Herzliya is a rare case with an actual, itemized engineering cost estimate (≈US$500,000 for two 100 m × 10 m submerged reef-ball breakwaters, 2007 dollars) [R2] for that class of structure on this same coastline.
- **Governance lesson:** the State Comptroller's finding that marina-building keeps being approved city-by-city without a coastwide review of its cumulative sand-starvation effect on cliffs [R7][R8] is directly relevant to any Haifa-area proposal — it argues for assessing regional sediment-budget impact before construction, not after erosion appears.
