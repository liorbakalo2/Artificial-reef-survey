## Motivation and who pushed for it

Haifa Bay is Israel's only natural harbor on the Mediterranean, and the British Mandate government built Haifa Port's main breakwater (1929–1933) purely to shelter shipping in the bay's south-western corner — not with any sediment-management goal [R11][R2]. No contemporary source found in this pass shows the British designers anticipating or planning for the breakwater's effect on the bay's sand supply.

A century later, the pressure driving the current mitigation works is the opposite problem: Kiryat Haim and Kiryat Yam residents and the local municipality have been living with a shrinking beach since at least 2011, when severe sand loss was first identified next to the Kiryat Haim oil tank farm [R3]. Israel Ports Company has led the current 7-breakwater plan since 2018 "in full cooperation with Haifa Municipality" [second WebSearch pass, discovery_israel.md — not independently re-fetched this session]. The Haifa District Planning Committee, chaired by Ruth Shwartz, approved depositing the plan in December 2024, calling it "an important plan for the public and Haifa/Krayot residents particularly" [R14]. Two environmental NGOs — Zalul and Adam Teva V'Din — pushed back with formal appeals, which the National Planning Council's appeals sub-committee rejected in full around 8 March 2026 [R4]. The regional committee itself framed the trade-off starkly, calling the environmental cost "a necessary evil to achieve beach rehabilitation" [R8].

## Design as planned

The 1929–1933 breakwater was engineered as a single-purpose shipping shelter: a rubble-mound wall with no sand-bypassing mechanism designed in from the start. There is no evidence in the sources fetched that its designers modeled its effect on the Nile littoral cell, which was not itself characterized scientifically until decades later (Perlin and Kit's directional wave-based LST estimates only began in 1994; Zviely's Holocene-scale reconstruction was published in 2006–2007) [R1].

The modern Kiryat Haim plan, by contrast, is explicitly designed around the sediment-budget problem: 7 detached breakwaters spaced along ~1,700 m of coast are meant to trap wave energy and let sand accumulate landward of them, paired with ongoing artificial sand nourishment — dredged sand trucked or pumped in and redistributed periodically [R4][R8]. Israel Ports Company VP Michal Tuchler said the "engineering design integrates environmental and coastal research" and that execution would be "fully coordinated" with the municipality [R14]. The NGOs argued the design itself is outdated: Adam Teva V'Din warned that "without comprehensive planning... we'll end up with a continuous line of breakwaters along the entire Israeli coastline," and both NGOs pushed for sand-filled geotubes or artificial reefs instead of rock breakwaters, citing sea-view blockage, surfer/swimmer hazard, and pollution trapped behind rock walls [R6]. Israel Ports Company's own reply was that geotubes had already been "rejected by the Environmental Protection Ministry" [R6].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

What got built at Haifa Port deviated sharply from its narrow shipping-shelter brief. The main breakwater immediately began trapping migrating sand: a sandbar had already reached the breakwater head by 1938 (5 years after completion) and, by the late 1950s, ran the entire length of the structure and bypassed its eastern head into the port's main entrance [R1]. The port authority dredged 1.3 million m³ from that sandbar in the early 1960s for a new quay, extended the main breakwater by 600 m at the end of the 1970s to protect a new eastern quay, and then dredged a further ~2.5 million m³ in 2005–2007 for the new "Carmel Port phase A quay" as part of the Bay Port/Carmel container terminal expansion (main breakwater extended a further ~880 m, secondary breakwater built to ~2.1 km total) [R1][R2][R7]. In effect, a structure designed only to protect ships against waves became — unintentionally — the single largest sand trap on the entire Israeli coast, permanently removing an average of 66,000 m³/yr of Nile-derived sand from the littoral system for 75+ years [R1][R2].

The Kiryat Haim 7-breakwater plan has not yet been built as of this research date (24 Sep 2026): it was still moving through Israel's statutory approval stages (District Committee deposit Dec 2024, appeals rejected ~Mar 2026), so no "as-built vs. as-designed" comparison is possible yet [R4][R8][R14].

## Outcome

**Sediment budget / erosion, quantified (Zviely et al. 2007, 2009, peer-reviewed Marine Geology / Geo-Marine Letters):**
- Before human intervention (1799–1928), Haifa Bay's sandy coastal sectors were expanding by ~50–150 m over the period (0.4–1.2 m/yr average) and accumulating ~70,000 m³/yr of sand bay-wide [R2].
- After the main breakwater's completion (1928–2006), the bay's coast went into a "steady state" with seasonal fluctuations under ±20 m, and the bay overall recorded a slight net erosion of ~7,000 m³/yr [R2].
- Sector-by-sector for 1994–2006 (the most recent period the 200-year study measured): Qerayot coast eroding ~27,800 m³/yr; Rafael coast eroding ~20,657 m³/yr; Akko coast eroding ~994 m³/yr [R2].
- The Akko sector — 3 km from the bay's northern end, far from the port — nonetheless retreated ~15 m over the last 50 years (as of the 2006 survey), which the authors attribute to human modification of the Na'aman River's natural flow and to coastal structures built in that period, not to the port directly [R2].
- Severe, locally acute erosion at Kiryat Haim (adjacent to the oil tank-farm industrial zone) was first flagged in 2011 [R3]; by 2019 researcher Naama Sharid (University of Haifa, supervised by Dr. Michael Lazar) warned that "if sand feeding stops, within months there will be no beach" at that site [R3].
- Beach depth at the affected Kiryat Haim/Krayot beaches reportedly shrank from ≥25 m in 2004 to between 20 m and 0 m by the time of the 2025 Times of Israel report [R6].

**Mitigation to date:** Israel Ports Company has performed annual sand nourishment at Kiryat Haim/Kiryat Yam since around 2016 — ~70,000 m³ added, ~90,000 m³ more planned as of a 2019 Zalul report — but the District Committee's own 2024 decision states plainly that "sand feeding, which Israel Ports Company performs annually, is not sufficient to correct the situation" [R4][R15].

## Why it worked / why it failed

The 1929–1933 breakwater unambiguously "worked" for its actual design goal: it has sheltered Haifa Port for nearly a century and was judged robust enough to be extended twice (late 1970s, 2000s–2010s) rather than replaced [R1][R2]. As a piece of coastal-sediment engineering, however, it failed by omission — it was built with zero provision for sand bypassing, at a location that happened to sit squarely across the Nile littoral cell's terminus, the point where 82,000–90,000 m³/yr of longshore-transported sand had been reaching the bay every year for roughly 8,000 years [R1]. Because the failure mode was "no bypass system," not "structural collapse," it produced a slow, cumulative, and initially invisible problem: the trap took decades to become an acute local crisis at Kiryat Haim, only surfacing publicly around 2011 [R3].

The Kiryat Haim breakwater/nourishment plan is a direct, explicit attempt to correct exactly this failure — by trapping sand deliberately, at a chosen downdrift location, instead of by accident far upstream. Whether it will "work" cannot yet be assessed since it has not been built, but the District Committee itself concedes the pre-existing sand-feeding approach (without the breakwaters) has already failed to keep pace [R4][R8].

## Unexpected results

- **A sediment trap by accident, not design.** No source found in this research shows the British 1929–1933 engineers anticipating the breakwater's role as the Nile littoral cell's dead-end sand trap — the effect was only understood decades later through geological drill-core and directional-wave research (Zviely, Kit, Klein et al., 1999–2009) [R1][R2].
- **Erosion at Akko, far from the port.** The Akko sector's 15 m/50-yr retreat is attributed not to the port breakwater at all but to unrelated Na'aman River modification and local coastal construction — a reminder that Haifa Bay's sediment problems are not a single-cause story [R2].
- **The trapped sand became a construction resource.** Rather than being wasted, the 1.3 million m³ dredged in the 1960s and 2.5 million m³ dredged in 2005–2007 were reused to build new port quays — turning the "problem" sand into fill material for the very port that trapped it [R2].
- **A slow-motion regulatory fight over the fix.** The proposed fix for erosion the port itself caused — more breakwaters — is now opposed by environmental NGOs precisely because it repeats the same structural logic (rock walls trapping sand and blocking views) that caused the original problem, rather than adopting the softer alternatives (geotubes, reefs) the NGOs prefer [R6].

## What could have been done better

**Experts' view (Zviely et al., peer-reviewed):** The 2007/2009 papers do not prescribe a fix directly (they are geological/sedimentological studies, not engineering-design papers), but they establish the diagnostic groundwork any better design would need — the bay's ~5 m closure depth, the 66,000 m³/yr trap rate, and the fact that only 8,000–10,000 m³/yr was bypassing the breakwater head naturally — implying that an artificial sand-bypassing system sized to move on the order of 60,000+ m³/yr around the breakwater, rather than one-off dredge-and-dump nourishment, would be the structurally consistent fix [R1][R2].

**NGO/public view:** Zalul and Adam Teva V'Din argue the Kiryat Haim plan repeats a discredited approach and call instead for softer engineering — sand-filled geotubes or artificial reefs — that would not "obstruct views of the sea, endanger surfers and bathers" or "trap polluted water and waste" the way a rock breakwater line does, and warn that approving this one project without a comprehensive Israel-wide coastal plan risks "a continuous line of breakwaters along the entire Israeli coastline" [R6]. Israel Ports Company's counter-view is that geotubes were already formally rejected by the Ministry of Environmental Protection for this use, leaving detached breakwaters as the approved option [R6].

**Officials' view:** Haifa District Planner Ronen Segal frames the 7-breakwater project as the tool that "will rehabilitate the shoreline, expand bathing beaches, prevent further damage, and enable future development," i.e. as the fix already chosen rather than one option among several [R14].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

**Tide range at the site.** The Mediterranean off Israel is strongly microtidal. General Mediterranean-wide figures put the tidal range at roughly half a metre or less, with narrow intertidal zones [WebSearch aggregate, no single fetched primary source — treat as background, not a numbered claim]. A 2024 peer-reviewed study using a mooring ~50 km offshore Haifa (DeepLev, 33°03.67′N 34°29.296′E) measured the dominant semidiurnal M2 tidal-constituent amplitude at ~11.3 cm on average across depths and seasons, with diurnal constituents (K1, O1) reaching up to ~10 cm in winter at depth and considerably less in summer [R16]. Separately, general references for the Israeli coast (Haifa/Ashdod) put the practical tidal range at roughly 0.4 m at spring tides down to ~0.15 m at neap tides, i.e. within the brief's ~0.3–0.5 m range [WebSearch aggregate, accessed 2026-09-24, no single fetchable primary URL surfaced this pass]. This is dramatically smaller than the multi-metre tidal ranges of many ocean surf-reef and coastal-protection sites elsewhere in the world's reef case studies, which matters directly for a fixed Haifa reef: a structure sized to a fixed target depth band (as in Lior's own design sketch, using "No Breaking" 1.45–1.7 m / "Wave Breaking Depth" 1.3–1.5 m bands) stays within its intended performance window across the whole tidal cycle in Haifa, whereas the same fixed structure at a macro-tidal site would alternately be far too deep (buried under tide) or far too shallow (exposed/breaking wrong) for large parts of each day [Lior's notes, lior_suggestions_and_swell_heights.md and hebrew_surf_route_summary.md — his own framing, labelled as his opinion].

**Wave climate.** Haifa Bay's breaker-zone significant wave height during storms (the 12-hour/year exceedance threshold used to derive the bay's ~5 m closure depth) is 3.0–3.3 m, measured by CAMERI's directional wave buoys on behalf of the Israel Ports Authorities over 15+ years of data collection starting in 1994 [R2]. The dominant net longshore-transport-driving waves approach from the southwest, and the resulting net sand transport for the whole Israeli coast is northward, terminating at Haifa Bay [R1]. This is consistent with, but independently sourced from, Lior's own Shikmona-buoy dataset citing a narrower, calmer "surfable window" (SWH 0.45–0.6 m, swell direction 284–300°/WNW) — the CAMERI storm data describes the high end of the wave climate a Haifa structure would have to survive, while Lior's buoy readings describe the low end it would need to shape into a rideable wave [Lior's notes, lior_suggestions_and_swell_heights.md].

**What transfers.** The Haifa Bay case is the clearest possible local proof that a fixed structure placed across a sand-transport pathway will trap sediment and starve the downdrift coast — exactly the engineering risk any fixed reef proposed off Haifa (Lior's own concept) must account for, since Haifa Bay is where the entire 650-km Nile littoral cell's sand supply terminates [R1]. It also demonstrates, in the CAMERI/IOLR/Geological Survey of Israel research tradition (Nir 1980; Hall 1976; Bakler 1975 — all cited within R1/R2), that this exact stretch of coast already has 90+ years of institutional data and modeling capacity (a 50×25 m physical wave-basin at CAMERI on the Technion campus, founded 1976 by Technion + Israel Ports Company) that a future Haifa reef project could draw on directly rather than starting from scratch [R13].
