## Motivation and who pushed for it

Netanya sits on ~40 m kurkar (soft calcareous-sandstone) cliffs along almost its entire coastline except the southern Poleg/Ir Yamim area, which is naturally low-lying [R21]. The cliffs have suffered chronic, sometimes fatal collapses; Israel's cliff belt nationally has recorded episodes at a rate of roughly one life-threatening collapse every two months according to the state company's own estimate [R12], and the national kurkar coastline (45 km of soft cliff, 22 km of it earmarked for reinforcement because public beaches sit at its foot) has caused at least two known deaths over two decades [R3].

Mayor Miriam Feirberg-Ikar is credited by multiple outlets as the person who began and led the campaign for state-funded protection, described variously as starting in 2009 [R14] or as "over 15 years" of struggle against "numerous government and professional bodies" [R18]. The push reportedly escalated into a joint petition to Israel's Supreme Court, which ordered the establishment of a dedicated government company with its own budget for this purpose [R14] — the "Government Company for Protection of Mediterranean Sea Cliffs" that ultimately ran the project. On the technical side, then-city-engineer Paul Weitel developed the initial marine-model-based plans, and his successor Arez Tal continued pushing them through, calling cliff protection "an environmental matter of supreme importance" [R18]. Environment Minister Idit Silman championed and publicly announced later funding stages, including the phase-2 groundbreaking in August 2024 and project handover in June 2026 [R4][R15][R16].

## Design as planned

The plan approved by Netanya's licensing committee in 2020 called for 12 detached rubble-mound breakwaters — a mix of emergent and submerged structures — built in two phases of 6 each along ~3.5 km of coastline, from Sironit Beach south to Argaman/Lagoon Beach, each breakwater roughly 100–200 m long and sited 150–200 m offshore in 4–7 m of water, intended to knock down incoming wave energy before it reaches the cliff toe [R9][R14][R18]. Behind the breakwaters, sand pumped from the sea would be used to widen the beach and physically push the waterline away from the cliff base [R2][R7]. The original 2020-era estimate was for the two phases to be completed in "approximately three years" for a combined cost then estimated around 170 million NIS [R18].

Construction used heavy marine plant — Hitachi EX1200 (26 m reach) and Kobelco 500SK (12 m reach, 50-t capacity) excavators — with DGPS-guided stone placement (BeamworX/eTrac systems) displayed to the operator for precision underwater placement, and articulated trucks moving ~12 m³ of quarried stone per load from source to shore [R9].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Phase 1 (6 breakwaters, northern section) began construction in April 2021 and was substantially finished by early September 2023, adding ~200,000 m³ of sand and roughly 30 dunams (~3 ha) of new beach [R4][R5][R13]. Phase 2 (the remaining 6 breakwaters, southern section from Lagoon Beach to Island Hotel) did not break ground until 6 August 2024 — a roughly year-long gap after phase 1's completion — and was not finished until June 2026 [R4][R14][R16]. In total the project therefore ran roughly six years from first construction to handover [R14], about double the "approximately three years" originally projected for both phases combined at the 2020 approval stage [R18], reflecting the funding delays described below.

Reported quantities also shifted or diverge across the project's life: sand volume for phase 2 is given as 250,000 m³ by most outlets [R4][R5][R13][R15][R23] but as 480,000 m³ in one Netanya-local report on the phase-2 approval itself [R11] — this dossier could not resolve which figure is the executed volume. Total project cost converges on ≈190 million NIS in the most recent (completion-stage) reporting [R14][R16], which sits within, rather than matching, the wider ≈100–200 million-NIS range quoted at earlier project stages [R4][R5][R13][R15][R18].

## Outcome (surfing quality / coastal protection / ecology / longevity / damage / removal, with measurements)

**Coastal protection:** The state company's own CEO, Ilan Levi, stated in April 2023 — after a fresh cliff collapse further south — that collapses had already been "successfully prevented in the northern section of Netanya where breakwaters were already installed," implicitly contrasting it with the still-unprotected southern stretch that kept collapsing [R3]. The July 2024 State Comptroller report found that, nationally, 8 of Israel's 13 km of cliff coastline still had no maritime protection at all and 14 of 27 land-side sections had no terrestrial protection despite being flagged as necessary — Netanya itself is named among the highest-risk stretches even after its marine project, both as part of the "Herzliya–Netanya" and "Netanya–Beit Yanai" risk zones [R12].

**Beach width:** ~30 dunams (~3 ha) of new beach were added in phase 1 alone, with a further "30+ dunams" targeted in phase 2, along the full 3.5 km protected strip [R4][R5].

**Surfing:** Netanya's best-known surf beach, Sironit/Kontiki (host of the ISA's "SEAT Pro Netanya" World Surf League Qualifying Series events), already sat behind an older, pre-existing breakwater system predating this specific 2021–2026 project [R7 target-list seed, R "surfer today" search snippet]. Surf-forecast.com classifies the general "Netanya (Poleg)" break as an exposed sandy beach break, but distinguishes nearby "Ambatia" and "Sironit" as "beach and reef" breaks — i.e. rock/breakwater-influenced — noting Sironit/Kontiki can produce A-frame barrels with the right south-easterly wind [R13, R websearch]. This dossier found no source directly measuring how the new 2021–2026 breakwater phases changed wave quality at Sironit/Kontiki specifically; the surf-forecast.com visitor-review page for Netanya had only two anonymous numeric ratings and no written reviews [R13].

**Ecology / other concerns:** Multiple outlets note "experts and environmental organizations fear the consequences these structures will have on adjacent beaches" [R15], in the general sense of interrupted longshore sand transport starving beaches further along the coast, but this dossier found no quantified study of that effect specific to this project. Aesthetically, a Haaretz "Shabbat trip" feature (2026) is explicitly headlined "Netanya's beach is changing — not everyone is satisfied," describing the new breakwaters as looking like "tiny islands someone scattered ... so we feel like in Dubai," obstructing the horizon and "creating another danger," while conceding that "walking on the beach is delightful" [R17].

## Why it worked / why it failed

**Where it appears to have worked:** the CEO's April 2023 statement crediting the completed northern (phase-1) breakwaters with halting collapses in that specific stretch is the clearest outcome claim found [R3].

**Why it took so long / nearly stalled:** the project's phase-2 funding was caught for years in a standoff between the Ministry of Environmental Protection and the Ministry of Finance. A Times of Israel investigation reports the first phase drew on 360 million NIS (~$115M) sourced via the Israel Land Authority, of which 330 million NIS had already been spent with the pot "exhausting in June" (year not specified in the excerpt obtained), while the Finance Ministry was reportedly willing to approve only 150 million NIS against a 360-million-NIS request for the next national tranche (covering Netanya's continuation plus Herzliya, Ashkelon, Bat Yam and Emek Hefer) [R2]. At a 14 January 2025 Knesset Interior & Environment Committee hearing, the committee chair, Jacob Asher, summarized the structural problem: "We need unified management. This isn't organized correctly between two separate government offices" — referring to the marine (Environmental Protection Ministry) and terrestrial (Interior Ministry) protection tracks being funded and managed separately [R20]. The Comptroller's July 2024 report reached a similar structural conclusion nationally [R12].

## Unexpected results

- **Aesthetic backlash:** the string of visible rock breakwaters drew a "not everyone is satisfied" reception, with residents/journalists likening the new seascape to scattered artificial islands blocking the horizon view from the promenade [R17].
- **Roughly double the planned timeline:** originally projected at ~3 years for both phases combined in 2020, the project actually took ~6 years to complete (2020/21 → June 2026), driven largely by the funding gap between phases 1 and 2 [R14][R18].
- **A parallel, distinct controversy on the same stretch of coast:** immediately south of the cliff-protection project, Poleg Beach (part of the same municipal coastal zone) was the site of a 2022 dispute when Netanya used a planning exemption to build temporary Maccabiah Games beach-sports facilities on protected dune habitat. The Society for the Protection of Nature in Israel (SPNI) and residents petitioned Israel's High Court, which declined to intervene; ecologist Prof. Avigdor Abelson (Tel Aviv University) argued "you can't change an ecosystem and claim it's recovered after covering it with sand," while activist Ofir Yuval alleged the city "chose this spot to establish facts on the ground ... to implement hotel plans" [R13]. This is a separate project from the cliff breakwaters themselves, but shows the same coastal zone facing parallel development-vs-conservation fights.

## What could have been done better (authors'/experts' view vs surfers'/public view)

**Experts' / state view:** the State Comptroller (July 2024) and the January 2025 Knesset committee both point to the same structural fix — unify marine and terrestrial cliff-protection funding and management under one authority instead of splitting it across the Environmental Protection and Interior ministries, which the Comptroller found had left 8 of 13 km of Israel's cliff coast with zero maritime protection and most terrestrial sections unbuilt even after the Netanya project's marine phase was done [R12][R20].

**Public / aesthetic view:** the Haaretz "not everyone is satisfied" feature is the clearest public-reaction source found; its complaint is specifically about the visual/horizon impact of a dozen offshore rock islands rather than about engineering failure [R17].

**Surfers' view — specific to this project:** not found. This dossier located a well-documented 2019 surfer protest (Adi Gluska, Eyal Szmulewitz and others) against a *different* proposed geotube breakwater off Tel Aviv's last open beach, logged separately in this project's `israel-geotubes-other-sites` dossier target — but found no equivalent surfer commentary specifically about the Netanya cliff-protection breakwaters built 2021–2026.

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Netanya's kurkar cliff geology, wave climate and sediment source are the same as Haifa's: both sit on Israel's Mediterranean coastal-plain kurkar (calcarenite) formation, and the beach sand along this whole coast is ultimately Nile-derived quartz, originally eroded from Ethiopian highlands and carried to the Egyptian coast by the Nile before longshore drift moves it north past Netanya toward Haifa Bay [R "Nahal Poleg" websearch, R Poleg nature-reserve source]. Israel's Mediterranean coast is essentially microtidal (astronomically negligible tidal range compared with ocean sites); this dossier did not find an external, citable numeric figure for Israel's exact Mediterranean tidal range and marks it "not found" — Lior's project-wide wave-climate note (Shikmona Buoy data: surfable-window significant wave height 0.45–0.6 m, swell direction clustered 284–300°/WNW) remains the best sourced baseline in this project and applies to Netanya as much as Haifa [Lior's notes, 00_master_list.md `israel-wave-climate-and-tides`].

What transfers directly to a Haifa scheme: (1) the same generic engineering approach — DGPS-placed rubble-mound rock breakwaters plus pumped-sand nourishment — used here at unit figures of ≈1,400 m of breakwater and ≈450,000 m³ of sand for ≈190 million NIS, giving a rough real-world cost benchmark (~54 million NIS per protected km) transferable to costing a comparable Haifa/Carmel-coast scheme [R9][R14][R16]; (2) the demonstrated multi-year funding-fragmentation risk (marine vs. terrestrial budgets split across two ministries) that delayed Netanya's phase 2 by roughly a year and could equally stall a Haifa project [R2][R20]; and (3) Poleg Beach's open, unprotected sandy stretch immediately south of the Netanya breakwaters offers a ready-made "control" site, already flagged in Lior's own notes as the best local place to study natural sand movement against the breakwater-protected Sironit/Kontiki section to its north [Lior's notes, 00_master_list.md `poleg-beach-submerged-reefs`].

**Poleg's two submerged reefs specifically:** Lior's notes flag two unidentified submerged reefs offshore of Poleg Beach as the best local site to study sand transport, with their origin (artificial structure, natural rock outcrop, or shipwreck) unconfirmed [Lior's notes, 00_master_list.md `poleg-beach-submerged-reefs`]. This research pass did not resolve that origin — no Netanya-specific source describing these two reefs by name, date or provenance was found; the only adjacent finding was a general note that natural rock ridges near Poleg/Nahal Poleg derive from ancient Nile-borne quartz sand cemented into kurkar sandstone, i.e. the same rock-forming process that produces the whole coastal cliff, which would be consistent with (but does not prove) a natural origin for the reefs [R "Poleg nature reserve" websearch].
