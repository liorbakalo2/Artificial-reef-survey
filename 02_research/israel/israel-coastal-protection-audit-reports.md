## Motivation and who pushed for it

Cliff-collapse danger was on the Knesset's radar well before the first formal Comptroller audit: on 23 March 2005 the Knesset's State Audit Affairs Committee held a session specifically on requesting a State Comptroller opinion regarding destruction of the kurkar cliff at Ashkelon, and handed responsibility for reviewing Ashkelon municipality's proposed solutions to the Protection of the Coastal Environment Committee (VALHOF) [R2]. In October 2005 the Ministry of Environmental Protection convened its own inter-ministerial committee to examine the cliff-collapse phenomenon; its report, originally expected by end-2008, was delayed to 18 June 2009 [R2]. The proximate human trigger cited in the Knesset's own June 2009 background paper (prepared for the Interior and Environmental Protection Committee ahead of the first Comptroller audit): on 30 May 2009 a young man was killed and another person lightly injured when a kurkar cliff collapsed near Netanya, roughly a year after a similar collapse near Hadera injured six people when a section fell onto a tent built beneath the cliff [R2]. An April 2010 policy document, "Collapse of the Coastal Cliff in Israel — Ways to Address It, and the Economic, Public, and Environmental Implications" (Ministry of Environmental Protection + Jerusalem Institute for Israel Studies), set out the land/sea division-of-responsibility policy the government later adopted [R1]. The State Comptroller's first audit (2010, covering 2009 fieldwork) found "local authorities and the state are not rushing to find solutions" and that no surveyed municipality had a completed cliff-protection plan [R15]. A government decision in April 2011 then formalized funding and institutional responsibility (~NIS 500 million, of which NIS 360M for sea defenses), leading to the Cliff Protection Company's 2012–2013 incorporation [R3][R5]. Since then, the Coastal Authorities Forum (representing all coastal municipalities, operating under the Federation of Local Authorities in Israel) and individual mayors — most visibly Herzliya's Yariv Fisher after the 2024 report and the 2025–2026 collapses — have repeatedly lobbied the Prime Minister's Office, the Knesset's Interior and Environmental Protection Committee, and the Finance Ministry for centralized authority and funding [R1][R12][R13]. The Comptroller's own office conducted the 2010, 2020 and 2024 audits as part of its routine local-government audit cycle, not at any single outside party's request for the later two rounds [R1].

## Design as planned

The 2010/2011 government policy split responsibility in two: the **state** (via the Cliff Protection Company and the Israel Land Authority) plans, funds and builds **sea** protections (breakwaters, Geotubes, sand nourishment), while **municipalities** are responsible for **land** protections (cliff-top stabilization, drainage, fencing/signage), with state assistance grants to help fund the municipal side [R1][R3]. National Outline Plan (TAMA) 9/13/A — the statutory plan for the coastal cliff — divides the ~45 km belt into 39 segments and specifies, by urgency level, whether each segment needs sea protection, land protection, or both [R1]. The plan envisioned Phase A sea protections (~NIS 360M) followed by a further Phase B (~NIS 360M); NIS 60M in assistance to 9 local authorities for land-protection planning/construction (allocated 2019); and the Company reaching full utilization of its own annual operating budget [R1]. The 2024 follow-up report also documents the Company exploring "innovative technologies" for sea protection — synthetic textile sleeves with metal conductors, embedded metal structures connected to an electrical circuit, and use of seaweed (consistent with the mineral-accretion/CCell-type pilot described in this project's separate Michmoret dossier) — plus a new "cliff stability index" meant to reprioritize which segments get treated first [R1].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

By August 2023, per the Cliff Protection Company's own internal document, sea protection existed on only ~5 of the 13 km of cliff needing it, and most land-protection segments (14 of 27) had nothing built at all [R1]. Only 5 of 8 audited local authorities — Ashkelon, Bat Yam, Netanya, Tel Aviv-Yafo, and the Emek Hefer Regional Council — had built even partial land or sea protections; Hadera, Herzliya, and Gan Raveh had built none [R1]. The Company's own budget-utilization rate for its "operations" category was only 5–19% in 2016–2018 per the 2020 audit; by H1 2023 this had risen to 66.6% (NIS 23.7M of NIS 35.6M spent, out of a NIS 71.2M full-year 2023 budget), still short of full utilization per the 2024 follow-up [R1]. Overall 2013–2018 company spending is separately reported as only NIS 26M of a NIS 303M budget — 8.6% per one Times of Israel figure [R3], 6.8% per a Ynet figure using the same NIS 26M numerator [R11] — sources disagree on the exact percentage though both describe the same roughly NIS 26M actual spend. Of the NIS 60M local-authority assistance budget (2019), only NIS 21M (35%) had been drawn down by July 2023, rising to ~40% by Feb 2024, with 4 of 9 authorities submitting zero payment requests [R1]. What the Company's own project list credits as actually delivered: Netanya wave breakers plus sand nourishment; Ashkelon North Geotubes plus sand nourishment, including rehabilitation of the northern breakwater in segment 38; ~50,000 m³ of sand placed at Ashkelon South (2022); and beach elevation/cliff protection work at Bat Yam (2021) [R5]. Two structural design failures never got fixed across three audit rounds: no government body ever agreed a joint framework for funding and building **land** protections (raised in 2020, still explicitly "not rectified" in the 2024 follow-up) [R1]; and no government resolution on continuing the Company's own work or funding Phase B **sea** protections existed until Resolution 1851 (9 June 2024) — passed only after the audit period had closed, and only extending the Company to June 2026 with no committed Phase B budget [R1][R3].

## Outcome

**Coastal protection:** as of the follow-up audit, 8 of 13 km (62%) of cliff needing sea protection had none at all, and 14 of 27 land segments (52%) had no land protection [R1]. **Damage/danger, post-audit:** the Blue Bay collapse on the Netanya–Emek Hefer border (December 2024) [R6]; the Sidna Ali Beach, Herzliya collapse (Saturday 21 February 2026 morning, permanently closed zone) [R7][R8][R9] — note one Ynet source's AI-summarized date gave "February 2025" for what appears to be the same Sidna Ali event, but the primary Walla/Mako coverage is explicit about Saturday 21 February 2026, so both are given here per the reference rule [R6][R8]; a second Herzliya-area collapse the same month after a rainy night [R14]; and three separate collapses across the Sharon region in one week (Monday at Neot Afeka, Wednesday at Emek Hefer, Thursday at Netanya — "hundreds of tons of kurkar rock" — per Ynet, exact date not given but clearly after the 2024 report) [R10]. **Human toll cited in the underlying sources:** a fatal Hadera-area cliff collapse (2011) [R11]; the 30 May 2009 fatality near Netanya and the ~2008 Hadera-area collapse that injured six people [R2]; a moderate injury in Emek Hefer (June 2019) [R11]; a December 2019 Tel Aviv promenade collapse [R11]; the Company's own estimate of roughly one life-threatening collapse every two months along the belt [R4]. **Rate of retreat:** 20–30 cm/year under normal conditions, up to ~1 m/year in softer-rock stretches like northern Ashkelon; Ashkelon's cliffs are reported to have retreated ~22 m over 20 years [R3][R13]. **Financial exposure if unprotected:** a BDO consulting estimate of up to NIS 2.5 billion in infrastructure damage against an estimated NIS 490M cost of building the needed land defenses [R13]; the 2024 report's own climate-scenario estimate of NIS 700M (0.5 m/yr risk-zone expansion) to NIS 2,450M (1 m/yr) in expected cliff-collapse damage [R1]; and a much earlier (2009) estimate of NIS 67–90M (0.5 m/yr acceleration) to NIS 276M (1 m/yr) in building-damage cost [R2]. Ecology and structure longevity beyond the above are **not found** in these audit-focused sources; see this project's separate Ashkelon Geotube dossier for structure-specific outcome detail.

## Why it worked / why it failed

The **sea**-protection side is rated comparatively well by the Comptroller: a single dedicated implementing body (the Cliff Protection Company) sits under an inter-ministerial steering committee (Ministry of Environmental Protection, Israel Land Authority, Finance Ministry budget division), and the 2024 audit rates the sea-protection cost-estimate deficiency as "fully rectified" and the Company's budget-utilization deficiency as "considerably rectified" [R1]. The **land**-protection side failed structurally: municipalities are legally responsible but cannot fund state-scale works — "a local authority like Emek Hefer cannot handle a crisis of this magnitude," per Interior Ministry official Sion Lahbi [R12] — while the Ministry of Interior never built (or, since 2019, even requested from municipalities) an updated, comprehensive land-protection cost estimate, and the Prime Minister's Office, Ministry of Environmental Protection and Ministry of Interior never agreed a joint funding framework despite 8+ years since the April 2011 government decision — a deficiency explicitly flagged as "not rectified" in both the 2020 and 2024 audits [R1]. Local planning friction compounded this: Herzliya rejected the Company's 2016 segment analysis and did not submit the alternative it had promised VALHOF until years later, and Herzliya's own Seafront Plan took roughly a decade to reach its district planning committee [R1]. Chronic underfunding is described directly in press coverage: NIS 400M+ in pledged amounts reported as "untransferred," and the Finance Ministry offering only NIS 150M against the Company's NIS 360M Phase B ask as of 2024 [R3][R13]. Governance also broke down within a single body: the Israel Nature and Parks Authority (INPA) unilaterally decided **not** to build land protections in Tel Ashkelon National Park without bringing that decision to the Ministry of Environmental Protection or VALHOF for review — a failure the Comptroller calls out directly [R1].

## Unexpected results

The Cliff Protection Company downgrades its own opinion of a structure it built and maintains: the 2024 report notes the Company still keeps up the Ashkelon Geotube system (sand-filled geotextile sleeves) even though it "considers this solution less effective" than a permanent breakwater — consistent with this project's separate Ashkelon Geotube dossier finding that 15 of 48 tubes were destroyed before the original layout was even completed [Lior's files, 00_master_list.md row 44]. The decision to keep the Company running at all arrived only after the 2024 audit period closed — Government Resolution 1851 passed 9 June 2024, in the shadow of the Comptroller's own then-unpublished follow-up findings, and secures funding only through June 2026, leaving no long-term framework in place [R1][R3]. Despite 14+ years of policy and two prior audit rounds (2010, 2020) flagging the identical land-protection funding gap, the 2024 follow-up found this specific deficiency completely unchanged — an unusually blunt "not rectified" repeat for a follow-up audit [R1]. A climate-change damage model the Company itself commissioned from an outside consulting firm (draft opinion, July 2023) was sent to the Ministry of Environmental Protection and the Climate Change Preparedness Directorate and, as of the audit's conclusion, had received **no response at all** [R1].

## What could have been done better

**Comptroller's own recommendations (official/expert side):** consolidate responsibility for funding and building land protections in one body — echoing what already broadly works for sea protections; have the Ministry of Interior obtain updated, comprehensive land-protection cost estimates from every municipality; route INPA decisions through the Ministry of Environmental Protection/VALHOF for review; and update the 2010-era policy and 2011 recommendations for current sea-level-rise scenarios [R1]. **Ministry official's view:** Interior Ministry's Sion Lahbi argued for centralizing everything — sea and land — under the Cliff Protection Company rather than splitting land responsibility across dozens of individual municipalities, a position six of the coastal municipalities themselves also supported at the August 2020 Knesset committee session [R12]. **Local politician's view:** Herzliya Mayor Yariv Fisher has publicly argued funding must arrive before disaster strikes, explicitly invoking the 2021 Mount Meron crowd-crush disaster ("what happened at Meron is only a matter of time") while demanding roughly NIS 150M in immediate cliff-defense funding for his city alone [R13]. **Contrasting ministry view:** Ministry of Environmental Protection official Galit Cohen argued the sea side "has no barriers... we're progressing rapidly," implicitly placing the blame for the systemic failure squarely on the land side [R12]. **Public/beach-user side:** because this dossier covers a government audit program rather than a single visitable structure, there is no surfer/visitor "review" constituency in the sources found — the closest equivalent is the INPA's own repeated public messaging asking beachgoers simply to respect closures and warning signage rather than proposing any engineering fix themselves [R7][R8].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

This dossier **is** the Israel case, so relevance is direct rather than transferred. The ~45 km Hadera–Ashkelon kurkar cliff belt audited here stops north of Haifa (Haifa itself sits on the rockier Mount Carmel coast), but the same national Cliff Protection Company, the same statutory Plan 9/13/A framework, and the same State Comptroller oversight regime would apply to any future protection works on Haifa's own coastal bluffs [R1][R5]. **Tide range:** Israel's Mediterranean coast is microtidal, on the order of 0.15–0.4 m [R16] — far smaller than the multi-metre tidal swings that shape reef/breakwater design on Atlantic or UK coasts covered elsewhere in this project (e.g. Borth's ~4.7 m spring range), so tidal exposure timing is a minor design variable here compared with storm-wave action and surge. **Wave climate:** Israeli Mediterranean storm waves can exceed 5 m significant height with periods up to ~15 s, concentrated October–April with January–February typically the most active [R17] — the same storm-season window the 2024 audit's own photographed Tel Gador collapse (Aug 2021 → March 2022) and the 2025–2026 collapse reports (Dec 2024 Blue Bay; Feb 2026 Sidna Ali; the explicitly rain-triggered Herzliya collapse) all point to [R1][R6][R14]. **What transfers to a Haifa/general-Israel artificial-reef discussion:** the audit's clearest lesson is that a technically competent, actively monitored **sea**-side program (rated "considerably"/"fully rectified" by the Comptroller) can coexist indefinitely with a completely stalled **land**-side program under the very same national policy — this is the financing/governance baseline any new Israeli reef or cliff-protection proposal should be measured against: money and institutional responsibility need to be settled before construction, not after, because Israel's own record here is three consecutive audits (2010 → 2020 → 2024) repeating the identical funding-gap finding [R1].
