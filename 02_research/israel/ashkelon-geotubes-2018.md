## Motivation and who pushed for it

Israel's Mediterranean coast has ≈45 km of unstable cliff between Hadera and Ashkelon, 10–45 m high; the Ashkelon cliff is repeatedly described in Hebrew press as the most dangerous cliff section in the country, worsened by the marina's own breakwaters interrupting the natural south-to-north longshore sand supply (itself fed originally from the Nile Delta) [R13]. **Correction (2026-09-24): the specific "~55 m recession between 1987 and 2017" figure previously cited to [R9] does not appear on that Hebrew Wikipedia page** — re-fetched and searched directly for "55" and found no match; the page's actual sections are Background/Establishment, Goals/Activities, Monitoring, Central Projects, Org Structure, See Also, External Links, Footnotes. No replacement source for this specific number was found within this pass's fetch budget, so the figure has been dropped rather than re-cited to a page that doesn't contain it. The state-owned Government Company for Protection of the Mediterranean Coastal Cliffs (MCCP), founded 2013 (confirmed — [R9]), published the tender for an "embedded geotube" solution at Ashkelon in 2016 [R2][R13]. (The claim that MCCP's founding was "largely in response to State Comptroller pressure" is plausible given the 2005–2006 episode below, but was not independently confirmed against [R9] or any other source fetched this pass, so it is stated here only as context, not as a sourced fact.) This was **not the first geotube attempt at this exact stretch of coast**: a smaller, earlier trial was commissioned in 2005–2006 by the *municipal* Ashkelon Economic Company (החברה הכלכלית לאשקלון, distinct from the later national MCCP), after Israel's Ministry of Environmental Protection recommended geotubes as a temporary fix in March 2005 and the relevant Environmental Committee approved it in August 2005 [R9][R11]. See "What was actually built" for how the two projects differ and why Lior's notes' "Israel's first Geotube installation" framing needs a caveat.

## Design as planned

Per Corinthe Ingénierie's 2016 design report (F. Gouaud), the 2016–2019 project called for an embedded/submerged geotube line to run along the cliff-foot as a "soft" breakwater absorbing wave energy before it reaches the cliff, sized (per Lior's notes) at 48 tubes over roughly 10 dunams at about 4 m height [R1][R14]. No public source fetched this pass reproduces Gouaud's original drawings or a stated design return-period/storm criterion; that level of engineering detail was not accessible (see Blocked sources). One general-search synthesis (not independently verified against a primary document) described individual fabric rolls sized "25 × 4 m" being deployed and inflated by divers with a water/sand slurry at high pressure — this figure should be treated as tentative pending confirmation [R6 general-search note].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Construction ran April 2018 – April 2019 per MCCP's own project listing, under the official name "אשקלון צפון – הקמת גיאוטיובים" ("Ashkelon North – Establishment of Geotubes") [R4]. By the end of 2018 nearly all planned tubes were in place [R5][R13]. During four significant storms in December 2018–January 2019, **17 geotubes were completely destroyed** by wave/rock impact and **2 more by boat/propeller collisions** — 19 of 48 total, i.e. roughly 40% of the layout, damaged before the project's nominal completion [R5][R13]. This contradicts the "15 of 48" figure in Lior's notes; the discrepancy may trace to a conflation with the *separate, earlier* 2005–2006 project, which itself comprised only "≈15 segments" (per a surf-community source) — a smaller, ~1.7 million-NIS installation of ~20 m/~440‑ton tubes commissioned by the Ashkelon Economic Company, not MCCP [R11][R17]. That earlier attempt failed within under two years: by 2008 winter seas had torn open large sections, compounded by vandalism (residents using the sand mounds as bonfire/barbecue sites) and deliberate damage, leaving plastic-fabric fragments and exposed rebar as swimmer hazards [R11][R12][R17]. **Correction (2026-09-24): the previously-stated "State Comptroller review" of that 2005–2006 episode — with its quoted finding "lack of supervision and failure to enforce proper implementation caused the system's failure" — is not on Hebrew Wikipedia's MCCP page as claimed.** A direct, targeted search of that page for "מבקר המדינה" ("State Comptroller") returned zero matches. No alternative source for a Comptroller review of the 2005–2006 project was found within this pass's fetch budget, so the quoted finding has been dropped as unsupported/likely fabricated rather than re-cited to a page that doesn't contain it. What remains supported for the 2005–2006 failure is the storm damage + vandalism account in the paragraph above [R11][R12][R17]. Whether the 2018–2019 project repeated any similar governance failures (design deviation, missing monitoring, unenforced maintenance obligations) was not established from sources fetched this pass.

Following the Dec 2018–Jan 2019 storm damage, Israel's Ministry of Environmental Protection or MCCP (exact attribution unclear from the paywalled source) halted further geotube laying around **February 7, 2019**, filing a police report because the company reportedly "could not explain how the sleeves were damaged," while stating the devices were "urgently needed" [R7][R8]. Contractor Ta'avura subsequently claimed **33 million NIS** in damages and refused to fund further maintenance, arguing the geotube method itself was unsuited to Ashkelon's marine conditions; MCCP countered that Ta'avura's inexperience, not the design, caused the failures, and the dispute moved toward arbitration (Ta'avura petitioned the District Court to appoint an arbitrator; MCCP opposed arbitration and wanted the court to hear the case directly) [R6].

## Outcome

- **Coastal protection:** Contested. MCCP's CEO told the Knesset (Aug 2020) the completed structure "withstood two winters and met its targets" [R18]. Press coverage from the same period instead frames the project as effectively stalled by the contractor dispute over unfunded repairs [R6].
- **Surfing quality:** Not designed for surfing; no source found assessing wave quality for surfers at this exact 2018–2019 structure. The *earlier* 2005–2006 geotube trial at essentially the same stretch of coast is described by a Hebrew surf-community site as having made previously-ridden breaks (Kever HaSheikh, Guta) unsafe and technical, contributing to surfers abandoning those spots — plastic/fiber debris in the water risked entangling surfers, and exposed shell fragments/rebar were a hazard once the tubes degraded [R17].
- **Ecology:** Zalul (marine environmental NGO) states the Ashkelon geotubes "caused severe environmental pollution" once damaged, and argues cliff erosion is a slow natural process that localized hard/soft structures cannot properly address — advocating instead for fixing the longshore sand-transport blockages (e.g., at ports/marinas) [R20].
- **Longevity/damage:** ≈40% of the 2018–2019 tube layout (19 of 48) was destroyed within one winter of substantial completion [R5][R13]; no confirmed count of subsequent repairs beyond Ta'avura's disputed, partial maintenance work [R6].
- **Removal:** Not found — no source confirms the structure has been formally decommissioned or removed as of the most recent sources fetched (2023–2024 State Comptroller material).

## Why it worked / why it failed

Two explanations run in parallel through the sources, and they are not fully reconciled:
1. **Official framing (MCCP, 2020):** the structure "worked" — completed on schedule (2019), survived two subsequent winters, met performance targets [R18].
2. **Press/contractor framing (2019):** it "failed" in the sense that a large fraction of tubes were destroyed by storms and boat strikes within the first winter, triggering a funding standoff that halted further work and left responsibility for repair unresolved [R6][R7].

Contributing causal claims, none independently verified against an engineering report this pass:
- Ta'avura (contractor): the geotube method itself is unsuited to Ashkelon's wave/storm conditions [R6].
- MCCP (client): Ta'avura lacked the professional experience to execute the design correctly [R6] — this matches Lior's notes, which attribute the (larger, 48-tube project's) cost overruns to "the contractor not being professional/skilled enough" and not consulting an experienced international firm before construction [R1].
- Lior's own hypothesis (his opinion, not sourced to any article): the seabed dropped sharply from 4–5 m to 2–3 m exactly where the tubes sat, concentrating storm wave-breaking energy at that line and tearing the tubes apart — he considers this the shared root cause of both the Ashkelon and Boscombe (UK) geotube failures [R1].
- For the distinct, earlier 2005–2006 trial: storm damage plus vandalism/deliberate destruction of the sand mounds is documented [R11][R12][R17]. A claim that a formal State Comptroller review attributed the failure to procedural/governance lapses (design deviation, missing drainage, no monitoring, no enforcement) was checked against its cited source this pass and could not be confirmed — see correction above; treat that specific governance-failure account as unverified.

## Unexpected results

- **Boat-strike damage** was cited as a distinct failure mode (2 of the 19 destroyed tubes) alongside storm-wave damage — echoing Lior's Boscombe note about a propeller damaging geotubes there, suggesting boat/vessel interaction is a recurring, under-anticipated risk for shallow submerged geotube structures generally [R1][R5].
- **Contractor vs. client legal/financial deadlock**: rather than a clean engineering post-mortem, the public record shows an adversarial dispute (33M NIS damages claim vs. an 18.5M NIS original contract) heading to arbitration — an outcome not anticipated in the original tender/design framing [R6].
- **Two, not one, Ashkelon geotube attempts at the same stretch of coast** (2005–2006 and 2016–2019), roughly a decade apart, with the earlier one apparently forgotten or unacknowledged in how the 2018 project was publicly framed ("Israel's first") — this is itself a notable finding for Israel's institutional memory around a repeated technique, not something anticipated in Lior's source notes [R9][R11][R17].
- **Marine-biologist survey** referenced in Lior's notes (a post-completion survey ~1 year after finishing, reportedly showing large seabed cobbles as evidence of local wave energy) could not be located online this pass — likely an internal/unpublished report, not a public document [R1].

## What could have been done better

- **Experts' / officials' framing:** MCCP's own account (via Ta'avura's countered claim) is that better contractor vetting — specifically consulting an internationally experienced geotube firm before construction — would have avoided the failure; this matches the explanation already in Lior's notes [R1][R6].
- **Earlier project's outcome:** the 2005–2006 trial failed to storm damage and vandalism within under two years [R11][R12][R17]. A specific "State Comptroller pointed to governance fixes" account (drainage, monitoring, enforcement) was in an earlier draft of this dossier but could not be confirmed against its cited source this pass — dropped as unsupported (see correction above), not restated here as a sourced recommendation.
- **Surfers'/public view:** the "Shomrei Hayam" (Sea Guardians) Ashkelon petition and the marine biologist Dr. Yaakov Nirarani explicitly called (Sept 2019) for construction of a *second* breakwater/geotube phase to be halted until lessons from the first phase's damage were properly gathered and analyzed — i.e., a "pause and learn" position, not a specific engineering fix [R19].
- **NGO (Zalul) view:** treat the underlying cause (blocked longshore sand transport, largely from port/marina breakwaters) rather than building repeated localized hard/soft structures at the symptom site [R20].
- **Lior's own view (opinion, not a cited source):** avoid the sharp 4–5 m → 2–3 m seabed depth transition that he believes concentrates storm-wave breaking energy on the tube line; this is his inference, not confirmed by any engineering document found this pass [R1].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Israel's Mediterranean coast, including Haifa, is essentially **microtidal** (tide range on the order of tens of centimeters — well under 0.5 m), so tide range is not a meaningful design driver at either Ashkelon or Haifa; the dominant hydrodynamic driver at both is **winter storm wave energy** (roughly Nov–Mar), the same regime blamed for destroying 19 of 48 Ashkelon tubes in a single storm season [R5][R13]. Directly transferable lessons for any Haifa-area soft/submerged structure: (1) submerged geotextile tubes are vulnerable to concentrated storm-wave breaking and to boat/vessel strikes, both observed at Ashkelon; (2) contractor experience with this specific technique matters — MCCP and Ta'avura's mutual blame points to execution/oversight failures compounding any design risk (the earlier trial's own State Comptroller finding could not be confirmed this pass — see correction above); (3) Israel's marine (MCCP) vs. land (municipality) split in cliff-protection responsibility was flagged at the Knesset (Aug 2020) as a structural source of delay and coordination failure nationally — a governance risk to plan around explicitly for any new Haifa coastal structure [R18]; (4) local opposition from surfers/environmental groups arose quickly and specifically around geotube use at more than one Israeli site (Ashkelon and, separately, Tel Aviv's Hof HaTzuk — see disambiguation below), suggesting any Haifa proposal using this technique should expect similar organized pushback.

**"Hof HaTzuk" (חוף הצוק) disambiguation, resolved this pass:** Lior's notes flag uncertainty over whether "חוף הצוק" refers to the Ashkelon cliff project or a separate site. It is a **separate site**: "Hof HaTzuk" ("Cliff Beach") is a well-known, named beach in Tel Aviv, administered by the Tel Aviv-Yafo municipality (tel-aviv.gov.il/Visitors/Beaches/Pages/Hatzuk.aspx) [R22]. A distinct, later breakwater controversy at that Tel Aviv beach — opposed by surfers including 12-time Israeli surf champion Adi Gluska and surfer/lawyer Eyal Szmulewitz (Times of Israel, Oct 2019; Ynet, "מחאת הגולשים: שובר הגלים בת"א – מכת מוות לענף"; timeout.co.il, "עולים על הגל: המחאה נגד שובר הגלים בחוף הצוק") — is unrelated to the Ashkelon cliff-foot geotube project covered in this dossier, beyond both involving Hebrew-language "cliff"/"HaTzuk" naming and similar surfer opposition patterns [R22]. This also matches the project's own master-list entry (`israel-geotubes-other-sites`), which already tracks the Tel Aviv Hof HaTzuk breakwater dispute as a separate row [R2].
