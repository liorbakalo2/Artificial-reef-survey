## Motivation and who pushed for it

The earliest documented driver was simple coastal-protection necessity: Tel Aviv's beach was narrow, backed by retaining walls and a low bluff, and winter storms could erode it to nothing in places, undermining the promenade [R3]. The Hebrew Wikipedia and TAU Urbanologia material trace a longer planning lineage: in the 1930s, city engineer Jacob Shipman and the "Greenblatt Plan" (reviewed by British engineers Randall, Palmer and Tritton around 1934) already treated the shoreline as a planning asset needing active management, though that round of planning concerned promenade/landfill layout rather than detached breakwaters specifically [R7]. The detached-breakwater programme itself is first documented at Tel Baruch beach, reported in the Hebrew daily *Kol Ha'am* on 13 February 1964 as a project "to create a wide sandy beach strip" [R1][R2]. From the late 1960s the municipal company "Atarim" was created explicitly to rehabilitate Tel Aviv's beaches, promising restoration within three years and water-quality fixes within four [R5]. A technical champion for the structural approach is I. Fried of Civil & Marine Engineering Co. Ltd. (Haifa), who designed and published (1976, ICCE) the movable-bed-model-engineered central-coast breakwater system for Tel-Aviv (7 offshore breakwaters + 1 end unit) [R3]. *(A claim in an earlier pass of this dossier — that the method was "initially controversial among professionals and the Geological Institute" before a municipal board was won over by success at Tel Baruch/Sheraton/Hilton — could not be confirmed in this pass: it was sourced only to a WebSearch result with no retrievable URL, and the Hebrew Wikipedia article it claimed to draw from does not contain it when fetched directly. Dropped as unsupported.)*

## Design as planned

The best-documented individual design is Fried's Tel Baruch structure: a 200 m rubble-mound breakwater tied to the shore by a 100 m groyne, founded at −3.0 m MSL on a rocky seabed, with 2–6 ton rock armor units on the seaward face at a 1:3 slope and a crown elevation of +1.0 m MSL [R3]. Fried's 1976 paper describes a *third-stage, central-coast* system — 7 detached offshore breakwaters (130 m each, 120 m gaps) plus one shore-connected end unit, crest at +1.75 m MSL — engineered with the help of a movable-bed physical model at the Technion (May 1971–Oct 1972, 14 layout alternatives tested); the layout (lengths, gaps, offshore distance) was tuned in the scale model before construction, rather than fixed by rule of thumb [R3]. This model-tested design applied only to that third, central-coast stage — not to the earlier Tel Baruch (1965) or Sheraton/Hilton (1968) breakwaters, which were built without model testing and were later used as calibration reference points for the model [R3]. No plan documents were found (in this pass) for the individual designs of the later Gordon, Frishman, Bugrashov, Trumpeldor, Jerusalem or Aviv-beach structures; per Hebrew Wikipedia, the tombolo-vs-salient outcome at each beach is a function of the length-to-offshore-distance ratio (≥~0.9–1.0 for a full tombolo, ~0.6–0.7 for a partial salient), implying each beach's breakwater was sized against that target rather than being a uniform, repeated module [R2].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Fried (1976) does **not** report the central-coast system as completed by 1976 — the paper's own construction log states breakwater No.1 was completed 1971, No.2 in 1972, No.3 "only in 1975" ("due to war and the policy of the authority concerned"), and No.4 was "in the process of being completed" that year, against a planned total of 7 breakwaters + 1 end unit [R3]. What the paper does report as an achieved result, as of 1976, is that "periodical hydrographic surveys... indicate that the sedimentation process is generally well in accordance with the prediction of the model study," that "no permanent erosion was observed on the adjacent shore," and a "substantial widening of the protected beach" [R3] — i.e. the partially-built system was already matching the modeled intent, though the full 8-structure system was not yet finished. Over the following decades the programme was extended well beyond that: current inventories count roughly a dozen beaches with detached breakwaters (Tel Baruch, Metzitzim, Hilton, Gordon ×2, Sheraton, Frishman, Bugrashov, Trumpeldor, Jerusalem, Aviv ×2 — 11 per one wiki enumeration, worded "13+" in some secondary counts) plus three additional structures protecting infrastructure (the former Dolphinarium site, the "Second Aliyah" promenade section, and the Jaffa cliff park) [R2]. No source reached in this pass documents a case where an individual breakwater's built form departed from its design (dimensions, position) — the available record is silent on this rather than reporting a match or mismatch beach-by-beach.

One documented divergence between intended and actual outcome, not of construction but of use: Tel Baruch, the first and pilot breakwater beach, remained closed to bathers for years after construction because of water pollution unrelated to the structure, and did not reopen until the early 1990s — decades after the sand-retention goal had been achieved [R5].

A separate, non-breakwater intervention with a large, well-documented sediment side-effect is the Tel Aviv Marina (a harbor structure, not a detached breakwater): its construction interrupted the north-to-south longshore drift, eroding the beach immediately north of the marina while causing Gordon Beach, just south of it, to become one of the widest beaches in the city [R5]. This is presented by the source as a stronger, faster driver of Gordon Beach's width than the detached breakwater at Gordon itself, and is a useful contrast case for how a solid updrift structure (marina) versus a permeable/detached one (breakwater) each redistribute sand.

## Outcome

- **Sand retention / coastal protection:** Confirmed effective at the sites documented: Tel Baruch accreted a sand tombolo within a few years of the 1964 breakwater [R2][R8]; as of 1976, the partially-built central-coast system (4 of 8 planned units, see Quick facts) had already produced a "substantial widening of the protected beach" with "no permanent erosion... observed on the adjacent shore," per its designer's own published account [R3]. A Tel Aviv-Jaffa coastal-strip history source states the breakwater programme overall "caused considerable beach expansion and enabled them to accommodate large numbers of swimmers" [R5].
- **Water quality / ecology:** Fish documented sheltering among Tel Aviv's breakwaters include rabbitfish (striped and dusky spinefoot), black-tailed bream, striped sea bream, and shoals of silversides/round sardinella, typically juveniles up to about 20 cm [R2]. Set against this, critics (see below) attribute algae buildup and reduced water circulation to the calm water the same structures create [R9]. Tel Aviv beaches as a whole have an active bacterial-pollution problem (Gaolah, Bugrashov, Aviv and Tzuk beaches flagged by the Ministry of Health as of a June 2022 report), but the specific pollution driver documented in the sources reached here is cross-connected storm-drain/sewage plumbing overflowing in rain events, not the breakwaters themselves [R10]. *(A claim that this trend is "currently worsening" was dropped this pass — it was sourced only to a WebSearch result with no retrievable URL and could not be independently confirmed.)*
- **Surfing / recreation:** Mixed and site-specific. Where a beach is left open (no breakwater), e.g. the surfers' section of Hilton Beach, a natural reef plus the calm water created by neighboring structures has made it Israel's best-known reef break and a "mini-California, surfer's paradise" per surfer Arthur Rashkovan [R12]. Where a *new* breakwater has been proposed over an open, unprotected surf beach (the Tzuk/HaTzuk "last natural beach" case, 2019), surfers organized a 300-person protest, calling it a "death blow" to the sport [R13].
- **Longevity:** The Tel Baruch structure has stood since 1964–65; the twin Sheraton/Hilton breakwaters since 1968; the central-coast system's first units (No.1–3, plus No.4 underway) were standing and judged a durable success as of its designer's 1976 report [R3]. No breakwater removals were found in this pass — contrast Dr. Yaakov Nir's (marine geologist) claim, in the context of the 2019 Tzuk protest, that "many nations" have removed such structures over drowning risk, without citing which Israeli structures (if any) were removed [R13].
- **Damage:** Not found for the Tel Aviv structures specifically. (For contrast, a geotube — a different, fabric-based structure, not a rubble-mound detached breakwater — installed at Ashkelon in August 2017 tore, polluted the water and created swimmer hazards; this is cited by opponents of the 2019 Tzuk plan as a cautionary precedent, not as damage to an existing Tel Aviv rubble breakwater [R13].)

## Why it worked / why it failed

Where it worked, the documented mechanism is straightforward wave-diffraction sand trapping: a structure placed offshore and roughly parallel to the shoreline reduces wave energy behind it, and the resulting eddy/diffraction pattern deposits sand landward of the gap, building first a salient and, once the structure's length-to-offshore-distance ratio passes roughly 0.9–1.0, a full tombolo connecting to shore [R2]. Fried's account attributes the central-coast system's success specifically to having engineered layout (lengths, spacing, offshore distance) against a movable-bed physical model rather than a fixed template — early alternatives with uneven gap spacing caused "serious erosion opposite" the wide gaps in the model, which the final, evenly-spaced 8-unit layout avoided, and the built result (breakwaters No.1–4 as of 1976) showed "no permanent erosion... observed on the adjacent shore" [R3]. Where results were judged poor or the method opposed, the objections were less about the physical performance (sand retention) and more about side effects: drowning risk from rip/whirlpool currents at the structure's edges (Dr. Yaakov Nir, marine geologist) [R13]; loss of open-coast wave energy needed for surfing [R13]; and, per the environmental NGO Zalul (writing about a comparable planned project on this coast, at Herzliya, not Tel Aviv itself), stagnant-water algae buildup, drowning risk, invasive-species colonization of the hard structure, and loss of horizon views, with sand nourishment proposed as the less-invasive alternative [R9].

## Unexpected results

- The marina at Tel Aviv, a different (solid, updrift) kind of structure from the detached breakwaters proper, produced the largest and most visually obvious sand-redistribution effect in the record found here: erosion immediately north of the marina and a markedly widened Gordon Beach to its south, as a side effect of interrupting the natural longshore drift [R5].
- Achieving the sand-retention goal at Tel Baruch did not translate into an open beach: the site stayed closed to bathers for pollution reasons for years after the breakwater succeeded, only reopening in the early 1990s — the structure solved the sediment problem it was built for but not the separate water-quality problem that kept the beach shut [R5].
- The presence of breakwaters elsewhere in the city appears to have concentrated Israel's surf culture onto the few beaches deliberately *left open*: sources describe surfers moving to Hilton Beach specifically once central Tel Aviv beaches were "blocked by new piers" (i.e., protected/tombolo'd) in the 1970s, making an unbroken, breakwater-free stretch a scarcer and more prized resource for wave-riding than for calm-water swimming [R12].

## What could have been done better

- **Engineers'/planners' view:** The only first-hand design-side account found (Fried, 1976) frames the physical-model-tuned central-coast system as the corrective improvement over the earlier, non-modeled Sheraton/Hilton breakwaters — whose tombolos grew to permanently block water exchange with the shore, an outcome the new design explicitly tried to avoid by engineering gaps and a sinusoidal shoreline target into the model [R3].
- **Public-health/critics' view:** Marine geologist Dr. Yaakov Nir argues, in the context of opposing a new (geotube) breakwater at Tzuk beach in 2019, that "most" Israeli drowning cases stem directly or indirectly from breakwaters because of edge currents, and that removal (not more construction) is the better-practice direction taken by "many" other countries — though no specific removed structure is named [R13].
- **Environmental NGO view (Zalul, re: a comparable Herzliya project, not Tel Aviv itself):** proposes substituting a multi-year sand-nourishment programme (an initial ~200,000 m³ of sand, then 30,000–50,000 m³/year, to hold a 30–40 m beach width) for new hard breakwater construction, arguing this avoids the stagnant-water, view-blocking and invasive-species problems attributed to detached breakwaters, while still meeting the storm-protection goal [R9].
- **Surfers'/public view:** Surfers' central ask, both in 2019 (Adi Gluska, 12-time Israeli surfing champion) and implicitly in the historical record of surfers relocating to Hilton, is simply to leave a meaningful stretch of open, unprotected coast rather than extending the tombolo/breakwater programme further — i.e., their preferred improvement is non-construction on specific remaining open beaches, not a design change to the existing breakwaters [R13].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Tel Aviv sits on the same open Israeli Mediterranean coast as Haifa, so the physics transfer directly. Tidal range on the Tel Aviv coast itself is small: Fried's 1976 design paper states it "seldom exceeds 50 cm," with storm surge (not tide) pushing water levels to a −50 cm to +70 cm range in extreme westerly/easterly wind events [R3]. This is consistent with the general Mediterranean pattern of small tides tied to its narrow connections to the Atlantic — Wikipedia's tide article notes "the largest tides are close to their narrow connections with the Atlantic Ocean" for both the Mediterranean and Baltic [R19]. That means, as already noted in Lior's own project files, tidal range is not a structural design driver for Israeli detached breakwaters the way it can be on large-tidal-range coasts; crest height and durability are set mainly against storm wave height rather than tidal excursion (see Lior's notes on Shikmona buoy wave-climate data, which give minimum surfable SWH 0.45–0.6 m and observed peak SWH up to ~1.24 m for the Haifa area) [R17]. The core transferable lesson from Tel Aviv for a Haifa reef/breakwater proposal is the length-to-offshore-distance tombolo/salient design rule documented on Hebrew Wikipedia (≥~0.9–1.0 ratio for a full tombolo, ~0.6–0.7 for a partial salient) [R2], plus the two recurring failure modes surfaced by Tel Aviv's own record: edge-current drowning risk (Dr. Nir) and stagnant-water/algae water-quality complaints (Zalul, re: a comparable project) — both of which a Haifa design would need to address explicitly, and both of which are more about maintaining wave/water exchange through or around the structure than about tidal range [R13][R9].
