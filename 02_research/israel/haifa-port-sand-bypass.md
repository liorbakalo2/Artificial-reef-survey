## Motivation and who pushed for it

The stated driver is straightforward coastal-engineering causation: researcher Naama Sharid's 2019 University of Haifa study (supervised by Dr. Michael Lazar, Department of Marine Geosciences) found that south Haifa Bay's erosion problem traces to a chain of 20th–21st century marine infrastructure — the British-Mandate-era main breakwater (1929–1933), the Carmel container terminal (2010), the Navy port, and the new Bay Port — each further blocking the longshore drift of sand that once reached Kiryat Haim and the tank-farm-area beaches [R1][R4].

On the political side, Haifa deputy mayor **Dr. Einat Kalisch Rotem** says she personally pushed the Israel Ports Company (IPC), starting in December 2018, to move beyond repeat annual sand replenishment (which IPC had preferred as the lower-effort fix) toward a permanent breakwater solution, describing it as a multi-year fight against IPC's institutional preference for the cheaper, repeat-nourishment approach [R9]. The district planning committee, chaired by **Ruth Shortz Hanoch**, formally moved the IPC-authored 7-breakwater plan forward for deposit on 15 December 2024 [R10]. Environmental NGOs **Zalul** and **Adam Teva V'Din** (Israel Union for Environmental Defense) pushed back against the breakwater design itself, not against acting at all — they argued for study of softer alternatives (see "What could have been done better," below) [R2][R10][R11].

## Design as planned

The 1929–1933 breakwaters were designed purely as port-shelter infrastructure; sand-transport impact on downstream beaches was not part of the original design brief — that impact became an externality addressed only decades later [R8][R17]. From at least 2011, the addressed "design" for the sand problem has been recurring dredge-and-spray nourishment: a dredging vessel pumps clean marine sand through a pipeline directly onto the shoreline, with the Ministry of Environmental Protection setting the zones, quantities and method for each campaign [R4][R12][R14].

The newer, still-unbuilt design is a hybrid: 7 detached (offshore) rubble-mound breakwaters spanning ~1,700 m of the Kiryat Haim coastline, intended to reduce incoming wave energy so that sand accumulates and stays between the structures and the beach, combined with continued periodic sand replenishment and a re-graded promenade/expanded public beach, timed to align with the broader "Gate of the Bay" (שער המפרץ) area redevelopment [R10][R11].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

What has actually been built and executed, as of this pass, is the recurring nourishment programme, not a permanent structural fix: a ~35,000 m³ campaign completed 6 May 2020 [R3]; a ~150,000 m³ campaign in 2022 (≈100,000 m³ from the Bay Port basin + ≈50,000 m³ near the naval port) [R14]; and a separate ~1-month Neot Beach (Kiryat Haim) campaign starting 31 March 2022 [R12]. Academic sources describe this as an essentially continuous practice of spraying ~30,000–40,000 tons of sand per year from a dredger's pipe [R16].

The permanent-breakwater design — the structural fix intended to replace repeat nourishment — has been approved through Israel's planning appeals process (National Committee rejected the NGOs' appeals, per reporting from around May 2025–March 2026) but had **not been constructed** as of the most recent source found (March 2026), with a stated completion target of 2029 [R2][R9][R10][R11]. In other words, what exists on the ground today is still the ad-hoc nourishment regime, not the planned permanent structure.

## Outcome (coastal protection / ecology / longevity / damage)

- **Erosion continued despite nourishment.** Kiryat Haim beach, at least 25 m deep in 2004, had shrunk to between 0 and 20 m deep by the time of the Times of Israel's 2025 reporting [R2]. The academic estimate is blunt: the annual 30,000–40,000-ton spraying programme "has failed to halt the erosion" [R16].
- **Ecological side-effects of the fix itself.** Naama Sharid found that nourishment sand differed chemically and physically from native beach sand, disrupting the local ecosystem — she called this "quite ironic: attempting to rehabilitate our damage, we caused additional environmental disruption" [R1][R4]. A 2019 campaign reportedly improved on this by better matching sand composition [R4].
- **A separate 2016–2017 south-Haifa-Bay nourishment project is characterized (via a search-engine summary of an MDPI paper) as less durable than comparable European beach-nourishment projects**, attributed to unsuitable beach morphology, insufficient sand volume, and imported sand grain size mismatched to native sand — this is a search-summary-derived claim, not independently verified against the paper's full text this pass (the paper itself returned 403 to WebFetch) [R16].
- **If nourishment stopped, Sharid warned, "within months there will be no beach"** at the tank-farm-area beach near Kiryat Haim [R1][R4] — underscoring that nourishment is treating a symptom, not the underlying sand-supply deficit caused by the breakwaters.

## Why it worked / why it failed

Nourishment has kept beaches nominally present between campaigns but has not stopped the underlying erosion trend, because it does not address the root cause — the port breakwaters (original 1933 structure, then the Carmel terminal, Navy port, and Bay Port extension) permanently intercepting the longshore sand supply that used to reach these beaches [R1][R4][R17]. The academic critique adds that even the nourishment itself has been executed sub-optimally: too little volume, wrong grain size, and (for the 2016–2017 south-Bay project specifically) poor fit to local beach morphology [R16]. The planned detached-breakwater solution is explicitly designed to correct this by permanently trapping sand near shore rather than repeatedly importing it — but as of this pass it remains unbuilt, so its real-world performance cannot yet be assessed [R2][R10][R11].

## Unexpected results

- The fix for one erosion problem (recurring nourishment) generated a second, distinct environmental problem: ecologically mismatched sand disrupting the shoreline habitat it was meant to restore — the "ironic" outcome Sharid describes [R1][R4].
- The National Planning Committee explicitly ruled that the new breakwaters' own environmental impact is "necessary harm," i.e., accepted an acknowledged environmental cost (altered views, altered marine circulation) as the price of stopping a worse ongoing harm (total beach loss) [R11].
- Opponents argue the structures will trap polluted urban runoff behind them and create dangerous rip currents for swimmers and surfers — a safety/pollution concern raised as a side-effect of the very structures meant to protect the coast [R2].

## What could have been done better (experts'/public view vs. official view)

**Environmental-NGO / critics' view (Zalul, Adam Teva V'Din):** the breakwater approach is "outdated"; alternatives that were "not thoroughly examined" during planning include artificial/ecological reefs, sand-filled geotubes, and perforated concrete balls designed to absorb wave energy more gently than solid rubble-mound structures [R2][R11]. Adam Teva V'Din's broader critique: "Coastal protections in Israel are always carried out at the last minute and not as part of a regulated policy" [R2]. Critics also fault the plan's scope as too narrow — addressing Kiryat Haim alone without assessing knock-on effects on neighboring beaches (a classic sand-starvation-downdrift risk, structurally the same mechanism that caused the original problem) [R11].

**Official / planning-committee view:** the Ministry of Environmental Protection itself concluded (per the May 2025 coastal-environment committee session) that there is "no preferable alternative" to the breakwaters, and that this option is environmentally best because it is expected to enable Kiryat Haim's beach to actually recover [R11 / project discovery notes]. The National Committee's appeals sub-committee rejected the NGOs' objections on this basis [R2][R11].

**Academic view:** better grain-size matching and larger, more consistent nourishment volumes (closer to the successful European practice) could have made the pure-nourishment approach more durable, rather than switching straight to hard structures [R16].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

This is itself the Israeli/Haifa case, so the transferable lesson runs in the other direction — to Lior's own Haifa reef proposal (per his outreach pitch to Deputy Mayor Avihu Han, described in Lior's own notes) [R18]:

- **Tide range:** not independently re-confirmed by a fetched source this pass; Lior's own project notes already treat Israel's small Mediterranean tidal range as a structural-stability advantage for a reef versus large-tidal-swing ocean sites — that framing is Lior's own and remains a project gap needing an external general Mediterranean tidal-range citation (flagged previously as Gap #6 in the project's master list) [R18].
- **Wave climate:** the project's existing Shikmona-buoy baseline (off Haifa) — minimum surfable-window significant wave height 0.45–0.6 m, swell direction clustered 284–300° (WNW), observed peaks up to SWH 0.98 m / max 1.24 m — is the relevant local wave-energy context for judging how much sand-trapping or wave-focusing effect any new Haifa structure (reef or breakwater) could realistically have [R18].
- **What transfers directly:** the Haifa Port case is a real, measured, decades-long demonstration of exactly the mechanism a Haifa surf-reef proposal must avoid or account for — a shore-parallel or shore-connected hard structure intercepting longshore drift starves the downdrift beach. Any reef design for Haifa needs to either be sited/shaped to avoid creating a new updrift/downdrift sand-starvation pair, or explicitly plan (and budget) for the same kind of recurring nourishment/monitoring regime the port has needed since 2011 [R1][R4][R16].
