## Motivation and who pushed for it

The driver was coastal erosion at a public tourism beach, not surf amenity or a private developer's project. Sigandu is described in the source paper as "a coastal tourism area" in the coastal zone of Batang District, Central Java, that came to "[face] severe coastal erosion problem" [R1]. The paper attributes the erosion specifically to construction of a jetty for the Batang Commercial Port (Pelabuhan Niaga Batang), which the authors say disturbed the "longshore transport equilibrium": the jetty's original purpose was to trap sediment, but "the classical problem of erosion and accretion take place in a short time, soon after the jetty construction finished" [R1] — i.e., the jetty caused erosion on the down-drift side rather than only the intended accretion. Batang Commercial Port is a real, still-operating facility with a 150 m pier handling fishery goods, basic necessities, and construction-material distribution [R5], consistent with this jetty being a working port structure rather than a minor pier.

Once erosion became visible, "the local government of Batang District" is described as having become aware of the problem, and the district's Marine and Fisheries Affair (Dinas Kelautan dan Perikanan) "initiate[d] the solution by applying low-crested breakwater structures using geotextile tube," with work commencing in September 2014 [R1]. This reads as a local/district-government-driven public-infrastructure response to a locally visible erosion problem at a public beach, not a privately financed tourism development or a national-level program — though no document naming a specific funding source, contractor, or approval process was found (unreferenced for the funding/procurement chain — needs source). Indonesian Wikipedia's (uncited) page on the beach separately describes a general history of the beach going through erosion phases severe enough to destroy tourist facilities, homes, and vendor stalls, which is broadly consistent with — but not independently sourced to — the same erosion problem [R4].

## Design as planned

The stated design concept was straightforward: install low-crested breakwaters made of geotextile tube to absorb/attenuate wave energy and halt the erosion caused by the disrupted longshore drift near the Batang Commercial Port jetty [R1]. No design report, drawing set, or design wave/return-period target was located. The paper is framed as a monitoring/case study of an intervention already decided on and built (i.e., a post-construction beach-profile study), not a design document, so it does not describe design promises about eventual beach width, ecology, or amenity value the way a design brief would (unreferenced — no design-stage source found).

## Outcome

The only outcome data found is from the paper's own abstract, describing a short-term result: "One month after the tube installation, rapid sedimentation occurred by the formation of salients and tombolos behind the structures" [R1]. A salient (a shoreline bulge toward, but not connecting to, a detached breakwater) or tombolo (a sand spit that fully connects the shoreline to the structure) forming this quickly is the classic sign that a low-crested/detached breakwater is trapping sediment as intended — but it is also a well-known risk factor for a badly designed low-crested structure (too continuous a crest, or built too close to shore, can silt up navigation/circulation or create an unwanted permanent land-tie). The paper states its beach-profile results came from "a series of monitoring and cross section measurement as well as visual observation" [R1], implying quantitative before/after profile data exists in the full paper, but no actual numbers (metres of accretion, volume change, rate over time) were retrievable in this research pass (unreferenced — needs the full paper). No source describes storm damage, structural failure, longevity beyond the initial monitoring period, or the site's condition as of 2026.

## Why it worked / why it failed

Only a short-term positive signal (rapid salient/tombolo formation) is documented, so this project cannot be called an outright success or failure on the evidence found — it is a case where an intervention against port-jetty-induced erosion appeared to be trapping sand within about a month, per the only source located [R1]. No source discusses the underlying engineering causes in the "why" sense the template asks for — geotube diameter/fill pressure and stability, crest height relative to tide range, wave climate at the site, marine growth on the tubes, or any storm event and its effect — because the full paper was not accessible (unreferenced — needs the full paper for a genuine causal analysis). What the abstract does establish causally is the erosion's own origin: a port jetty trapping/blocking longshore sediment transport, which is a well-documented general coastal-engineering failure mode (updrift accretion, downdrift erosion) rather anything specific to the geotube fix [R1].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

The Java Sea north-Java coast where Sigandu sits is, like Israel's Mediterranean coast, a small-tidal-range environment, though not as small as Israel's. A live sample of the Batang tide table shows a typical tidal range on the order of 0.6–0.9 m (highs around 0.83–0.95 m against lows around 0.05–0.35 m across the days sampled) [R7], compared with a live sample for Haifa showing a typical range of only about 0.3–0.4 m (highs around 0.36–0.39 m against lows around 0.01–0.07 m) [R8]. So Israel's coast is even more micro-tidal than this Indonesian site — meaning a low-crested/submerged structure's crest-depth design margin has even less tidal variation to absorb at Haifa than it did at Batang, which if anything makes precise crest-depth tuning more forgiving to plan for in Israel, not less.

The more directly transferable lesson is the erosion mechanism itself, not the structure's dimensions (which remain unknown here): a port or harbor jetty interrupting longshore sediment transport, causing downdrift erosion that then gets treated with a secondary low-crested/geotube breakwater, is exactly the kind of "port-adjacent erosion, later patched" pattern relevant to Israeli ports (e.g., the sand-bypassing and nourishment programs already flagged as an open Israel-research topic in this project's master list [R10]). The apparent quick sedimentation response (salients/tombolos within a month) is a useful positive data point for "geotube low-crested breakwaters can trap sand fast," but without the full paper's quantitative profile data or any confirmation of multi-year durability, it should not be over-generalized as proof the design works long-term — the same caution the master list already raises about most reviewed ASR/geotube projects lacking confirmed long-term outcome data [R9][R10].
