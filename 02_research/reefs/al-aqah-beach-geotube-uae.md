## Motivation and who pushed for it

The driver was coastal erosion, not tourism marketing or politics as such. Rubble-rock groins already protecting the beach had degraded and were failing under "repeated wave actions and yearly unexpected cyclone attacks" [R1]. The 2014 and 2013 papers both open with the same framing: erosion at Al Aqah Beach worsened after the cyclonic storm Gonu damaged the existing groins, which "lacked substantial protection" [R1][R2][R3]. Cyclone Gonu made landfall in the Gulf of Oman region in June 2007 [R5] (the papers themselves do not give the cyclone's exact date; R5 is a separate hotel-news source that independently confirms the June 2007 date for Gonu's regional impact). The client is identified only as "Le Meridien Hotel" in the manufacturer's own case study [R1] — i.e., the push came from the beachfront resort operator wanting to protect its shoreline and guest amenity, not from a public municipal program that this research could find. Lior's files do not discuss this site's motivation beyond citing the same 2014 paper as the master-list source [R9].

## Design as planned

The manufacturer's design brief, per its own account, weighed "tourist attractions, project budget, and environmental demands" and selected geotextile tubes over re-building in rock because tubes offered "easy installation, eco-friendliness and cost-effectiveness" [R1][R2]. The chosen layout was an "anti-U shape": two 200-m tube arms projecting from the shore on the beach's south and north sides, enclosing a 228 m × 225 m zone as a combined groin-plus-submerged-breakwater system meant to cut incoming wave energy and nourish/retain the beach behind it [R1]. Construction method as planned: tubes pumped and filled with sand in situ to first act as a temporary cofferdam, then left as the permanent submerged structure [R1]. The stated design promise was to make the coast "well-controlled" against further cyclone-driven erosion while keeping the structure inconspicuous for a resort beach (i.e., a submerged rather than emergent structure) [R1]. No wave-height, wave-period, or design-storm-return-period target was found in any source reached.

## Outcome

The manufacturer states plainly that the intervention made erosion "well-controlled" and "prevented Fujairah coast from further attacking by cyclones," and that the beach was "successfully restored" [R1]. No independently measured outcome data were found: no beach-width or volume survey, no wave-attenuation measurement, no ecological survey, and no record of the structure surviving (or failing under) a subsequent storm. ## Why it worked / why it failed

Only the manufacturer's framing is available, so this section should be read as the designer's own account of its predecessor's failure mode and its own fix, not an independent audit:
- **Why the earlier rubble groins reportedly failed:** they "sink due to the repeated wave actions and yearly unexpected cyclone attacks" [R1] — i.e., insufficient mass/stability of the rock structure under cumulative wave loading and periodic cyclone surge, per the manufacturer.
- **Why the geotube fix was expected to work:** combining a rebuilt groin (to trap/retain sand against longshore drift) with a submerged breakwater (to cut incoming wave energy) addresses both the sediment-transport and the wave-energy side of the erosion problem simultaneously, using a material (filled geotextile) the manufacturer describes as easy to install and cost-effective compared with re-building in rock [R1][R2][R3].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Al Aqah sits on the Gulf of Oman, a micro-tidal to low-meso-tidal environment: live tide-table data for Al Fujayrah shows a typical daily tidal range on the order of 1.5–2.0 m (e.g., highs of 2.15–2.75 m against lows of 0.17–1.58 m in the data sampled) [R7]. This is a substantially larger tidal range than Israel's Mediterranean coast (Haifa/Ashkelon are effectively micro-tidal, well under 0.5 m — per Lior's other project files, not re-derived here). That difference matters directly for transferability: a submerged geotube breakwater's crest freeboard/depth has to work across the whole tidal cycle, so a design calibrated to a ~1.5–2 m Gulf-of-Oman tide range is not a like-for-like template for Haifa's much smaller tidal envelope — the same crest depth would behave very differently (more consistently submerged, or more consistently exposed) on Israel's coast. What *does* transfer conceptually is the core design trade-off the project illustrates: pairing a groin (sediment retention) with a submerged breakwater (wave-energy reduction) to fix erosion without an emergent, view-blocking wall — the same submerged-vs-emergent trade-off Lior's ICM transcript notes elsewhere (an unnamed UAE project with "an 8-year stable beach guarantee behind this submerged structure," [R10]) — but the wave climate, sediment type, and structure sizing would need independent recalculation for Haifa, and no wave-height data for Al Aqah was found in the sources reached to make that comparison quantitative (unreferenced — needs a source with Gulf-of-Oman significant-wave-height data for Fujairah).

Note on a possible duplicate: Lior's files flag that this Al Aqah project might be the same UAE project mentioned in an ICM (International Coastal Management) conference talk transcript — an unnamed "UAE coastline project" comparing an emergent breakwater (client 1) against a submerged structure with "an 8-year stable beach guarantee" (client 2) on "one stretch of coastline in the UAE" [R10]. This research found no name, city, or client match between the ICM transcript's project and Al Aqah/Le Méridien, so the two should be treated as **unconfirmed / possibly distinct** until a source names the ICM project's location.
