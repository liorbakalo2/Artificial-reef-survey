## Motivation and who pushed for it

In 1984 the California Coastal Commission approved a 900-foot rock groin for Chevron's El Segundo refinery, built to stop beach erosion that threatened the refinery's offshore oil-tanker pipeline [R1][R7]. Tom Pratte, a founding member of the Surfrider Foundation, got a condition written into that permit: Chevron would have to monitor the surf near the groin, and if the groin measurably degraded it, Chevron would have to mitigate for the lost surfing resource [R1][R7]. In 1990 the Coastal Commission affirmed that the surf north of the groin — the Grand Avenue break — had indeed been adversely affected, both by the groin's shape and by sand buildup burying the break [R7]. The Commission, Chevron and Surfrider then spent roughly eight years (1990–1998) weighing mitigation options — Surfline's 2008 retrospective specifically mentions ideas as basic as beach showers and better parking being discussed before an artificial reef was settled on in 1998, with Chevron agreeing to pay $300,000 [R7]. It then took roughly two more years to secure the money, obtain permits and decide who would build it, before construction started in fall 2000 [R7]. Tom Pratte himself died in 1994, before the reef existed; it was named in his honor once built [R1]. This is the first case Surfrider cites in which the Coastal Commission treated a surf break as a protectable coastal recreational resource — a precedent that, per Surfrider's own framing, helped establish the legal groundwork later used to help protect the Trestles break from development [R7].

## Design as planned

The original 1997 CEQA (California Environmental Quality Act) filing by Surfrider Foundation, submitted through the City of Los Angeles Department of Parks & Recreation, described the project as "10–20 large geo-textile fabric bags containing approx. 5,000 cubic yards of sand," reviewed by the Coastal Commission, Department of Fish and Wildlife, State Lands Commission and Regional Water Quality Control Board over a Dec 2, 1997 – Jan 2, 1998 state review period [R8]. As engineered by Skelly Engineering, the built design called for a V-shaped (or delta-shaped) reef with its arms set at roughly a 45° angle to the predominant swell direction, intended to focus and peel incoming waves the way a natural point or reef break does, cresting close to the low-tide waterline so it would break consistently [R4]. The stated goal was purely to restore a lost surfing amenity at the Grand Avenue break — not beach nourishment or ecological habitat creation, unlike some later multi-purpose reefs elsewhere (Narrowneck, Gold Coast) [R4][R1].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Construction proceeded in two funded phases rather than one: 110 geotextile bags placed in fall 2000 using the initial $300,000 Chevron payment, then a further ~90 bags added in spring 2001 once the California Coastal Conservancy contributed a further $200,000–$250,000 (sources disagree on the exact figure) — bringing the total to roughly 200 bags [R1][R7]. Even after that expansion, the built reef was far smaller than the original 1997 proposal's ~5,000 cubic-yard (~3,800 m³) target: built volume is reported at only ~1,400–1,600 m³ [R2][R4] — well under half the size first proposed, and, in Surfrider's own later accounting, roughly one-seventh the volume of the Mount Maunganui (New Zealand) reef and somewhere between one-tenth and one-thirtieth the volume of Narrowneck (Gold Coast, Australia) [R7]. Chad Nelsen, Surfrider's Environmental Director, later put the gap down to budget: "we probably should have said $300,000 is not enough" [R7]. Wikipedia's comparative reef-volume table separately notes the individual bags/containers used at Pratte's were markedly smaller (~8 m³ each) than those used at Narrowneck (140–320 m³ each) — consistent with the same "too small" diagnosis reached from a different angle [R2].

## Outcome

Wikipedia's summary states the reef "had very little effect on the surfing conditions at Dockweiler Beach and ... no measurable effect on the shoreline morphology" [R1][R2]. Surfline's 2008 feature is blunter: over "eight plus years, Pratte's Reef was as useless as useless gets" and "it never produced a consistently surfable wave" [R7]. Chad Nelsen (Surfrider) explained the mechanism: "when the surf gets big, it breaks outside the reef" [R1] — the crest was too deep to trigger breaking on larger swells, while the post-groin beach itself (straightened and steepened by sand trapped on the north side of the Chevron groin) produced mostly closeouts regardless of the reef [R7]. Diver surveys found that bags had moved, sunk, and been buried by sand, and that the black polypropylene material was visibly deteriorating [R4] — a Wikipedia-sourced (Multi-purpose reef) description says the reef had "largely disintegrated" within about two years of construction, from bag rupture, wave scour and self-burial [R2]. No coastal-protection or ecological benefit is reported anywhere in the sources found; this was a pure surf-amenity structure, and by that single metric it failed. The reef was removed over two phases: Phase I in fall 2008 (Sept 30–Oct 17, a roughly three-week operation, ~200 bags at 14,000 lb each brought ashore by a dive crew and shore crew) and a Phase II cleanup in fall 2010, coinciding with the expiry of the original 10-year experimental permit [R3][R6][R7][R9][R10]. It was the first-ever removal of a purpose-built artificial surfing reef [R6].

## Why it worked / why it failed

- **Undersized for its budget and site.** Every source agrees the core problem was money: a $300,000–$550,000 construction budget bought a reef roughly 1/7 the size of Mount Maunganui's and 1/10–1/30 the size of Narrowneck's — both of which also underperformed expectations, but at far larger scale [R7].
- **Crest depth mismatch.** On big swells the reef's crest was too deep to trigger breaking, so waves broke outside it; a Surfrider-sourced figure quoted in the Surfline comments put the reef at "8 feet deep" at zero (low) tide, which a commenter (see Reviews section) called too deep to ever be "nearly dry" the way a good reef break needs to be at low tide [R7]. (Note: this 8-ft/2.4 m figure conflicts with a separate technical description putting the crest at only ~0.9 m below MLLW — see Quick facts.)
- **Pre-existing degraded beach.** The Chevron groin itself had already straightened and steepened the beach north of it and buried the original Grand Avenue break in trapped sand — conditions a small reef mitigation project could not reverse [R7].
- **Material and structural failure.** Diver surveys documented bags moving, sinking, being buried, and the polypropylene fabric itself deteriorating within roughly two years — a sand-filled fabric bag resting on a sandy, mobile seabed with no rigid foundation is inherently prone to settling and burial under wave and current action [R4][R2].
- **Insufficient pre-construction monitoring.** Nelsen, in hindsight, said more monitoring of sand bars and beach conditions before construction "would have told us the reef was too small before we even put it in" [R7].

## Unexpected results

- No fishing-pressure or ecological side effects are reported for Pratte's Reef in any source found — a contrast with Narrowneck (Gold Coast), where the geotextile containers were colonized by macro-algae, benthos and pelagic fish over time and the reef "has become popular with locals for (spear-)fishing, diving and snorkeling"; the same assessment separately flags that "geo-textile containers ... can be easily damaged by vessels," i.e. a boat-anchor/propeller vulnerability, as a design risk rather than a documented incident [R17].
- The project's most consequential outcome may have been legal/political rather than physical: the mitigation condition Tom Pratte secured in the original 1984 permit is credited by Surfrider as the first case in which the California Coastal Commission formally recognized a surf break as a protectable coastal recreational resource — precedent Surfrider says later helped in the fight to protect Trestles from development, a far more consequential outcome than the reef's own surf performance [R7].
- Surfrider itself came to see the whole mitigation model as risky, updating its institutional policy on artificial reefs after this project and moving toward a stance of protecting existing surf breaks rather than promising to recreate lost ones: "I don't think we should ever trade a surf spot for the promise of building something else" (Chad Nelsen) [R7].
- No litigation was found in any source reviewed for this dossier.

## What could have been done better

**Authors'/experts' view (Surfrider's own Chad Nelsen, in the 2008 Surfline retrospective):**
- Should have told Chevron up front that $300,000 was not enough to build a working reef [R7].
- More monitoring of sand bars and beach conditions before construction would have revealed the reef was undersized before it was built [R7].
- More broadly, Nelsen argues mitigation itself is often "a false promise" — his preferred fix, looking back, is protecting existing surf breaks rather than trying to engineer replacements after the fact [R7].

**Surfers'/forum view (named commenters on the 2008 Surfline article, see Reviews section below):**
- Depth/tide design was wrong — commenter "Andrew" argued the reef should have been "nearly dry on a negative tide to be legit," not 8 feet deep at zero tide [R7].
- Distance from shore was wrong — but commenters disagreed on which direction: "Randy Wright" thought it was "simply too far away from the shore" [R7], while coastal engineer Dr. Jose Borrero, quoted in a separate secondary summary, called it "too small and too close to shore" [R4].
- Some argued for cruder, cheaper materials — commenter "prattes is a joke" suggested rocks or "smashed up cars" would have worked as well or better than engineered geotextile bags [R7].
- Several commenters blamed a lack of surfer input in the engineering process ("evan": "sounds like surfrider should hire a new team of engineers, not a bunch of pencil pushers that don't know the first thing about surfing slabs") [R7].
- At least one commenter ("Ryan Vick") argued the reef should simply have been left in place rather than removed, since it did occasionally produce a rideable wave on larger swells [R7].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

- **Tidal range is the single most transferable engineering lesson from this project.** Pratte's Reef's documented failure mode included a crest depth that was wrong for the local tide swing — too deep to break big swells, and (per a Surfrider-sourced figure quoted by a Surfline commenter) around 8 feet (~2.4 m) deep even at zero/low tide [R7]. Southern California's Santa Monica Bay has a mean tidal range on the order of 1.4–1.7 m (based on live tide-table data for Los Angeles, accessed 2026-09-24) [R12]. Haifa, by contrast, has a mean tidal range of only about 0.3–0.4 m (live tide-table data for Haifa, accessed 2026-09-24) [R13] — consistent with the general statement that "the Mediterranean Sea experiences extremely small tides" because its narrow connection to the Atlantic cuts off the main astronomical tidal forcing [R11]. A crest depth calibrated for a ~0.3–0.4 m tidal swing is a much smaller, more predictable engineering target than one that has to work across a ~1.5 m swing — directly supporting Lior's own thesis (recorded in his outreach draft to the NGO Zalul, see 01_source_notes/pptx_goldcoast_swells_people_photos.md) that Israel's small tidal range is a structural advantage for reef stability and reduces the risk of the exact "sometimes too deep, sometimes exposed" failure mode Pratte's Reef suffered [R15].
- **Budget-driven undersizing is a transferable risk, not a site-specific one.** Pratte's Reef's core failure — a reef sized to a fixed legal-mitigation budget ($300k–$550k) rather than to the wave climate it needed to shape — is a governance/funding lesson as much as an engineering one, and applies to any small pilot reef Israel might build on a limited budget.
- **Material degradation (UV/wave abrasion of polypropylene bags within ~2 years) is a warning for any low-cost geotextile design** proposed for the Israeli coast, including Lior's own sketch of "cheap & light materials... for fast deployment and removal" (01_source_notes/pptx_goldcoast_swells_people_photos.md, slide 9) [R15] — Pratte's shows that cheap fabric bags in an active wave environment have a short structural lifespan even before considering tidal stress.
- Israel has no directly comparable built ASR to compare against yet (per the project's master list, Israel's only confirmed built geotextile project is the Ashkelon Geotube, a shore-protection structure, not a surf reef) [R14].
