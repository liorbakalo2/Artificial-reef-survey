## Motivation and who pushed for it

Japan's national artificial-reef practice grew out of a policy shift, not a single local campaign. The 1999 revision of the Coastal Law moved national coastal policy away from relying solely on emergent seawalls ("linear defense") toward combining a submerged wide-crested reef with a gently sloped seawall behind it ("areal defense") [R1]. The explicit reasoning given by prefectural coastal offices is dual: reduce wave run-up/overtopping onto the seawall and stabilise/retain beach sand, while keeping the structure below the waterline "for aesthetic reasons" so the shoreline view is not blocked the way a conventional emergent offshore breakwater (離岸堤) would block it [R2]. At Niigata, the driver was direct: the coastline there has retreated roughly 350 m at its maximum from the Meiji era to the present, a shift attributed by one review to the combined effect of Shinano River sediment management, port development interrupting longshore sand transport, and severe Sea-of-Japan winter storms — not a single natural cause [R7]. Niigata's own protection works (offshore breakwaters and artificial reefs together) began around fiscal year 1977 [R3]. At Iburi Coast, Hokkaido, the driver documented in the national public-works evaluation is storm-surge (high-tide) flood risk to the industrial cities of Tomakomai and Shiraoi, whose population has been roughly flat but whose household count has been rising, alongside the coast's ongoing use as an active fishing ground — which meant the project required negotiation with fishery stakeholders over impacts to fish habitat, not just sign-off from disaster-prevention officials [R9].

## Design as planned

The nationally promoted design promises three things: (1) reduce wave energy and overtopping onto the seawall behind it, by using a crest so wide (≈30–50 m or more) that it dissipates waves the way a natural fringing reef does [R1][R6]; (2) trap and stabilise beach sand behind the structure, reversing or halting erosion [R1][R2]; (3) preserve the coastal view/landscape by keeping the crest submerged, typically 0–2 m below the design water level, rather than building an emergent breakwater [R2][R13]. Newer designs explicitly add an ecological promise: replacing the concrete cap with natural stone, and using it as a base for marine-organism attachment and habitat, which is cited as improving on older, purely concrete designs [R6]. No design document was found that promises surf amenity — unlike the Australian/Californian "artificial surf reef" (ASR) concept the Bar & Drimer (2023) paper surveys, Japan's programme is framed entirely around coastal protection and, secondarily, ecological/recreational multi-use, not wave-riding [R1][R6].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Individual-site build records are fragmentary in the sources gathered, but a consistent pattern emerges: designs are revised mid-programme rather than fixed at the outset. At Iburi Coast, the national re-evaluation record states explicitly that the block mass/specification used in the artificial reef was "ranked up" (upgraded) after reviewing quantities and construction cost, and that this specification change was itself the stated cost-reduction measure for the project — implying the reef's block sizing was not settled at first design and had to be strengthened and re-costed as the project proceeded [R9]. At Niigata, the reef-and-breakwater programme has been built incrementally since fiscal 1977 rather than as a single completed structure, with a separate stretch (the Arima-hama/Niigata-West-Coast section) apparently built later, around fiscal 1986, according to one source [R3]. A 1989 Japan Society of Civil Engineers conference paper is titled "A survey of the actual subsidence of artificial reefs on the Niigata coast" (新潟海岸の人工リーフの沈下実態調査), covering the Ariake and Igarashi beach sections — its existence as a dedicated subsidence study strongly implies that at least some built reefs there settled into the seabed by more than the design anticipated, but the paper's actual findings (how much subsidence, over what period) were not accessible in this research pass [R15] (unreferenced — needs source for the magnitude).

## Outcome

The clearest documented outcome is from the FY2022 national public-works re-evaluation of the Iburi Coast project (Tomakomai/Shiraoi, Hokkaido), which states as an official finding: "Through the construction of the artificial reef, it has been confirmed that the surrounding beach topography has stabilised and the sand beach has regenerated" (人工リーフの整備により、周辺海浜地形が安定化し、砂浜が再生していることが確認されている) [R9]. The same project's cost-benefit tables report an erosion-prevention area of 264 ha and a flood-protection area of 1,179 ha covering 12,089 households, with a very high benefit-cost ratio of 14.3 (¥2,136.6 billion of discounted benefit against ¥149.1 billion of cost) — though this B/C reflects Japan's standard avoided-damage cost-benefit methodology (present value of prevented flood/storm-surge losses), not an independent measurement of the reef's physical performance [R9]. At Niigata, the coastal-protection programme (offshore breakwaters plus artificial reefs since FY1977) is credited by a local civil-engineering society with achieving "beach recovery" for the protected central Niigata City shoreline [R3], while a documented 1989 academic study into reef subsidence at Ariake/Igarashi beach — title only accessed — indicates the programme has not been trouble-free everywhere [R15]. No damage events, storm failures, or removals were found in the sources gathered for this pass; this is a gap, not a confirmed clean record (unreferenced — needs source).

## Why it worked / why it failed

Engineering causes documented in this pass: crest width is the single design variable that defines the type (≥30–50 m marks the line between a narrow submerged breakwater and a true "artificial reef"), because a wide crest is what lets the structure dissipate wave energy across its width the way a natural reef flat does, rather than reflecting/concentrating it the way a narrow structure would [R6]. Crest depth (commonly 0–2 m, occasionally reported as deep as 4 m or as shallow as −0.5 m in different design tables) is a direct trade-off between wave-dissipation effectiveness (shallower is more effective) and the "preserve the sea view" aesthetic goal that partly motivates choosing a reef over an emergent breakwater in the first place [R2][R13]. Block mass/specification is treated in the Iburi Coast case as a live design parameter that was increased mid-project for stability, echoing the same "undersized armour units" failure mode that the Bar & Drimer (2023) survey found to be the single most commonly cited cause of failure among Western artificial surf reefs — suggesting the risk is not unique to the Western "surf reef" concept but is a generic geotechnical/coastal-engineering hazard for any submerged rubble structure [R9]; see also `Bar, D. & Drimer, N. (2023)`, Lior's file note [R17]. Seabed settlement (subsidence) is a documented risk at Niigata specifically, per the existence of the 1989 JSCE subsidence study, though the underlying mechanism (soft alluvial seabed under wave-cyclic loading is the standard explanation for this class of structure, but this specific causal claim was not found stated for Niigata in the sources gathered) is not confirmed in what was accessed (unreferenced — needs source).

## Unexpected results

Fishery impact and negotiation: the Iburi Coast national evaluation states that because the coast "is also actively used as a fishing ground," the responsible bureau held repeated consultations with fishery-industry stakeholders about the impact of the coastal protection facilities on fisheries and marine resources, and ran seabed-quality and fish/shellfish/seaweed habitat surveys both before and after construction of the artificial reef specifically, feeding the results back into the facility design [R9]. This indicates the reef's effect on local fisheries was treated as a live, negotiated issue rather than a settled positive, though the evaluation does not record whether the net effect on catch was positive or negative. Ecological colonisation as a stated design feature: newer Japanese reef designs explicitly favour natural stone over concrete caps specifically because it is "friendlier" for marine-organism attachment, which the source frames as an improvement, implying earlier concrete-block reefs were seen as ecologically inferior even though they served the same coastal-protection function [R6]. Subsidence, discussed above, appears to have been enough of a concern at Niigata to justify a dedicated academic monitoring study in 1989 [R15].

## What could have been done better

Authors'/experts' view: the Iburi Coast case shows the block specification for the artificial reef being revised upward mid-project specifically as a cost-and-stability optimisation, which — read alongside Bar & Drimer's (2023) finding that "too-small size" armour units are a leading cause of ASR failure worldwide — suggests Japan's own domestic practice has not been immune to the same initial under-sizing problem that has dogged Western artificial surf reefs, even though the purpose here is coastal protection rather than surfing [R9][R17]. The existence of a dedicated 1989 subsidence study for Niigata's reefs similarly suggests that at least one generation of built reefs there settled more than the design anticipated, an issue better addressed by geotechnical seabed investigation ahead of construction — though no source found in this pass states this recommendation explicitly (unreferenced — needs source). Surfers' view: not found. Japan's national artificial-reef programme is coastal-protection engineering, not a surf-amenity project, and no surfer commentary — Japanese or otherwise — specifically about Japan's own domestic reefs was located in this research pass; the closest material found was Japanese pro surfers Nao Omura and Emiri Nishimoto discussing an artificial reef at Palm Beach, Gold Coast, Australia, in the context of whether the approach could be applied in Japan, which is about a different country's reef, not Japan's own programme [R10].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Japan's central regulatory move — the 1999 Coastal Law shift from a single emergent seawall ("linear defense") to a submerged wide-crested reef plus a gently sloped seawall behind it ("areal defense"), explicitly justified in Fukui Prefecture's own coastal-engineering material as preserving the sea view — is directly relevant to any Haifa/Israel proposal that wants coastal protection without a visible offshore wall blocking the horizon [R1][R2]. The design threshold that turns a plain submerged breakwater into an "artificial reef" (crest width ≥30–50 m, crest depth commonly 0–2 m) is a concrete, transferable design rule rather than a vague concept [R6]. The Iburi Coast case's practice of running seabed and marine-life surveys before and after construction, and negotiating directly with the fishing industry, is a transferable governance lesson for any Mediterranean coast where fisheries stakeholders would also be affected [R9]. Tide range at the Japanese example sites and a rigorous wave-climate comparison to Haifa were not found in the sources gathered in this pass; Israel's Mediterranean coast is known (from other project files, not from this pass's sources) to be effectively microtidal, and Japan's Sea-of-Japan coast (where the Niigata and Fukui examples sit) is also commonly described as microtidal, but no source fetched in this research pass states either tide figure directly, so this comparison is flagged unreferenced — needs source rather than asserted as fact.
