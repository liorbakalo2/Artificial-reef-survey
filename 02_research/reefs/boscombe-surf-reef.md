## Motivation and who pushed for it

Boscombe was a rundown 20th-century seaside suburb of Bournemouth in need of regeneration; the reef was one plank of a wider council-led seafront renewal that also refurbished Boscombe Pier and built new beachfront "pods" and flats [R1][R10]. A local surf-shop figure, Paul Clark of the Bournemouth Surfing Centre, was an early booster, promising the reef would be "the nearest thing to an Atlantic roller this side of Cornwall" [R10]. Bournemouth Borough Council formally pursued it as tourism/economic-development infrastructure: a feasibility study was approved in 1999 and detailed plans approved in March 2003, with construction not starting until 2008 — a near-decade gestation [R6][R1]. Financing was structured opportunistically around unrelated seafront property sales (the car-park sale to Barratt Developments, and beach-pod sales) rather than a dedicated capital grant [R1][R4].

## Design as planned

The reef was designed as a submerged, angled ramp of stacked geotextile sand containers that would intercept the dominant swell direction and reshape it into rideable peeling waves, rather than "creating" waves from nothing. ASR director Shaw Mead described the mechanism directly: "The reef doesn't actually create waves. It's angled to catch the mean wave direction, and it acts as a ramp which changes the way the waves break" [R10]. The design target was a "Grade 5" wave (on ASR's own in-house surf-quality scale) under good swell conditions, producing rides of roughly 75 m to the right and 35 m to the left, suited to bodyboarders and surfers [R10]. Eleven specific design/performance objectives were set for the project, including materially increasing surf-day frequency (from ~77 to ~154 good surf days/yr) and beach-visit numbers (from ~5,000 to ~10,000/yr) [R1][R2].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

The structure that was eventually installed — 55 sand-filled geotextile bags forming a ~1-hectare submerged mound ~13,000 m³ in volume — matched the general geotube concept, but construction ran roughly a decade behind the original 1999–2003 planning timeline and cost more than double the original £1.4 million estimate, landing at £3–3.2 million for the reef alone [R1][R2][R6]. Construction itself was repeatedly delayed and beset by "setbacks during building works" before the reef finally opened in November 2009 [R1] (one NZ source claims the project was "over a year behind schedule" and puts total cost above NZD $6 million — a divergent figure not corroborated by UK sources; see Quick facts) [R8]. Shaw Mead, one of the two ASR designers, later blamed the outcome on "wrangling over funds, political in-fighting and bad project management decisions," explicitly distancing his own role — which he described as "design/surf science," not construction management — from those problems [R8].

## Outcome

**Surfing quality:** A University of Plymouth interim evaluation (Dr Mark Davidson, published May 2010) found the reef met only 4 of its 11 original design objectives. Davidson's report stated: "The wave length is rather too intense and challenging [and it] is not quite as consistent as it should be," and noted the waves produced were shorter than the design criteria required [R1][R2]. In practice the reef worked best around low tide, with rides largely limited to steep, short, "ankle-high" sections at the breaking point — powerful and hollow enough that it was mainly ridden by bodyboarders rather than the standup surfers it was marketed to, and it produced "plenty of broken boards" [R9-search][R1]. (Lior's own notes independently describe the same pattern: very heavy wave-breaking, ridden mainly by bodyboarders — his opinion, not an engineering finding [R13].)

**Coastal protection / ecology:** Not a stated primary objective and no measured coastal-protection or ecological outcome data were found in any source consulted — (unreferenced — needs source). A council ranger, Ben Wallbridge, noted dolphins were observed apparently investigating the new structure, framed as a curiosity rather than a measured ecological effect: "Dolphins are intelligent creatures and inquisitive by nature. They may well be playing in the new reef's surf, as it's something they haven't seen before" [R10].

**Longevity / damage / removal:** A routine inspection in March 2011 found "substantial changes" to the reef's profile; it was closed for safety on 31 March 2011 [R1]. In April 2011 the structure was found further damaged, with one geo-container removed and another repaired, apparently after an unidentified boat's propeller struck the reef [R1][R9]. ASR agreed to carry out repairs from early August 2011 but suspended the work for winter, and it was never satisfactorily completed [R1][R7]. ASR Ltd went into liquidation in September 2012 (one source: November 2012) with director Nick Behunin untraceable and the company owing the council roughly £14,800–£15,000 against a final withheld payment of £55,000 [R1][R4][R6]. The council obtained a £306,531 insurance settlement in 2013 [R1]. A 2012 plan to repurpose the site as a sunk-object dive/snorkel trail was itself abandoned; a council spokesperson said the plan's elements "were not taken forward for reasons including popularity, take-up and implementation" [R5]. The site was formally rebranded in April 2014 and again in 2017 as a "Coastal Activity Park" focused on diving, snorkelling, windsurfing/kitesurfing, volleyball, table tennis and gym facilities, with surfing dropped as a priority [R1][R6]. Debris from the decaying structure was reported washing up on the beach as late as Boxing Day 2017 [R8].

## Why it worked / why it failed

**Materials:** ASR's engineering approach relied on sand-filled geotextile bags rather than rock. Per Shaw Mead's own later analysis, this was a structural dead end: "the sandbags were an evolutionary dead-end" for durability in an active surf zone, and he subsequently moved ASR's later multi-purpose reef designs to traditional rock construction [R8]. The core failure mechanism he described is systemic to the fabric-bag approach: "as soon as even a single sand-filled container broke and lost sand, the whole structure changed shape due to the fluid nature of sand," and because wave-breaking depends on precise seabed geometry, any shape change degraded surf quality even before outright structural failure [R9].

**Proximate damage cause:** The specific event cited for Boscombe's structural failure is a boat-propeller strike, which is also the mechanism Lior's own notes independently point to (Lior frames it as "apparently" a boat propeller, i.e. not confirmed with certainty) [R9][R1][R13]. Geosynthetics Magazine's survey of ASR's projects groups Boscombe's cause ("propeller strike") alongside other ASR-reef damage mechanisms elsewhere — anchor damage at Gold Coast, debris strikes at Kovalam, over-filling during construction at Mount Maunganui — as evidence of a general pattern of geotextile-reef vulnerability to point-impacts [R9].

**Design/engineering shortfall independent of damage:** Even before the propeller strike, the University of Plymouth's Davidson evaluation had already found the reef under-delivering against its own design brief — meeting only 4 of 11 objectives, with wave length "too intense" and inconsistent — indicating the wave-shaping design itself, not only later physical damage, fell short [R1][R2].

**Project management / funding:** Shaw Mead's own explanation for the broader project's failure emphasises non-engineering causes — "wrangling over funds, political in-fighting and bad project management decisions" during a near-decade gap between 2003 planning approval and 2008 construction start — rather than a single technical flaw [R8][R6][R1].

**Sharp seabed transition (Lior's hypothesis, unconfirmed by any engineering source found):** Lior's own notes propose, as his personal inference and explicitly labelled as such ("לדעתי" / "in my opinion"), that both the Ashkelon (Israel) geotube failure and Boscombe shared a common root cause: an overly sharp transition in seabed depth combined with the inherent fragility of geotextile containers under storm wave-breaking. No source found in this research independently corroborates a sharp-depth-transition mechanism specifically for Boscombe; it remains Lior's opinion, not a documented engineering finding [R13].

## Unexpected results

- **Media reach and short-term tourism boost:** The project drew media coverage in an estimated 60 countries around its 2009 opening, and local Boscombe hospitality businesses reported a real economic uplift regardless of the reef's technical shortcomings — Katie Sutton of the Urban Reef restaurant acknowledged the expense but noted tourism benefits to local restaurants, hotels and shops [R2].
- **Inflated public expectations vs. delivered wave:** Some media coverage carried headlines like "Pipeline Comes To Bournemouth," setting expectations the reef's actual short, steep, low-tide-only wave could not meet, which likely sharpened the public perception of failure beyond the underlying 4-of-11 objectives shortfall [R9-search].
- **Safety concerns beyond wave quality:** The site was closed in 2011 over safety concerns, described in press coverage as involving hazardous undercurrents and dangerous gaps that had opened up in the deteriorating structure — sufficiently serious that local surfer/photographer Chris Skone-Roberts warned "it is just a question of time before someone dies" [R2]. Skone-Roberts separately described the failed structure itself, after a competition, as looking like "a stranded, dead, stinking whale" [R8].
- **Persistent physical litter/debris legacy:** Loose material and debris from the decaying geotextile bags continued washing ashore for years after closure, documented as late as Boxing Day 2017, six years after the reef's last operational season [R8].
- **Wildlife curiosity:** Dolphins were reported investigating the new structure shortly after construction, an unplanned/unmeasured ecological footnote rather than a designed outcome [R10].

## What could have been done better

**Authors'/experts' view:** Shaw Mead (ASR co-designer), reflecting after the fact, concluded the material choice itself was wrong — sand-filled geotextile bags are, in his words, "an evolutionary dead-end" for a structure that must survive years of surf-zone wave energy and occasional impacts, and he moved ASR's subsequent multi-purpose-reef designs to rock construction instead [R8]. Geosynthetics Magazine's broader review implies the fix is structural redundancy/robustness: a single ruptured bag should not be able to reshape (and thus degrade) the whole reef, which points toward either more failure-tolerant bag engineering or abandoning fabric containers for point-impact-prone sites like a boat channel [R9]. The University of Plymouth's own evaluation (Davidson, 2010) implies the wave-shaping geometry needed further hydrodynamic refinement even independent of material failure, since only 4 of 11 objectives were met while the structure was still intact [R1][R2].

**Surfers'/locals' view:** Local critics, including councillor Ben Grower, framed the entire project as a governance and money failure rather than a fixable engineering detail — calling it "a complete waste of money" and, on the 2012 rescue bid's collapse, "I'm not surprised in the slightest... the plan was always a load of rubbish" [R2][R5]. Surfers' practical complaints centred on the wave itself being too short, too tide-dependent (rideable mainly around low tide) and too hollow/steep for the broad recreational-surfer audience the project was marketed to, better suited to bodyboarders than standup surfers [R9-search][R1][R13].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Bournemouth/Boscombe's tidal range is modest by UK Atlantic-coast standards — a same-day check of Bournemouth tide predictions showed high tides around 1.95–2.05 m and lows around 0.97–1.07 m, i.e. an observed range on that check of roughly 0.9–1.1 m (Poole/Christchurch Bay's range is locally reduced by the Isle of Wight "double tide" effect); this is not a full spring/neap statistical range and should be treated as illustrative only [R12]. By contrast, the Mediterranean coast at Haifa is effectively micro-tidal; no sourced numeric tidal-range figure for Haifa was found in this research pass (searched isramar.ocean.org.il and general tide-data sites without success) — (unreferenced — needs source; flagged as an open gap in the project's own master list, which independently notes Israel tidal-range data is missing from all of Lior's source files) [R14]. What does transfer directly: Boscombe's central lesson is that geotextile-sandbag construction is vulnerable to point-impact damage (boat strikes, anchors, debris) and to cascading shape-change once any single bag ruptures, regardless of tidal regime — a materials-and-impact-exposure lesson, not a tide-driven one [R8][R9]. Haifa's proposed reef site is assessed against real Shikmona-buoy wave data (significant wave height 0.45–0.8 m in the surfable window, occasional peaks to ~1.2 m) which is a much smaller and gentler wave-energy environment than the UK Channel/Atlantic-influenced south coast that shaped Boscombe's much larger design wave — meaning Haifa's structure would face less storm-wave loading than Boscombe did, but also underscoring that any Israeli project should still plan explicitly for boat-strike/anchor exposure in a working coastal-shipping and fishing environment, independent of tide range [R14].
