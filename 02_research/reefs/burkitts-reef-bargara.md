## Motivation and who pushed for it

The push came entirely from one local surfer, Greg Redgard, a member of the Bargara Boardriders Association, not from council, tourism bodies, or a coastal-erosion program [R1][R3][R6][R8]. Redgard's own account: the point break was "unrideable" because "you'd take off, go for about 2 or 3 metres and you would just run into rocks" [R1]. He first applied to the relevant authority in 1982; his first application was rejected, and it took "more than a decade" of persistence plus community lobbying before the Beach Protection Authority of Queensland and the Burnett Shire Council approved the work, with formal approval landing in 1997 [R1][R4][R6][R8]. There is no evidence in any source found of a broader political, tourism-marketing, or erosion-mitigation agenda — this is explicitly framed everywhere as a grassroots, surfer-driven DIY project, the rarity that makes it notable [R1][R3][R4].

## Design as planned

There was no formal engineering design, wave model, or scale test — Redgard designed and built it by instinct, telling ABC "Not rocket science, really, when you think about it" [R5][R6]. The stated plan was simple: break down/relocate the boulders (and discarded railway-track debris) blocking the takeoff zone so a wave could "break over it," turning a hazard-strewn stretch into a rideable right-hand point break [R1][R5][R8]. No modelled promises about wave height, beach width, or ecological effect were made or found in any source — this stands in explicit contrast to modelled multi-purpose reef projects like Narrowneck or Palm Beach [R3][R4].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Because there was no formal design, there is little "planned vs. built" gap reported — sources broadly agree the construction (completed February 1997) matched Redgard's simple intent, though the duration is reported two ways (R4/R8: two days / ~8 hours of excavator work; R1: the whole job finished "within weeks" of approval — see Year-built note): a large Kobelco hydraulic excavator worked at low tide (exploiting the ~2.5 m local tide range) to break down and reposition at least 12 large boulders, clearing a deep gutter that had previously split the wave into two disconnected sections, and gaps were filled partly by hand — reportedly including carrying bricks out on a surfboard [R1][R4][R6][R8]. One source (via the ABC "Bright Side" radio feature) adds that some of the large rocks removed from the takeoff zone were relocated to the shoreline rather than discarded, where locals still use them for fishing [R5]. No cost overruns, phasing, or construction problems are reported in any source — likely because the job was small, self-funded, and completed in a single short window.

## Outcome

- **Surfing quality (with numbers):** Before the works, the point broke only "a handful of days" per year; afterwards, surfable days rose to roughly **30 days a year**, with overhead (4+ ft) conditions on the order of **4 days a year** [R4][R6][R8] — this figure (attributed to SurfingRamps.com.au researcher Andrew Pitt) appears consistently across the Raised Water Research spot page and an independent Swellnet forum comment [R4][R8]. Wave face height is reported at 2–6 ft (0.5–2 m), period 7–10 seconds, described as "a consistent peeling face that occasionally barrels," with "very rare" barrel sections [R3][R4]. Best swell window: November–April [R1].
- **Coastal protection:** Not a stated goal and no coastal-protection outcome data found — **not found**.
- **Ecology:** Burkitts Reef (the same headland reef) is today also a popular scuba/shore-dive site, described as covered in "coral gardens and small bommies," with nudibranchs, crayfish, moray eels, sea snakes, tasselled wobbegongs and unspecified "schools of pelagic fish" [R10]. (An earlier draft of this dossier named the pelagic fish as "trevally and barracuda" — checked against the source and corrected: the source's only named fish species, "golden trevally," belongs to a different Bundaberg dive site on the same page, Hoffman's Rocks, not Burkitts Reef, and "barracuda" is not mentioned anywhere on the page.) No source directly measures how the 1997 boulder-moving affected this ecology one way or the other — this is reported as the reef's general character, not as a documented before/after study. Flagged as a gap: **no ecological impact study found**.
- **Longevity:** The modification has held for nearly three decades with no reported maintenance, storm damage, or re-work found in any source — it is presented in the 2024 ABC feature as unchanged and still functioning as originally built [R1][R5].
- **Damage events / removal:** None reported — **not found**.

## Why it worked / why it failed

By the consensus of every source found, this is treated as a rare unambiguous success among artificial/enhanced surf reefs, and the reasons given are:
- **Working with an existing natural reef, not building one from scratch** — Redgard reshaped basalt rock already there rather than importing new structure (unlike sandbag/geotextile reefs such as Opunake, Kovalam or Boscombe, which had to create a totally new bathymetric feature from imported material) [R3][R4].
- **Simple, low-risk intervention** — removing hazards and gap-filling rather than attempting to engineer a specific wave shape, so there was little to go wrong structurally (contrast with geotextile-bag reefs whose fabric tore, shifted, or sank) [R3][R4].
- **A large natural tide range (~2.5 m)** made the boulders workable at low tide with ordinary excavation equipment, without needing marine plant or diving contractors [R4].
- **Rock/boulder material is inherently durable** compared with the sand-filled geotextile bags used at most other reefs of the era, which are vulnerable to UV degradation, vandalism, and wave-tearing [R3] (general pattern noted across the wider reef literature, not stated specifically for Burkitts, but consistent with its near-30-year survival without reported repair).

No source in this search reports a failure mode, so there is no "why it failed" content to add for this case — flagged explicitly rather than invented.

## Unexpected results

- **Fishing use:** boulders cleared from the takeoff zone were relocated to the shoreline, where they are now used by locals as a fishing spot [R5] — an unplanned secondary benefit.
- **Media/tourism attention:** the project was picked up by TIME magazine as (per ABC) the "world's first surf enhancement project," and resurfaced in Australian national media (ABC News, ABC Radio's "The Bright Side") decades later, in January 2024, as a good-news/DIY-hero story [R1][R5][R6].
- **Dual-use with diving:** the same reef is independently marketed as a scuba/snorkel attraction ("Burkitts Reef Dive Site") by tourism operators, unrelated to its surf reputation — an example of one enhanced natural reef serving two recreational audiences [R10][R13].
- **Family/legacy angle:** Redgard's daughter Tahlija, who grew up surfing the reef, became a professional surfer and credits it directly: "He took Burkitts Reef, redefined what was possible and made what was once unrideable an absolute playground" [R1].
- No litigation, safety incidents, localism/crowding conflict, or negative unexpected consequence was found in any source searched — **not found**.

## What could have been done better

- **Experts'/engineering-literature view:** None of the sources reviewed (including the Raised Water Research and ICM coastal-management comparison pages, which do critique other reefs' bag sizes, crest depths, and funding models) offer a specific critique of Burkitts Reef's execution — it is consistently used as the positive contrast case, not a subject of critique [R3][R4]. Jackson and Corbett's 2007 "Review of Existing Multi-Functional Artificial Reefs" is cited by secondary sources as having assessed it among other reefs, but this dossier could not fetch that paper directly (access blocked), so no first-hand engineering critique is reported here — **flagged, not invented**.
- **Surfers' view:** No critical surfer commentary was found. The one detailed surfer/forum contribution retrieved (Swellnet user "truebluebasher") is descriptive/historical, not critical, and frames Burkitts as a rare successful DIY case in explicit contrast to failed professionally-designed reefs elsewhere [R8]. ## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

- **Tide range:** Bargara's ~2.5 m tide swing [R4] is far larger than the Israeli Mediterranean coast's tide range, which is well under 0.5 m (micro-tidal, not sourced in this dossier's reference set — flagged: **not found** in the sources fetched here; cross-check against other Israel-focused dossiers in `02_research/israel/`). This matters because Burkitts' construction method — exploiting low tide to work boulders with a land-based excavator — assumes tidal exposure of the work zone that a Haifa-area (near-tideless) site would not get; any similar reshaping on the Israeli coast would likely require different plant (barge/crane, diver-assisted work) rather than simple low-tide excavator access.
- **Wave climate:** Bargara faces the Coral Sea/Pacific swell window with a defined swell season (Nov–Apr) [R1] and produces a 2–6 ft, 7–10 s point-break wave [R3]. The Israeli Mediterranean coast is generally smaller and more wind-swell dominated with shorter period — the specific wave-height/period figures are not established in this dossier's source set (**not found** here) and should be pulled from Israel-specific dossiers or the project's wave-climate notes for a real comparison.
- **What transfers:** The core transferable lesson is procedural/economic rather than technical — a **modest, self-funded, reshape-what's-already-there** approach (relocating existing boulders rather than importing new engineered material) achieved a multi-decade, apparently maintenance-free improvement for roughly AUD $10,000, in contrast to the far more expensive, professionally engineered geotextile-bag reefs elsewhere that have frequently failed or required costly renewal (e.g., Narrowneck's ~$2M renewal, Opunake's budget blowout, Boscombe's £3.2M failure — per the project's other reef dossiers, not directly re-verified here). For any Haifa/Israel natural reef or rock outcrop with existing hazards, a similarly minimal "clear/reshape the hazard, don't rebuild the seabed" approach is the direct analogy worth testing, if geology and permitting allow — this is a judgement call, not sourced from any Israel-specific document.
