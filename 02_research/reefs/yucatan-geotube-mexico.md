## Motivation and who pushed for it

Yucatán's erosion problem is described (by the field project's own engineers) as spanning roughly 300 km of coast, with about 60 km facing infrastructure risk; drivers cited include the **Progreso pier's disruption of longshore sediment transport** and **unauthorized, empirically-built groins put up by individual property owners**, both of which worsened erosion for neighbours [R4]. Mexico's federal government (via the Yucatán office of the environment ministry) launched a **Beach Rehabilitation Program in 2001**, which became urgent as "the situation became critical" in 2002, and commissioned the private engineering firm Axis Ingeniería to design a soft, removable solution — geotextile tubes — rather than hard rock structures [R2][R4]. This is a government/engineering-driven project, not one initiated by surfers or a tourism operator specifically.

The 2025 Progreso installation is a **municipal government initiative** (Ayuntamiento de Progreso) commissioning a local contractor (Constructora Pech) to protect the malecón's tourist-facing eastern stretch, including the accessible "Playa Incluyente" beach and the landmark tourist "letras" sign — i.e., a tourism/public-infrastructure motivation this time at city rather than federal level [R5][R9].

## Design as planned

The master-list seed citation (González Leija, Chávez, Álvarez, Mendoza & Silva, 2014) is explicitly framed as an **"Experimental Study"**: a physical (lab) model at UNAM's Coastal Engineering Laboratory, using a 37 × 0.8 × 1.2 m wave flume, tube models built at **1:5 and 1:10 scale**, and twelve capacitance wave gauges recording surface elevation to derive transmission (Kt) and reflection (Kr) coefficients [R1]. The design intent tested was for the tubes to act as **low-crested, low-permeability submerged breakwaters**: dissipate incoming wave energy through controlled breaking over the tube crest, let sand settle on the shoreward side, and avoid fully blocking longshore drift — matching the field tubes' stated dual role as "Dune Reinforcement" (protecting/restoring dunes already destroyed by wave action) and as "submerged breakwaters" (mitigating wave energy to recover eroded beach) [R1][R2].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

The 2005 field tubes were built by pumping a sand-water slurry (10–30% sediment concentration) into geotextile sleeves at 1,000 gallons/minute until they reached about 70% of their cross-sectional capacity, joined in 2 m segments into a continuous 4 km run roughly 15–30 m offshore [R2][R3]. This differs from a single "ideal" engineered cross-section: the fill ratio and pumping method are field-construction compromises, and the tubes' final crest height/submergence relative to local wave height (the parameter the 2014 lab study found to be the make-or-break design variable) is not confirmed to have been engineered against that later research, since the lab paper post-dates the field build by roughly a decade [R1][R2]. Durability concerns were flagged directly in the field-monitoring literature: **UV exposure degrading geotextile tensile strength**, and **abrasion from littoral drift and wave attack reducing seam strength** over time [R3].

The 2025 Progreso project is architecturally different again: instead of one continuous multi-kilometre run, it uses **seven discrete, shorter tubes (6–10 m each)** — a smaller, modular, and reportedly faster-to-place design (~1.5 months, ~10 workers) [R5][R8][R9]. Press coverage frames this explicitly as a **second attempt** in the same stretch of coast, implying the first round(s) did not durably solve the problem [R5].

## Outcome

- **Sediment accretion (2005 project, short/medium term):** measured shoreline accretion of 0.45–0.90 m at monitored cross-shore points, with ~70% of total accretion occurring in the first six months post-installation [R3].
- **Longer-term claim (project designers' own 2012 report):** the associated Progreso Beach 7 km nourishment was "stable for about 10 years, even without maintenance works" [R4] — note this is a claim by the same firm (Axis Ingeniería) that designed the project, not an independent audit.
- **Wider regional picture (2025 journalism):** effectiveness across Yucatán's various geotube projects (including CICY/Conacyt-linked ones) is described as **"dispares" (mixed/disputed)**, generating "a scientific debate about its utility, its impact, and finally whether or not its use should continue"; some projects recovered only 5–8 m of beach in a year; erosion is reported as continuing to affect marine fauna and tourism as of mid-2025 [R6].
- **Longevity/damage:** press reports note geotextile material "can deteriorate, collapse, or fracture in under 12 months" in some deployments [R5][R9] — no specific storm-damage event for the Yucatán tubes (comparable to Ashkelon's 15/48-tube construction failure or Boscombe's propeller strike, both documented for other sites in this project's Israel/UK dossiers) was found in the fetched sources.
- **Surf quality:** not applicable / not found — none of the fetched sources describe this as a surf-enhancement structure; its stated purpose throughout is beach/dune protection, not wave quality for surfing.
- **Removal:** no removal event found; the site instead saw a **second, separate installation round in 2025**, which implies at least partial degradation or ineffectiveness of the earlier work rather than formal decommissioning [R5].

## Why it worked / why it failed

- **Crest height vs. wave height (engineering cause, per the seed lab study):** the 2014 flume experiments found transmission performance is governed by the ratio of freeboard to design wave height; optimal freeboard should not exceed twice the design wave height. Below that, violent wave-breaking over the crest dissipates energy as intended; if the crest is too high relative to the wave, the wave fails to break and transmission coefficient can exceed 1 — i.e., the structure can be **worse than no structure at all** [R1].
- **Material durability:** UV degradation of exposed geotextile fabric and seam-strength loss from littoral-drift abrasion and wave attack were identified as specific failure mechanisms in the field-monitoring literature [R3]; 2025 press repeats a general industry pattern of tube fabric deteriorating or collapsing within 6–12 months [R5][R9].
- **Underlying erosion drivers not addressed by tubes alone:** the 2012 engineering report attributes Yucatán's erosion fundamentally to the **Progreso pier blocking longshore sediment transport** and to **unauthorized private groins** — structural interventions that a beach-parallel geotube line does not by itself fix [R4]. 2025 journalism echoes this: authorities' repeated artificial-fill/geotube efforts "have not succeeded," partly because such structures "interrupt sediment transit and generate accumulation on one side and erosion on the other," with structures built to retain sand in one zone documented as having stripped sand from adjacent beaches [R6].
- **Funding/maintenance:** the one long-duration success claim in the record (10-year stability) is explicitly credited to a nourishment project working "even without maintenance" [R4] — implying that where maintenance is not sustained, degradation (as seen in the 2025 need for a fresh install) is the more typical outcome.

## Unexpected results

- **Warned but unmeasured side effects:** 2025 press coverage cites unnamed experts ("profesionales") raising concern that geotubes could disrupt ocean currents and harm marine flora/fauna and neighbouring coastlines — framed as a warning/debate point rather than a measured outcome at this specific site [R6][R9].
- **Cross-shore erosion transfer:** general reporting on Yucatán geotube/fill efforts states that structures built to retain sand in one zone have been observed to strip sand from adjacent beaches — an unintended down-drift consequence rather than a benefit [R6].
- **Adjacent-project "bonus" reef/tourism use (different site, same contractor's portfolio):** at Playa Bonita, Lerma, Campeche (not Yucatán, but the same contractor, Constructora Pech, working with Dutch supplier "Tecante"), a 200 m geotube breakwater was explicitly designed with a **secondary function as an artificial reef**, with Mexico's National Anthropology Institute (INAH) planning to install shipwreck artefacts there to promote diving tourism [R7]. This is flagged as a *different location*, included only because it shows the same regional contractor treating geotube breakwaters as dual-purpose (protection + reef/tourism) elsewhere in the Gulf.
- No fishing-pressure, rip-current, or litigation outcomes specific to the Yucatán geotube sites were found in the fetched sources (not found).

## What could have been done better

**Authors'/experts' view (from the fetched engineering and press sources):**
- The 2014 lab study's own conclusion — that transmission performance hinges tightly on the freeboard-to-wave-height ratio — implies that field installations built without that design rule (i.e., most real-world tubes, since the rule was only published in 2014, years after the 2005 field build) risk being mis-sized for local wave conditions [R1].
- 2025 press frames geotubes generically as a **"limited solution"** that needs to be paired with a comprehensive strategy addressing root causes (blocked sediment transport from the pier and illegal groins) rather than repeated stand-alone tube installations [R6][R4].
- Durability fixes flagged: better UV-resistant fabric and seam protection against littoral abrasion were identified as specific technical gaps in the field-monitoring record [R3].

**Surfers' view:** not found — this is not documented anywhere as a surf-enhancement site, and no surfer commentary was located in the fetched sources.

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

- **Tide range / wave climate parallel:** the Yucatán coast is explicitly described (in the seed lab paper) as having "a microtidal regime and low energy, short waves (Hs < 1 m, T = 4–6 s)" [R1] — a wave-energy and tidal regime broadly comparable in scale to the Mediterranean/Israeli coast's own low-tide, moderate-swell environment that this project's other dossiers treat as central to Israel's site conditions (see project master-list open item on Israel tidal range, not independently re-sourced in this dossier).
- **Design-rule transfer:** the 2014 study's core finding — that a submerged breakwater's crest must sit within roughly 2× the design wave height of the surface, or the structure either breaks violently (energy dissipated, as intended) or fails to break at all (transmission coefficient can exceed 1, making the structure counterproductive) [R1] — is directly transferable to any Israeli geotube design discussion, including Lior's own hypothesis (in his personal notes) that the Ashkelon geotube project failed in part because the seabed transitioned sharply from 4–5 m to 2–3 m depth at the tube line, producing violent storm-wave breaking that broke the tubes apart [R12]. The Yucatán lab result gives an independent, peer-reviewed engineering mechanism (crest-to-wave-height ratio) that is consistent with — and could help formalize — Lior's own inference about Ashkelon.
- **Durability/maintenance transfer:** Yucatán's documented failure modes — UV degradation, seam abrasion, and material collapse within 6–12 months in some deployments [R3][R5][R9] — are a relevant cautionary data point for any Israeli project also considering geotextile (rather than rock) construction, alongside Ashkelon's own higher-than-expected maintenance costs (per Lior's notes) [R12].
- **Governance transfer:** Yucatán's repeated, contested geotube rounds (2005, and again 2025) without published cost or independent audit data [R2][R5][R8][R9] is a caution for Israeli municipal planners about the value of transparent cost/outcome reporting from the start.
