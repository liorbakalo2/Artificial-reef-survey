## Motivation and who pushed for it

Narrowneck's erosion problem predates the reef by decades. A 1971 report by Delft University of Technology (Netherlands), commissioned by the Queensland Government, recommended building a groyne at Narrowneck to stop erosion of the isthmus [R9][R13]. Training walls built at the Spit in 1985 helped erosion further north but had no effect at Narrowneck itself, and Cyclone Nancy (1990) eroded the beach back to the boulder wall protecting the Nerang River [R2]. In response, Gold Coast City Council — rather than build the conventional groyne Delft had recommended — chose an artificial reef, branding it a "Multi-Purpose Reef" (MPR) specifically so that "surfing amenity" could be folded into the PR case for the project in a surf-centric city [R2][R9][R13]. Surfers on the northern Gold Coast were reportedly "rejoicing" at the 1999 announcement, expecting a genuine new wave [R2]. There is no evidence in the sources found of state/federal political involvement or of a single named political champion; the driving actor throughout is Gold Coast City Council as an institution, with Mayor Tom Tate later becoming the public face of the 2017–2018 renewal [R10].

## Design as planned

The reef was designed by Kerry Black (University of Waikato professor) and Shaw Mead, using refraction-diffraction (WBEND) and Boussinesq wave modelling, checked against a 1:50-scale physical model built at the University of New South Wales Water Research Laboratory [R5][R11]. The stated design targets were: widen the beach by 20–30 m in the reef's lee, and produce surfable waves under a defined set of conditions [R5 search-derived]. A 2007 performance report (cited by Swellnet) stated the reef needed "long period, clean swell" to replicate the modelled wave shape, and that "larger swells, lower tides, and offshore winds have the potential to produce hollow, plunging breakers" [R2] — i.e. the promised surf was explicitly conditional, not an every-swell wave. The original design was a split "V" shape intended to allow both left- and right-hand rides with a paddling channel between the two arms [R3][R9].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

The most consequential change was a **late, last-minute decision by the council to lower the reef's crest by 1 metre before construction** — "the change would have no effect on sand accretion but was terminal to Narrowneck becoming a good surfing reef," in Swellnet's assessment [R2]. This single change is identified by an independent source as the reason the wave underperformed relative to what was promised, even though the coastal-protection function was unaffected [R2]. Beyond that, the built reef otherwise followed the ~400-geotextile-container, ~70,000 m³ design, placed via split-hull barge with GPS [R1][R3][R9]. By ~2011, an underwater survey found several containers missing or damaged and the seaward containers buried by sand [R4 (Jackson et al. 2012, via ICCE proceedings page)]. By 2017, the bags had "settled further into the sand" over 17 years, and the reef had become noticeably deeper than as-built [R2]. Gold Coast City Council's designer/co-designer, Shaw Mead, later attributed geotextile-bag failures across all his firm's projects worldwide to a common cause: "The containers failed due to a number of reasons including anchors (Gold Coast), debris strikes (Kovalam), during construction with over-filling (Mount Reef), propellor strike (Boscombe)" — i.e. at Narrowneck specifically, boat anchors were the identified failure mechanism [R11].

## Outcome

**Coastal protection:** Judged successful by the council and by most independent sources. An 11-year monitoring study (Jackson et al., cited via the ICCE Coastal Engineering Proceedings) found that erosion from major 2009 storm events "was accommodated within the wider beach created in 1999," with gradual post-storm recovery in the reef's lee producing a mild groyne effect [R4]. A 2019 City statement to Swellnet described continued sand-volume increases, though specifically in **offshore sandbars rather than the upper beach** — contradicting earlier (2010-era) reports of a "distinct" and "consistent" salient (beach-width increase) of 30–50 m, which Google Earth historical imagery does not corroborate as ever having appeared [R14]. One source (Wikipedia's *Multi-purpose reef* article, citing Turner 2006) gives an independently monitored beach-widening figure of **~20 m wider** in the reef's shadow versus unaffected beach [R1] — this is lower than, and not fully consistent with, the 30–50 m figure reported elsewhere [R14]; both are given per the reference rule.

**Surfing:** Consistently described as underperforming relative to expectations. "So far the reef has worked well as a coastal control point, but has been disappointing in its secondary objective to improve surfing" [R9]. Swellnet's own webcam and local anecdotal reports both show "surfers seldom surf the reef," a pattern that persisted even after the 2017–2018 renewal [R14]. The City's own survey data claims "a significant increase in the frequency of wave breaking on the reef" post-renewal, a claim Swellnet notes is not supported by camera observations or local reports [R14]. An earlier study (cited by ecoshape.org) reported the reef broke waves "roughly 50% of the time" with faces of 0.7–2.0 m, average ride length 150–200 m, average ride time 30 s (max 60 s), described qualitatively as "fun, but not hollow, in average conditions" [R5].

**Ecology:** Widely reported as an unplanned success. The reef became a popular fishing, diving, and snorkelling site, with macro-algae, fish, and reported turtle activity establishing on the geotextile substrate [R1][R3][R8][R9]. The 2011 survey found "high abundance and biodiversity" despite the sand-burial and container damage noted above [R4].

**Longevity / damage events:** The original bags degraded over ~17 years (1999–2017): "shifted, settled into the sand, or split, often due to anchor strike" [R14]. Cyclone-driven erosion recurred at least twice: 2009 storms [R4] and Cyclone Oma in February 2019, described by a Swellnet commenter as the "worst erosion at Narrowneck... worst in 40 yrs" [R14, comment]. A June 2018 renewal added new mega sand containers on top of the existing footprint to raise the crest back up, following remedial works begun in 2017 [R2][R9][R14]. As of the reef's most recent photograph found (April 2025, in the aftermath of Tropical Cyclone Alfred), the reef and adjacent beach remain a subject of active coastal-management debate [R12].

## Why it worked / why it failed

**Coastal protection worked** primarily because the reef's core hydraulic function — dissipating wave energy offshore and trapping sediment in the nearshore sandbar system — does not depend on precise crest depth or bag integrity to the same degree that surf-wave shaping does; even a degraded, buried, or partially damaged reef still perturbs the sand-transport regime [R4][R14].

**Surfing underperformed** for a documented, specific engineering reason: the council's last-minute 1-metre crest-lowering decision, made for reasons unrelated to surf quality, put the structure too deep for consistent wave-breaking under normal (not just exceptional) swell/tide/wind combinations [R2]. Compounding this, geotextile bags are inherently fragile compared to rock: Shaw Mead (the reef's original co-designer) stated that because a surf break's wave shape depends on the exact shape of the seabed, losing even a single sand-filled container changes that shape and degrades wave quality — and that Gold Coast's containers specifically failed due to **boat anchors** [R11]. Mead's broader verdict, from direct correspondence quoted by The Inertia: he "no longer believes in the viability of geotextile bags for artificial reef construction," having conducted "a critical review of the performance of sand-filled containers for MPR construction approximately a decade ago, which led to the change to traditional rock construction methods of the most recent MPRs built" [R11] — i.e. the industry, including Narrowneck's own designer, moved away from the exact material Narrowneck was built from.

## Unexpected results

- **Ecological colonisation and the resulting fishing/anchor-damage cycle**: the reef's unplanned success as a fish habitat drew recreational fishing traffic, whose boat anchors are identified as the specific mechanism that tore the geotextile containers [R11] — an ecological win directly causing the structural failure that required the 2017–2018 renewal.
- **"Moving the goalposts" on beach-widening claims**: early (2010-era) monitoring reports described a clear 30–50 m salient; by 2019 the City's official position had shifted to say a salient was "never expected" and that sand build-up was always meant to be offshore — a shift multiple Swellnet commenters (see Reviews, below) flagged as inconsistent with what residents were originally told [R14].
- **A 2026 political sequel**: 27 years after Narrowneck was built to avoid a conventional groyne, the Gold Coast mayor proposed fast-tracking a new rockwall/groyne immediately adjacent to it — prompting exactly the kind of updrift/downdrift erosion-displacement objections coastal engineers raise about hard groynes generally [R12].
- **Depth measurement uncertainty**: the City's post-renewal survey data claims no settlement of the new containers, directly contradicting "anecdotal" reports from locals that the new bags were already breaking less often and appeared to be settling — an unresolved discrepancy between official monitoring and lived local experience [R14].

## What could have been done better

**Experts'/engineers' view:** Shaw Mead, the reef's original designer, has publicly stated (in a critical review he conducted roughly a decade after Narrowneck was built) that sand-filled geotextile containers should not be used for multi-purpose reef construction at all, and that the industry has since moved to rock construction for this reason [R11]. Separately, Swellnet's own 2017 reporting singles out the crest-lowering decision as the single design change most responsible for the surf underperformance, implying a purely surf-focused (rather than protection-focused) crest depth would have produced a better wave without necessarily compromising protection [R2].

**Surfers'/locals' view:** Commenters on Swellnet's coverage were often blunter. One (handle "warddy," 2019) wrote: "Absolute waste of money ... When will these clowns realise just drop some rocks in a triangular shape and whamo pick a peak and dissipate the sand surge at the same time plus create a fisherie" [R14]. Another ("Fish Face," replying) added sarcastically that a simpler rock design "would be too easy and wouldn't justify the jobs of the 'experts' who created this abomination" [R14]. A long-time local ("the-spleen_2") noted that "beach widening" had been "the core of the project" as originally pitched by Griffith University, and found it "funny to see how the idea has changed over time" toward the offshore-sandbar framing — i.e. locals' core critique is less about engineering detail and more about a perceived retroactive redefinition of what "success" means [R14].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Narrowneck's central lesson for a Haifa or other Israeli Mediterranean project is the **crest-depth-versus-tide-range trade-off**: the reef's surf underperformance is traced by an independent source specifically to a council decision to change crest depth relative to the tide datum, made for reasons unrelated to surfing, which then dominated wave-breaking outcomes [R2]. The Gold Coast has a meaningful astronomical tide range (the reef's crest was explicitly set "1.5 m below the low tide waterline," implying a tide range on the order of a metre or more) [R2][R3], which is the kind of variable a designer must actively engineer around — a fixed crest depth performs differently at high tide than at low tide. The Mediterranean coast at Haifa is known qualitatively (per Lior's own notes and outreach material) to have a much smaller tidal range, which Lior's own draft outreach email (in a Hebrew-language slide deck) argues is an *advantage* for reef stability and maintenance ("תנאי הגאות והשפל כאן מאפשרים יציבות גבוהה ותחזוקה טבעית לאורך זמן" — "the tide conditions here allow high stability and natural long-term maintenance") [R18] — this is Lior's own opinion/hypothesis, not an independently sourced engineering conclusion, and no numeric Haifa or Mediterranean tide-range figure was found in the sources fetched for this dossier (unreferenced — needs source). If Lior's premise is correct, a Haifa reef would face a smaller version of Narrowneck's tide-datum problem, making crest-depth choice a one-time design decision rather than a chronic multi-condition compromise. Separately, Narrowneck's identified failure mechanism — boat anchors tearing geotextile bags that had become a popular fishing spot [R11] — is directly transferable: any Israeli geotextile-based reef that succeeds ecologically should expect the same anchor-damage risk and may want to restrict anchoring or use more anchor-resistant materials (e.g. rock, per Mead's own post-Narrowneck design shift [R11]).
