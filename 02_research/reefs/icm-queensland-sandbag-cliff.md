Both threads are documented separately below and the mismatch is not resolved.

## Motivation and who pushed for it

**Thread A (ICM transcript, unnamed site).** In the ICM conference talk that is the sole source for this thread, the presenter describes a clay-based sea cliff that was actively eroding and falling into the sea; ICM built a wall along its base to stop the erosion. The presenter frames the material choice — 5-ton sandbags rather than rock — as a direct response to how the public actually uses the site: "it's a popular community beach where people like to sit and walk on the structure," and sandbags are more amenable to that use than rock [R1]. When ICM had to rebuild the structure at the end of its design lifespan, the same community pushed to keep sandbags rather than switch to rock — described in the transcript as the community "actually want[ing] to have the sandbags instead of rocks" [R1]. No council, developer, tourism body, or named individual is identified as the driving actor beyond "ICM" and "the community."

**Thread B (Scarborough Cliffs Stabilisation Project, candidate site).** This project was **initiated by Moreton Bay Regional Council (MBRC)**, not by a community petition or developer, in response to long-term natural erosion causing the Redcliffe Peninsula's red cliffs to retreat landward, which "posed a potential risk to public safety and infrastructure" [R3]. MBRC explicitly wanted a solution that both stopped the erosion and preserved "the natural, iconic aesthetic and public amenity of the cliffs as much as possible" — i.e., amenity/walkability was a stated design driver here too, which is part of why this site was flagged as the leading candidate for Thread A despite the material mismatch [R2][R3]. MBRC engineers worked jointly with ICM to develop the "innovative and alternate site-specific options" rather than defaulting to a standard rock-armour revetment [R3].

## Design as planned

**Thread A:** no planning detail beyond the material choice is given in the transcript — no wave-quality, beach-width, or ecological promises are described for this structure; it reads as a straightforward toe-protection groin/wall, not a multi-purpose reef [R1].

**Thread B:** MBRC and ICM explicitly considered a **conventional rock armour revetment** first and rejected it because it would have had "significant negative impact on the aesthetics of the cliffs and constrained foreshore accessibility" [R3]. The chosen design was a two-part solution: (1) a "lower cliff hardening" shotcrete wall designed to visually replicate the natural notch-eroded form of the lower cliff, including a bullnose wave-return shaped like the existing natural notch to reduce overtopping and reflect wave energy similarly to existing conditions; and (2) separate "upper cliff stabilisation" works (reprofiling, removing unstable vegetation, applying a soil binder) to improve upper-slope stability and safety [R3]. The design also built in the option to raise the wall's crest in future to adapt to sea-level rise, and ICM ran small- and large-scale in-situ colour/texture trials before construction to make sure the shotcrete could visually match the cliff [R3].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

**Thread A vs. Thread B — the unresolved mismatch.** The two seed URLs supplied for this dossier target — the ICM company case-study page and the peer-reviewed ICCE conference paper on the Scarborough Cliffs Stabilisation Project — are, between them, the only two full accounts of a Queensland ICM clay-cliff project available online, and **neither mentions sandbags anywhere**. Both independently and consistently describe the built structure as a coloured/textured GFRP-reinforced **shotcrete** wall with soil nails, not sandbags of any tonnage [R2][R3]. The full peer-reviewed paper (fetched in full, not just abstract) goes further: it explicitly states that a rock armour revetment was the *only* alternative considered and rejected, with no reference to a sandbag option ever having been used at this specific site, at this or an earlier phase [R3]. This directly contradicts the ICM presenter's own verbal description, in the same company's conference talk, of "5-ton sandbags" at an unnamed Queensland clay cliff, refurbished at least once in kind at community request [R1].

Two explanations remain open and unresolved by this research pass: (a) the presenter's "Queensland clay cliff" example is a **different, still-unidentified site** from Scarborough — ICM's public project list (checked against Munna Point Groynes, Maroochydore Groynes and Seawall, and Conway Beach Seawall) turned up sand-filled **geotextile containers** at Munna Point and Maroochydore, and plain **rock** at Conway Beach, but no other named ICM Queensland project using loose sandbags at a clay cliff [R6][R7][R8]; or (b) Scarborough itself went through an earlier, unpublished sandbag phase before the shotcrete solution documented in 2020–2023, which the ICCE paper (written specifically to document the shotcrete innovation) simply does not mention. No source found in this research pass supports (b) — the paper describes the shotcrete design as the project's original, sole erosion-protection solution, developed against a "pre-works" baseline of a bare eroding cliff, not against a prior structure [R3]. On current evidence, **(a) is the better-supported reading**, and this dossier explicitly does **not** confirm Scarborough as the true Thread-A site — it documents Scarborough on its own terms as the best-available published candidate and flags the contradiction for a future research pass to resolve (e.g., by tracking down the original YouTube video referenced in the transcript's source note, to see if on-screen images pin down the actual location).

**What is documented for Scarborough as actually built:** severe notch erosion was found in two sections extending up to 1.6 m into the cliff base immediately before construction began; treatment there included removing the overlying loose material and filling the notch with shotcrete, incorporated into the overall wall [R3]. Construction was staged: site safety works (removing near-vertical negative slopes and undermined vegetation) → re-profiling the cliff base → installing grouted GFRP soil nails, drainage and GFRP mesh reinforcement → removing loose sand/gravel/vegetation → layered shotcrete application to the design profile [R3]. The tender was awarded by MBRC to **Australian Marine & Civil Pty Ltd**, and the works were completed in **March 2020** [R3]. No cost overruns, schedule slippage, or construction problems are reported in the paper — the account reads as a clean build with no design-vs-built gap of the kind (phasing, overruns) the task brief asks about [R3].

## Outcome

**Thread A:** the transcript reports an implied success with no described failure: the sandbag wall stopped the erosion, reached the end of its intended design life, and was rebuilt in kind (sandbags again) at community request — no numbers (longevity in years, storm events survived, damage) are given [R1].

**Thread B (Scarborough):** post-construction monitoring, following storm events in **December 2020** and **February 2022**, found the works "have been successful in protecting the lower cliff from further erosion and preventing the retreat of the cliff" [R2]. The peer-reviewed paper adds that "the shotcrete wall remains well integrated with the cliff face" and that monitoring is ongoing to evaluate long-term performance and future adaptation needs for sea-level rise [R3]. No surfing-quality data applies (this is a cliff-toe erosion wall, not a surf reef). No ecological outcome data was found in either source beyond the incidental design note that the coloured/textured shotcrete surface was intended to "allow natural marine habitat creation for mollusc species" as a co-benefit of matching the cliff's texture — no follow-up ecological survey results were found [R2]. No damage events or removal have been reported as of the sources available [R2][R3].

## Why it worked / why it failed

**Thread A:** the transcript frames the sandbag choice itself as the reason it "worked" for its intended use-case — sandbags are softer and more walkable/sittable than rock, so choosing them (over a more conventional rock groin look-alike) satisfied both the erosion-control function and the community's recreational use of the structure; no engineering failure mode is described [R1].

**Thread B:** the paper attributes Scarborough's stated success to matching the engineering solution precisely to the site's specific failure mechanism — the cliffs' retreat was driven by "slow notch erosion of the cliff base resulting in translational block failure of the overlying laterized cliff face and rotational collapse of the weaker cliff crest soils," so the design specifically targeted the notch (the shotcrete's bullnose wave-return replicates the shape of the natural notch to reduce overtopping and reflect wave energy the way the original cliff face did) rather than applying a generic revetment [R3]. The explicit rejection of plain rock armour — which the authors judged less able to meet the project's combined erosion-control, aesthetic and access objectives — is presented as itself a success factor, not merely a preference [R3].

## Unexpected results

Thread A: none reported — the only "unexpected" element is the community's active preference for sandbags being reaffirmed at rebuild time, which the transcript frames as validating rather than undermining the original design choice [R1]. Thread B: none reported in the sources found — no litigation, safety incidents, ecological surprises, or unexpected tourism/fishing effects are mentioned for Scarborough [R2][R3]. **Not found** beyond what is stated above for both threads.

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Both threads are relevant to Lior's Haifa proposal mainly as **material-choice precedents at low-energy, community-amenity coastal sites** rather than as wave-climate analogues:

- **Tide range:** Scarborough sits in Moreton Bay, a semi-enclosed embayment with a moderate-to-large tidal range (the paper's own photos show clear inter-tidal exposure of the wall and note the wall's "inter-tidal location" as a specific design driver for its drainage bladder) [R3] — this is a materially larger, more sheltered-embayment tidal regime than the Mediterranean's near-negligible (~0.3–0.5 m) tide range at Haifa, so Scarborough's inter-tidal drainage engineering (weep holes, geotextile-wrapped aggregate bladder to stop wave-driven water from washing out cliff sediment) is less directly transferable to a Mediterranean micro-tidal setting, where that specific failure pathway is less pronounced.
- **What transfers directly:** the core trade-off articulated in both threads — choosing a softer/more walkable material (sandbags, or a texture/colour-matched finish) over a harder, more standard option (rock) specifically because the public uses the structure recreationally — is highly relevant to any Haifa design where local residents would sit, walk, or fish on a shore-protection structure. Scarborough's colour/texture-matching shotcrete approach (matching the exact hue and surface texture of the natural cliff via in-situ trials) is a directly transferable technique for any Haifa or Israeli coastal-cliff or seawall project where visual/aesthetic integration with the existing shoreline is a stated public concern [R3].
- **Caveat:** because Thread A (the actual "5-ton sandbags" project) remains unidentified, no confirmed sandbag-specific engineering data (bag size rationale, load capacity, refurbishment interval) is available to transfer — only the qualitative community-preference finding described in the transcript [R1].
