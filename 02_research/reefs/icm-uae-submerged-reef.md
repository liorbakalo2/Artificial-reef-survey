## Motivation and who pushed for it

The driver was engineering failure under time pressure, not politics or tourism marketing as such. Per ICM's own account, the Fairmont Ajman resort sits on "an exposed Arabian Gulf coastline" and suffered "persistent beach erosion driven by shamal storm conditions" (shamal = the strong northwesterly wind system characteristic of the Persian/Arabian Gulf) [R1]. A prior protection attempt — "a low-crested rubble breakwater and beach nourishment" — had already been tried and had "failed to provide adequate protection or maintain the site's aesthetic appeal" [R1]. The push came specifically from the client (the resort itself) against a hard deadline: "with only 14 weeks before the resort's grand opening, a comprehensive and resilient solution was required to create a stable beach and a safe swimming area for guests" [R1]. The client's own explicit brief, per ICM, combined two normally competing goals into one request: "a guaranteed stable beach guarantee for 8 years while maintaining clear, uninterrupted views out of the Arabian Gulf" [R1] — i.e., the client wanted engineering-grade erosion protection without a visible, view-blocking emergent wall. The conference-talk transcript frames this same trade-off as a live comparison on "one stretch of coastline in the UAE" between two clients: one (unnamed) accepted a fully emergent breakwater that "doesn't move at all" but blocks the view, while the other (now identified as Fairmont Ajman) chose the submerged design specifically to keep the view [R2]. Lior's own notes flag this submerged-vs-emergent trade-off as the single most relevant precedent in the source transcript for a Haifa "keep the horizon clean" design goal [R3].

## Design as planned

ICM's stated design combined three elements into one system rather than relying on a single structure [R1]:
1. **Submerged Breakwater Reef** — "a wide-crested submerged reef was designed to reduce erosion during typical storm events, maintaining the natural beach profile" [R1]. The transcript's framing of the general submerged-vs-emergent trade-off elsewhere in the same talk clarifies the underlying engineering logic: a submerged structure does not exclude all wave energy the way an emergent one does, so "once you start getting submerged, then you get into the conversation of... how much sand do you need to maintain the system? Because you're not keeping all the wave energy out" [R2] — implying the design accepts some ongoing wave transmission and beach-profile dynamism in exchange for preserving the view.
2. **Terminal Seawall** — sand-filled geotextile containers, including rapid-deployment "trapbags," positioned as a landward backstop specifically to "limit landward erosion during severe weather while minimising user impacts" [R1] — i.e., a soft, low-amenity-impact fallback rather than a hard seawall, consistent with the same soft-material logic ICM describes elsewhere (e.g. the transcript's Narrow Neck geotube reef and the Queensland sandbag cliff wall, both chosen partly because soft materials are safer/more tolerable for public use than rock) [R2].
3. **Nourished Profile** — engineered beach nourishment to reshape and replenish the beach for guest amenity and (per ICM) "a resilient coastal ecosystem" [R1].
No wave-height, wave-period, design-storm-return-period, or reef-crest-depth target is stated in any source reached. No design-stage rendering, model result, or numerical-modelling output (despite "Numerical Modelling" being listed among services provided) was found or described in text form [R1].

## Why it worked / why it failed

Only ICM's own account (as the designing engineer) is available, so this should be read as the designer's self-report, not an independent audit:
- **Why the prior rubble breakwater reportedly failed:** it was "low-crested" and, combined with beach nourishment alone, "failed to provide adequate protection or maintain the site's aesthetic appeal" under shamal storm conditions [R1] — no further engineering detail (crest height, storm event, failure mechanism) is given.
- **Why ICM's fix is presented as working:** combining a wide-crested *submerged* reef (to cut incoming wave energy during storms without fully blocking it or the view) with a soft geotextile terminal seawall (to absorb the residual landward erosion during severe events) and an engineered nourished profile addresses the erosion problem in layers rather than relying on one structure to do everything [R1]. The general engineering logic the presenter gives elsewhere in the same talk — that a submerged structure trades some wave-energy exclusion for view preservation, and that the trade-off has to be actively managed with enough sand volume to "maintain the system" — is the closest thing to a stated causal mechanism for why this kind of design can hold a beach without an emergent wall [R2].
- No source discusses crest depth relative to tide datum, reef material durability, marine growth, bag/container sizing, or any specific storm event the completed structure has withstood since 2015 (unreferenced — needs an independent source).

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

**Tide range:** No direct tide station exists for Ajman on tide-forecast.com; the nearest listed station, Sharjah (Ash Shariqah) — an adjacent emirate a few kilometres along the same Arabian Gulf coast — shows a live tidal range on the order of roughly 1.0–1.2 m (e.g. a low of 0.29–0.31 m against highs of 1.25–1.56 m across a two-day sample) [R4]. This is used here as a same-coast proxy for Ajman, not a confirmed on-site figure (unreferenced — needs a source specific to Ajman). By contrast, Haifa's Mediterranean coast is close to tideless: live tide-table data shows highs of only ~0.28–0.41 m against lows of ~0.00–0.20 m, i.e. a tidal range under half a metre [R5] — roughly a third to a half of the Arabian Gulf figure. That gap matters directly for transferability: a "wide-crested submerged reef" calibrated to stay submerged-but-effective across a ~1 m Gulf tidal swing has an easier job holding a stable relationship to the waterline at Haifa's near-flat tide, which is a genuine (if modest) point in favour of a similar design being *easier*, not harder, to size correctly for Israel — consistent with the same conclusion reached independently in this project's other dossiers on tidally larger sites (Narrowneck, Opunake, Pratte's Reef) [R5].

**What transfers conceptually:** the core lesson of this specific case — that a submerged, wide-crested reef paired with a soft geotextile backstop and beach nourishment can deliver a *stated* multi-year stable-beach outcome while preserving an unobstructed sea view, as an explicit alternative to a fully emergent, view-blocking breakwater — is the most directly relevant precedent in Lior's source material for a Haifa "keep the horizon clean" design brief [R2][R3]. What does **not** transfer without independent verification: no wave-height, wave-period, reef crest-depth, or sand-volume-maintenance figures exist for this project in any source found, so none of the actual sizing parameters that made an "8-year guarantee" achievable at Ajman can be scaled to Haifa's own wave climate (Haifa/Shikmona-buoy baseline used elsewhere in this project: significant wave height roughly 0.45–0.8 m in the surfable window, per other dossiers, not re-derived here) [R5]. The claim itself also remains a single design-firm's self-reported success with no independent verification located (see Outcome, above) — it should be cited to Lior as "the clearest UAE precedent for the submerged/view-preserving trade-off," not as an independently audited case study.
