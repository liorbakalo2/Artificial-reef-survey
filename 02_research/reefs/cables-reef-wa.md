## Motivation and who pushed for it

The reef sits on the site of a natural point break, "Cable Station reef," that had been surfed since the late 1940s and was a popular, well-regarded right-hander through the 1950s–1980s [R3]. According to Len Dibben, described by Surfing Down South as a "WA Surf Industry legend," "Cable Station reef used to break really good, a great right hander" — but Dibben says the natural break was degraded over time by coastal engineering works, including the Cottesloe Groyne, the Dutch Inn Groyne and other groynes/moles built along the coast [R3] (Dibben's opinion, as reported by the outlet, not an independently verified engineering finding in this dossier).

That perceived decline fed a long-running local campaign: the WA Surf Rider Association's 1975 "Build a Reef" campaign, involving surfers John Balgarnie and Geoff Berry [R3]. Formal site-selection work began in 1988 (M. Crawford) [R2], and a 1994 engineering report to the WA government covered site selection, design specifics, construction methods and potential environmental impacts [R5]. The push to actually build was carried through to 1999 by Cottesloe surfers John Balgarnie and Keith Campbell, working with the Perth Artificial Surfing Reef Committee [R3][R6]. Funding and institutional backing came from the WA Department of Sport and Recreation and the state government's marine/engineering agencies (WA Department of Marine and Harbours, University of Western Australia) [R2][R5] — i.e., this was a state-government-funded, surfer-lobbied amenity project, not a port-mitigation or erosion-control scheme.

## Design as planned

The design goal, as stated by the Perth Artificial Surfing Reef Committee, was a reef that would generate consistent left- and right-hand waves with a "gently barreling" face, working across a swell range of 0.5 m to 3.5 m and a peel angle around 45° suited to intermediate surfers [R1][R2][R5]. Design studies used physical model testing — a random-wave flume and a 1:40-scale model in a 40 m × 40 m wave basin — to test reef shapes; the optimum bottom slope for a plunging breaker was found to be 1:20, which the researchers noted matched both the site's natural bed slope and slopes recorded at many surf breaks worldwide. Several reef shapes were tested, and a "boomerang-shaped" form that minimised the volume of fill required and merged with the existing natural reef was selected as the final design [R8]. The chosen approach — placing rock directly onto the existing limestone bedrock rather than building a sand-filled geotextile-bag structure on a sandy seabed — was explicitly meant to avoid sinking into sand and to remove the need for any sand cover over the structure [R2][R5].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Construction began in February 1999 and reached roughly 90% completion by May 1999, but was not fully finished until December 1999 [R2][R5] — a longer build than the initial ~90%-by-May milestone implies. WA Limestone carried out a cyclical barge-transport-and-placement process, using over 10,000 tons of quarried granite boulders (individual boulders up to 3 m across) placed on the limestone bedrock [R2][R5]. One documented mid-project change: once it became clear the rock-on-bedrock method prevented sinking and needed no sand cover, that realisation "led to an extension of the reef's southern end" beyond the original footprint [R5] — a design-to-built change, though the source does not give a size or cost figure for the extension (unreferenced — needs source for the extension's dimensions/cost). No cost-overrun figure is reported in any source found for this dossier (not found).

## Outcome

Early academic monitoring was positive. Stacey Bancroft's 1999 performance-monitoring study found the reef's peel angles closely matched the ~45° design target, and recorded the number of surfable days exceeding the predicted values across three different measurement methods [R2][R5][R9]. Charitha Pattiaratchi's monitoring through 1999–2001 concluded the reef was "performing according to its design and its performing as well or better than predicted," citing roughly four to six months of conditions conducive to wave-breaking in 2000 and 2001 — though the paper notes this conclusion "has been disputed by some members of the local surfing population" [R9][R2].

That dispute widened over the following decade. A 2009 assessment by Andrew Pitt (SurfingRamps.com.au) found wave frequency to be the primary shortfall: the reef "only provides a 'few surfable days per month'" (Pitt's own phrase, as quoted by the source), and does not reliably break chest-high waves, contrary to the design's 0.5 m minimum-swell target. Pitt attributed this to the reef's crest depth (around 1.5 m below the lowest tide) and to the site's position in the "swell shadow" of Rottnest Island and other outer reefs, which block a significant share of incoming wave energy; he recommended shallowing the reef and extending its seaward nose to capture more swell [R2][R5]. Wikipedia's own summary similarly notes the reef's performance is "hampered by the presence of offshore islands and reefs which block a significant fraction of the wave energy," and that the site is "used for surfing when wave conditions are large enough" [R1].

The two headline numbers found for this dossier directly conflict and are both given here: some sources describe the reef as delivering rideable waves on the order of "150 days per year," while Pitt's 2009 assessment and other surfer commentary describe "only a handful of good days a month" — i.e., closer to a few dozen days a year [R2][R5][R6]. This dossier could not resolve which figure is more current or how each was measured; treat the 150-days figure as an early/optimistic estimate and the "handful a month" figure as a later, more critical local assessment.

Structurally, the granite/limestone reef has proven durable and resistant to wave damage, with better shape-retention than the geotextile-bag reefs built elsewhere in the same era [R2][R5]. Ecologically, Bancroft's monitoring documented significant biological recolonisation of the new rock surfaces [R2][R5]. No coastal-protection outcome (beach widening/salient formation) is reported, consistent with the reef never being designed for that purpose — one source explicitly notes "no salient formation was reported" [R2].

## Why it worked / why it failed

Cable Station Reef is repeatedly cited, alongside Burkitts Reef (Bargara, Qld) and Palm Beach Reef (Gold Coast), as one of the few artificial surf reefs built from solid rock rather than sand-filled geotextile bags/containers — and one of the few that did not structurally fail [R6][R7]. In a 2021 investigative feature, surf scientist Shaw Mead (co-founder of the reef-design firm ASR, whose geotextile-bag designs failed at Boscombe, Mount Maunganui, Opunake and Kovalam) told Newsroom (NZ): "while the structures have failed (except for Cable Station (limestone), Borth (rock), Palm Beach (rock) and Narrowneck (sandbags maintained)), all have created surfing waves that have replicated the designs (numerical and physical modelling)" [R7]. The Inertia frames the same pattern: "Cable Station, Burkitts, and Palm Beach are artificial reefs in Australia that were created using stone instead of sandbags... None of these locations were transformed into a world-class break, but each one had modest, measurable improvements in the quality of waves" [R6].

The engineering logic given across sources is that geotextile sand-filled containers fail because losing sand from even one bag changes the whole structure's shape (and hence the wave it breaks), whereas rock placed directly on a hard limestone bedrock — as at Cable Station — does not sink, does not need sand cover, and keeps its shape under wave attack [R2][R5][R6]. This is why Cable Station's structure has lasted, in contrast to Narrowneck, Mount Reef, Pratte's/Chevron Reef and Boscombe, all of which used sand-filled bags and suffered bag failure, sand burial or displacement (see Lior's own file note on the "bags falling apart" caption below).

Why it under-delivers on wave frequency, per the sources: (1) the reef crest sits too deep for smaller/moderate swells to break reliably (~1.5 m below lowest tide, against a 0.5 m minimum-swell design target) [R2][R5]; and (2) the site lies in the lee of Rottnest Island and other offshore reefs, which absorb or block a meaningful share of incoming swell energy before it reaches Cable Station [R1][R2][R5]. In short: the structure is durable and shaped correctly, but placed where it doesn't receive swell often enough at a workable depth.

## Unexpected results

Access to the site has caused dune erosion, as surfers cross the cliff face on foot to reach the break [R2][R5]. On the positive side, Bancroft's monitoring found significant biological recolonisation of the placed rock — an ecological benefit not central to the reef's original surf-only design brief [R2][R5]. No reports of increased fishing pressure, dangerous rips, drowning incidents, or litigation tied specifically to Cable Station Reef were found in the sources reviewed for this dossier (not found) — this contrasts with Mount Maunganui's reef, where a scour hole and worsened rip currents became a serious, separately documented safety hazard (not part of this dossier, included here only for contrast, per R6/R7 background reading).

## What could have been done better

**Experts'/engineers' view:** Andrew Pitt's 2009 assessment recommended making the reef shallower and extending its seaward "nose" further offshore, to intercept more swell before it is blocked by Rottnest Island and the outer reefs, and to allow smaller swells to break as originally designed [R2][R5].

**Surfers' view:** the recurring complaint across sources is consistency, not quality — surfers "appreciate the quality of the wave when it breaks" but see "lack of frequency as the biggest problem," with the reef reportedly not breaking reliably at the sub-chest-high swell sizes the original design targeted [R1][R2][R5][R6].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

**Tide range.** Fremantle/Perth sits on a Mediterranean-climate coast (Köppen Csa) with the well-known afternoon "Fremantle Doctor" sea breeze [R11], but no source fetched for this dossier gave an exact numeric tidal range for Fremantle/Cable Station (unreferenced — needs source; Western Australia's south-west coast is generally understood to have a small, mixed/mainly-diurnal tide, but this dossier could not confirm a specific figure from a fetched source). Israel's Mediterranean coast (including Haifa) is itself close to tideless: Wikipedia's general "Tide" article notes that "the oscillation modes of the Mediterranean Sea... do not coincide with any significant astronomical forcing period," which is why Mediterranean tides are small [R12] — but again, no specific Haifa/Israel tidal-range figure in meters was found via a fetched source in this dossier (unreferenced — needs source). What transfers directly regardless of the exact numbers: Cable Station's core engineering lesson — build in solid rock placed directly on hard substrate rather than sand-filled geotextile bags on a sandy bed — removes tide-driven sand-burial risk as a failure mode, which is directly relevant to any Israeli reef design, tidal range aside.

**Wave climate.** Cable Station was designed for a 0.5–3.5 m swell range with a 45° peel angle, and its real-world problem has been insufficient swell energy reaching the reef, not excess swell [R1][R2][R5]. Lior's own field measurements at the Shikmona buoy off Haifa (isramar.ocean.org.il) define the "minimum surfable wave" window there as a 0.45–0.6 m significant wave height (0.6–0.8 m maximum wave height), with a tightly clustered swell approach direction of ~284–294° (WNW) and peak periods around 5.7–6.2 s [R15]. That is a substantially smaller, more marginal wave climate than the swell range Cable Station's designers had to work with. The direct transferable lesson is Cable Station's own core failure mode — a reef crest set too deep to reliably break the smaller end of the local swell range, compounded by offshore blocking of swell energy — which is exactly the risk profile a low-energy site like Haifa/Shikmona would face if a reef crest were set too conservatively (too deep). Cable Station's Pitt-recommended fix (shallower crest) is the same design lever Lior's own sketch addresses directly — his "Light Construction" concept explicitly places its crest near the natural "Wave Breaking Depth" line rather than deeper [R15].

**What transfers.** (1) Solid rock on hard substrate is more durable than sand-filled bags — but Cable Station shows durability alone does not guarantee frequent, high-quality surf if the crest is set too deep or the site sits in a swell shadow; getting the crest depth and site swell-exposure right matters as much as the construction material. (2) A government/community-driven, surfer-advocated funding model (Cable Station: WA Dept of Sport and Recreation, after a 24-year advocacy campaign from 1975 to 1999) is a plausible model for an Israeli project pursued through local surfer/NGO advocacy (per Lior's own outreach material to Zalul referenced elsewhere in the project) rather than requiring a large commercial or port-mitigation budget.
