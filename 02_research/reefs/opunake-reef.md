## Motivation and who pushed for it

The project was driven by the South Taranaki District Council and a local community body, the Opunake Artificial Surf Reef Trust, as a tourism and economic-development play rather than a coastal-protection one: New Zealand Geographic's 2006 pre-construction piece states plainly that "none of these reefs has been designed to protect beaches from erosion; rather, all three [Mount Maunganui and the two planned Opunake reefs] are intended to improve conditions for surfers," and that "the principal reason for building the reefs is to draw more visitors to the town and so create jobs in a district where the population has been in slow decline for some time" [R3]. Baseline research found surfable waves at Opunake only about one day in eight; the reef was meant to lift that to about one day in three [R2][R3][R4]. The scientific/engineering push came from Kerry Black and ASR Ltd, who by 2006 had roughly 30 artificial-reef projects under way worldwide and had already built the first multi-purpose reef at Narrowneck, Gold Coast [R3].

## Design as planned

As planned pre-construction, the Opunake project was actually **two** reefs: a cheaper north reef to be built first, and a larger south reef to follow once the north reef was working. The north reef was costed at about NZ$1.3 million, would use geotextile bags across a 90 m × 20 m footprint, and was designed to produce a right-hand wave 1.8–2.5 m high on the wave face, rideable for about 100 m. The (never-built) south reef was to cost more and, on paper, deliver a 200 m ride on waves up to 3.6 m high [R3]. ASR tested the design in a small-scale wave pool at its Raglan facility before construction [R2][R4]. Black publicly believed the north reef could be finished within about two months of construction starting [R2][R4]. As with ASR's other "multi-purpose reef" designs, the pitch combined three promises — better surf, and (in the general MPR concept, though not claimed as a goal for Opunake specifically) coastal protection and marine-habitat enhancement — but Opunake's own reef was explicitly surf-only, not erosion mitigation [R3].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Only the (cheaper) north reef was ever attempted; the south reef was never built. Construction, planned to take about two months, instead dragged on for years: by 2009 all 27 geotextile bags were reportedly in place, but the project still needed "fine tuning" — filling some bags with more sand and closing gaps between bags — and Neil Walker, acting chairman of the Opunake Artificial Surf Reef Trust, told Stuff.co.nz the wave was "already breaking on a couple of points," while spending had already passed NZ$1.7 million against the original $1.1 million allocation, with the Trust seeking a further $400,000 [R2][R4]. Around the same time, and just before ASR's majority sale to the US company Sealutions LLC, Shaw Mead met with the South Taranaki District Council mayor to propose finishing the reef by topping the geo-containers with rock, estimated at $150,000; as part of the ASR sale negotiations Sealutions (Nick Behunin and Chris Jensen) agreed to set aside $150,000 to complete the reef, with the council to repay the funds once it worked — but Sealutions reneged on that commitment [R2][R4]. In 2010 Stuff reported further delays from changes in the seabed: one part of the reef was working better than the other two, and Nick Behunin (Sealutions managing partner) said "We may have to change the bag layout and how full the bags are" [R2][R4]. By August 2011 the relationship between council, Trust and ASR had broken down: Mayor Ross Dunlop publicly criticised ASR's performance and wrote off the council's $400,000 loan to the Trust; ASR called this "defamatory," and marketing director Chris Jensen said "The council's stance has been that it would not put any more funds into the project for the past three years," adding that ASR believed at least $133,000 more was needed to finish the job [R2][R4]. The Trust sued ASR later in 2011; the council wrote the debt off as "unrecoverable"; ASR went into liquidation not long after, in 2012 [R1][R2][R4].

## Outcome

No source found gives a measured/instrumented surf-quality outcome (no wave-height, peel-angle or ride-length monitoring data of the kind that exists for Narrowneck) — "not found." Qualitatively, every source agrees the reef never produced the promised rideable waves: a 2021 investigative piece states flatly "the artificial reef didn't produce the expected waves" and quotes NZ Surfing Magazine editor Cory Scott saying Opunake's fate mirrored other ASR reefs, with "more excuses of construction error or being over budget" [R1]. The same article notes the reef did attract fish and other sea-life — fulfilling one general aim of a "multi-purpose reef" — but that the structure itself began to fall apart, with pieces washing ashore [R1]. No coastal-protection outcome is reported because none was designed in [R3]. No removal/dismantling of the structure is documented in any source found — "not found" (contrast with Mount Maunganui, New Zealand's other ASR reef, which the Bay of Plenty Regional Council formally removed in 2014 for NZ$87,000 [R4]).

## Why it worked / why it failed

It did not work. Causes given across sources:

- **Chronic under-budgeting and funding gaps.** The council's $1.1 million allocation (or the pre-construction $1.3 million estimate) proved far short of what was needed; by 2009 spend had already passed $1.7 million with more required, and the extra funding (first $400,000, later a $150,000 rock-capping fix, later $133,000) repeatedly failed to materialise or arrive in time [R1][R2][R4].
- **Geotextile-bag durability/shape problems**, the same failure mode ASR's own co-founder later identified as the "fatal flaw" of the whole technology: "as soon as even a single sand-filled container broke and lost sand, the whole structure changed shape due to the fluid nature of sand… [which] leads to a reduction in quality for surfing." Shaw Mead listed causes across ASR's reefs including anchors (Gold Coast), debris strikes (Kovalam), over-filling during construction (Mount Reef) and propeller strike (Boscombe); for Opunake the specific failure mechanism is not itemised beyond "over budget" and seabed change, but it belongs to the same fabric-bag family [R5].
- **Seabed changes over time** meant different sections of the reef performed unevenly by 2010, undermining the original bag layout [R2][R4].
- **Corporate instability at ASR**: Black and Mead sold a majority stake to Sealutions LLC around 2009–2010; Sealutions did not honour a $150,000 pledge to finish the reef; ASR itself was removed from the companies register (liquidated) in 2012, ending any prospect of a fix [R1][R2][R4].
- **No coastal-protection fallback.** Because the Opunake reef was designed purely for surfing, once the surf failed to materialise there was no secondary benefit (erosion control, ecology-as-headline-goal) to justify the sunk cost, unlike some other multi-purpose reefs [R3].

## Unexpected results

- **Ecological colonisation despite structural failure**: the reef reportedly did attract fish and sea-life even as it failed to produce surf [R1].
- **Debris and litter**: pieces of the failed reef washed ashore over time [R1] — a pattern repeated more dramatically at Mount Maunganui (large folds of material mistaken by beachgoers for wreckage from the 2011 Rena shipping disaster) and at Boscombe (a damaged geo-container removed in 2011, more debris washing up as late as Boxing Day 2017) [R1].
- **Litigation and write-offs, not just underperformance**: the Trust suing its own contractor (ASR) in 2011, and the council writing off first a $400,000 loan and then its whole claim as "unrecoverable," turned an engineering failure into a protracted financial and legal dispute [R1][R2][R4].
- No source found documents fishing-pressure, rip-current/swimmer-safety incidents, or tourism data specific to Opunake (contrast with Mount Maunganui, where the Bay of Plenty Regional Council's Eddie Grogan said the reef created "a large scour hole which affects waves and currents, increasing the frequency and intensity of rips which pose a serious risk to swimmers," a hazard severe enough that the council said "we have to dismantle it") [R1][R5] — "not found" for Opunake itself.

## What could have been done better

**Engineers'/designers' view:** Shaw Mead (ASR co-founder, later reflecting publicly on the whole ASR portfolio) concluded the geotextile-bag construction method itself was the core weakness — "I undertook a critical review of the performance of sand-filled containers for MPR construction approximately a decade ago, which led to the change to traditional rock construction methods of the most recent MPRs built" [R5] — and in a separate email to Newsroom called the sandbags "an evolutionary deadend, seemed to have potential to begin with…they are still useful for large volumes if the shape is not important" [R1]. At Opunake specifically, ASR's own 2009 fix proposal was to cap the geo-containers with rock (est. $150,000) rather than keep tuning bags with sand — i.e., partially converting to the rock-based approach Mead later endorsed generally — but the funding for that fix was not delivered [R2][R4]. Jim Moriarty, CEO of the Surfrider Foundation, drew the broader lesson that ocean-based artificial reef attempts as a class have "absolutely horrible" results and argued effort should go into protecting natural breaks instead of trying to manufacture new ones [R6].

**Surfers'/local view:** Cory Scott (NZ Surfing Magazine editor/photographer) was unequivocal: "It is my opinion and belief that not one artificial reef has created a wave of any quality, despite claims to the contrary" — a blanket surfer's verdict on the whole ASR-designed reef program, Opunake included [R1]. No Opunake-specific surfer quote proposing an alternative design or fix was found — "not found."

**Process/funding view (implicit across sources):** the repeated pattern of underestimating both time (Black expected 2 months; it ran years) and cost (three separate top-up requests — $400k in 2009, $150k in 2009–10, $133k in 2011 — each unmet or partly unmet) suggests better contingency budgeting and a firmer completion contract with ASR/Sealutions would have been needed to finish the job at all [R1][R2][R4].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

**Tide range** is the single biggest physical-environment contrast. Opunake sits on an open, moderately macrotidal New Zealand coast: a sample tide cycle shows a high water of 2.94 m against a low of 0.47 m, i.e. a tidal range on the order of ~2.5 m [R7]. Haifa, on Israel's virtually tideless Mediterranean coast, shows a high tide of only ~0.36–0.40 m against a low tide of ~0.00–0.07 m — a tidal range of well under half a metre [R12]. That means an Opunake-style geotextile-bag reef has to keep working correctly across a ~2.5 m swing in water depth over the structure every tide cycle (submergence at high tide, much shallower/more exposed water at low tide), which is a substantially harder crest-depth design problem than any Israeli Mediterranean site would present, where the crest depth relative to mean sea level barely varies with the tide. This is a genuine engineering point in Israel's favour if a similar reef were proposed at Haifa or elsewhere on the coast: designers would not need to accommodate anything like Opunake's tidal swing.

However, the dominant failure modes actually documented at Opunake — chronic under-budgeting, repeated funding shortfalls for "finishing" work, corporate instability at the design/construction firm, and the intrinsic durability problem of sand-filled geotextile bags (bag tears → sand loss → shape change → wave-quality loss) [R1][R2][R4][R5] — are not tide-dependent and would transfer directly to any Israeli geotextile-bag project, including the Ashkelon geotube project already logged in this project's master list (`ashkelon-geotube-israel`), which itself suffered partial construction failure (15 of 48 geotubes destroyed) and higher-than-expected maintenance costs. The clearest transferable lesson from Opunake for Israel/Haifa is therefore financial and material, not tidal: secure full contingency funding and a completion-guarantee contract before committing to a geotextile-bag structure, and weigh rock-based construction (which ASR's own co-founder ultimately endorsed over bags) given the technology's poor track record on sand-bag durability specifically.

Wave climate: Opunake is an open, exposed Tasman-Sea-facing beach with fairly consistent groundswell and windswell, rated "fairly consistent" surf with clean conditions about 26% of the time in the best month (April) [R7] — a higher-energy, more consistent swell environment than the fetch-limited Eastern Mediterranean off Haifa, which typically needs stronger, more localised storm systems to generate comparable surf-height swell (general knowledge, not sourced in this research pass — flagged, not a citable fact).
