## Motivation and who pushed for it

Bunbury sits close to Margaret River, one of Australia's premier surf destinations, but Back Beach itself was not known for good waves — described by local coverage as having swell without the bathymetry to shape it into rideable form [R1][R2][R6]. The push came from two directions: Waveco founder Troy Bottegal, a Dunsborough-based inventor who had spent roughly a decade developing the inflatable-bladder concept and was seeking a real-world test site [R2][R10] (correction: R7 was also cited here in an earlier draft but does not discuss the development timeline or motivation — checked directly), and the City of Bunbury under Mayor Gary Brennan, who saw the project as a low-cost way to brand the town as a surfing destination and draw competitions and business tourism: "The Bunbury benefit will be attracting surfing competitions and more business coming here" [R2]. The City Council formally approved a A$75,000 sponsorship in June 2019 [R1][R2], after an August 2018 Kickstarter campaign to fund the concept had failed to raise enough money [R3]. The University of Western Australia also committed to a 12-month scientific study of the reef's performance once installed [R9].

## Design as planned

The Airwave was designed as a 12 m-diameter, dome-shaped inflatable rubber bladder, anchored to the seabed with sand ballast and filled with a mix of air and sand/water, sitting roughly 30–45 m offshore in about 1 m of water at low tide [R2][R3][R9]. The stated aim was to "lift, peak, and hold the swell to create a concave, tapered wall of water that retains its shape as it peels toward the beach" [R19] (verification note: this quote is from a 2018 SurferToday preview article, not from R7 as the earlier draft mistakenly cited — R7 is a different, later SurferToday article about the December 2019 tear and does not contain this phrase; checked directly), producing an A-frame peak with both left and right breaks (per R3's "Direction: Left and right breaks" spec) where there had previously only been closeouts [R3] (correction: an earlier draft cited [R6][R7] for this whole claim, but neither contains "left and right breaks" or "closeouts" in this context — R6 only supports the general A-frame concept, from Bottegal's unrelated bathtub-demo anecdote, checked directly). Bottegal explicitly modelled the intended wave on Thunderbomb, a wave in Nicaragua [R4]. The design promised to work across a range of swell — improving faces from roughly 2 to 8 feet — with a 1 m swell producing smaller, learner-friendly waves and a 2 m swell producing "a heaving barrel at the back of it, with a long peeling wall" [R2][R3]. Expected ride length was about 40 m / 26 seconds [R3][R4]. Bunbury was said to receive an estimated 150 days per year of rideable swell that the reef could work with [R9]. A state-of-the-art webcam with a hard-wired optical cable was planned to monitor the reef and its wave production for the university study [R9]. The concept was explicitly pitched on environmental and reversibility grounds rather than ecological habitat creation: because it is inflatable, "if they don't do what you want them to do, or prove to be an environmental nuisance, they take half a day to move" [R10] — i.e. removability, not reef ecology, was the sustainability pitch, and it was also pitched as far cheaper than a conventional (rock/geotextile) artificial reef [R10].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Construction/fabrication was reported "halfway complete" in October 2019 with a target of having the reef installed and ready to surf by November 2019 [R4]. In practice, installation slipped into December 2019. Divers and a boat crew got the bladder to roughly 90% complete — deployed but not yet fully anchored — when, on a Friday in mid-December 2019, an unexpectedly large and deep offshore swell drove strong undertow through the line-up during the final fill/anchoring phase [R1][R5][R6]. Bottegal: "We had a lot of undertow — a new swell came through that was very deep swell that we weren't planning on... The pressure from the airbag going back and forward with the undertow and incoming swell has put some pressure on that particular seam" [R1][R5][R6]. The tear was along a glued seam, not through the rubber itself ("it hasn't torn the actual rubber, which is very strong") [R1]. A later, more detailed account (Bunbury Mail) attributes the root cause to the choice of Hypalon rubber, which could not be welded and so had to be glued at the seams — a joint that could not withstand the pump pressure reached during the final fill phase [R8]. The partially-inflated bladder was removed from the water within about three days of the tear [R8]. City of Bunbury CEO Mal Osborne called the installation attempt itself "no mean feat" [R1]. The city confirmed that any repair or replacement would be at no cost to ratepayers [R1][R5].

## Outcome

The installation was not completed in December 2019: the bladder was pulled from the water after tearing and was not surfable in its intended form [R1][R5][R8]. Despite this, the Bunbury Mail account states that during the brief period the reef was in the water and partially working, "a wave did break on it... peeled nearly all the way to the beach... created a 0.75 metre wave" [R8] — the only quantified wave-performance data found for this installation, describing a small, partial, uncontrolled result rather than a validated trial. No formal University of Western Australia study results were found (the planned 12-month study appears never to have started, since the reef was never fully operational) [R9]. Bottegal insisted publicly that "the Airwave prototype installation certainly was not a failure" and that Waveco was continuing to develop the technology, including studies on adding rock to existing inshore reefs — this is a direct quote from R8 (Bunbury Mail), not an unfetched search-summary as the earlier draft stated; the earlier mislabelling is corrected here [R8]. R8 is dated 2022-03-22 (not "undated" as the earlier draft said — checked the page's publication metadata directly), so this "not a failure" defence and the "Summer 2024" target date were both stated more than two years after the December 2019 tear, with Bottegal still framing the relaunch as unfinished business [R8]. A separate, contemporaneous South Western Times article (2020-07-09) — previously flagged in this dossier as an unfetched high-priority follow-up (R18) and now retrieved via web.archive.org — shows an intermediate step not previously captured here: Waveco was already planning a second Airwave prototype installation for December 2020, requiring a further A$300,000 in funding, incorporating lessons from the 2019 tear (installation method and prototype design both changed) [R18]. No source located in this research confirms whether that December 2020 second-prototype installation actually took place or succeeded — the trail goes cold between the 2020-07-09 SWT article and the 2022-03-22 Bunbury Mail article, which speaks only of a future "Summer 2024" attempt, implying the Dec 2020 attempt did not result in a working reef either [R18][R8]. Bottegal later said the project had moved fabrication to a Western Australian manufacturer and secured new funding, with an expected return to the water "in time for Summer, 2024" [R8].

Note: R18 also contains a direct quote from Bottegal about the December 2019 removal — "We successfully removed the prototype in half a day, proving it was easily removable if necessary" [R18] — which conflicts with R1/R5/R8's "removed... within three days" for the same event; both figures are given here because neither source can be shown to be wrong (they may describe different phases: total time from tear to removal vs. the physical extraction operation itself), but readers should treat the exact removal timeframe as unresolved.

As of September 2025, Waveco's own current website and Instagram activity show the company's public-facing Bunbury project has shifted toward a "granite artificial reef" design (rock/ASR-style, not the inflatable rubber bladder), with a dedicated project site at bunburyartificialreef.info framed primarily around coastal-erosion protection rather than surf amenity; a company Instagram post (2025-09-10) says "Our granite artificial reef design for Bunbury in Western Australia is suitable for many coastal locations globally" [R20]. This does not confirm whether the original inflatable Airwave was ever successfully reinstalled at Bunbury — it suggests that by 2025 Waveco's active Bunbury proposal was a different reef technology altogether. This is new information not in the original draft of this dossier and materially updates the "unconfirmed/unclear" status below. A Waveco Instagram post from 24 June 2024 shows an active Bunbury Back Beach surf-cam feed [R11], but no source located in this research confirms a completed, working reinstallation or documents post-2024 wave performance. Longevity, coastal-protection effects, and ecological effects: not found — the reef never reached a completed, functioning state in any source located.

## Why it worked / why it failed

**Failure mechanism (installation, Dec 2019):** an unexpectedly deep offshore swell during the final anchoring/fill phase generated strong undertow that flexed the bladder ("going back and forward"), concentrating stress on a seam [R1][R5][R6]. The underlying materials cause, per the Bunbury Mail's more detailed retrospective, was the use of Hypalon rubber, which cannot be welded; the glued seams used instead could not withstand the pressure reached during the final pump/fill stage [R8]. This is a materials/fabrication-method failure compounded by an installation-timing/weather-window problem (the swell that hit during install was larger/deeper than planned for), rather than a wave-performance or siting failure — the reef never reached a state where its surf-shaping performance could be properly judged. Note: Israel's project notes on a separate, unrelated failure (Boscombe Surf Reef, UK) attribute failure there to a sharp seabed depth transition and later damage from a boat-propeller strike [Lior's opinion, R14] — a different, unconfirmed failure mode not established for Bunbury; no evidence in the sources found here ties the Bunbury tear to bathymetry or crest depth.

## Unexpected results

The one recorded moment of the Airwave actually functioning — before the tear forced its removal — reportedly produced a wave that "peeled nearly all the way to the beach" and reached about 0.75 m, an unplanned, brief, uncontrolled glimpse of the concept working, according to Bottegal's later account [R8]. No sources found in this research document fishing-pressure effects, ecological colonization, rip-current changes, safety incidents, or litigation connected to this installation — plausible because the structure was never operational for an extended period. Local news noted the reputational "deflation" for Bunbury when the trial failed on a high-profile install day, with one tourist quoted as sympathetic but willing to wait ("would have been good for the city") [R1].

## What could have been done better

**From Waveco/the designer's own account:** the fix identified was materials and fabrication method — move away from glued Hypalon seams (relocating manufacture to a WA-based manufacturer) and secure fresh investment before re-attempting installation [R8]. Bottegal also framed the setback as a timing/weather issue (an unplanned deep swell during the vulnerable, partially-anchored install phase) rather than a fundamental design flaw, maintaining "100 percent" confidence the concept would work [R6].

**Surfers'/commentators' view:** not found in detail from primary surfer voices in the sources successfully retrieved (several forum and opinion pieces — Swellnet, The Inertia, BeachGrit — returned HTTP 403 and could not be read in full during this research; see References for the URLs, which a future pass with different access could revisit). The tourist quote captured directly ("would have been good for the city," Camille-Audrey Perron) reflects disappointment but patience rather than a substantive critique of the engineering [R1].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Bunbury sits on a microtidal Western Australian coast: recorded tide heights at Bunbury for a sample day show values of roughly 0.0–1.2 m (four daily tides of 0.4 m, 0.7 m, 0.3 m and 0.6 m), i.e. a tidal range on the order of ~1 m or less [R13] — broadly comparable in scale to the Mediterranean's small tidal range at Haifa, so the "reef stays in a fairly constant depth band across the tide cycle" argument that applies to Israel would plausibly also have applied at Bunbury; not itself a factor implicated in the Bunbury failure. Wave climate is different in kind: Bunbury/SW WA faces the Indian Ocean and receives Southern Ocean groundswell (an estimated ~150 rideable-swell days/year was cited for the site) [R9], a much more energetic and consistent swell environment than the short-period, wind-driven Eastern Mediterranean seas off Haifa that Lior's other notes describe (Shikmona buoy baseline: significant wave height 0.45–0.6 m). What transfers to Israel: (1) the low-cost, rapidly-removable inflatable concept is attractive precisely for a wave-poor, low-tidal-range coast like Israel's, since it avoids a large capital commitment before wave performance is proven; (2) the documented failure mode — glued (not welded) seam material failing under dynamic swell/undertow loading during the vulnerable partially-anchored installation phase — is a fabrication and installation-window risk that any Israeli inflatable-reef pilot would need to solve first, independent of Israel's own wave/tide conditions; (3) because Israel's swell energy is much lower than SW Australia's, an Airwave-type device sized for Bunbury's 2–8 ft face range may be oversized or mismatched for Haifa's much smaller everyday wave heights — this would need dedicated re-sizing/modelling, not found in any source here.
