## Motivation and who pushed for it

The Mount Maunganui Reef Trust obtained resource consent in August 2000 for a pilot offshore submerged reef, conceived partly as ongoing University of Waikato surf-reef research and partly as a community/tourism project to give the town "a superior surf break" and attract surfing visitors [R4]. The Reef Trust itself argued the reef would "return more than 30 times its cost through visitor spending," an economic-development pitch used to solicit the public donations and community-funding-group grants (TECT, Pub Charity, Bay Trust) that ultimately paid for it [R8][R6]. It was explicitly framed by its designer, ASR Ltd/Kerry Black, as a wave-quality project rather than a coastal-protection project: "Like Cable Station, the Mount Reef was designed purely to improve surfing wave quality" [R1].

## Design as planned

The design, credited to Kerry Black and ASR Ltd (design/concept paper Black & Mead, cited by the project master list as "Black, S.M.; Kerry, A. 1999, *Coastal Manag.* 27:355–365" [R13]), called for a reef built from large sand-filled geotextile containers, sized larger than the earlier Narrowneck (Gold Coast) units, for a total design volume reported as approximately 5,000 m³ by one source [R1] and 6,000 m³ by another [R2]. The design promised world-class, high-quality peeling waves able to host up to 50 surfers at once [R4], plus (implicitly, from the "multipurpose" framing used across the sources) some coastal-protection and amenity value [R13]. An early construction-phase account (Nov 2005) describes a V-shaped layout of two 70 m × 30 m arms set 240 m offshore [R8], while the reef as intended in its final form is described elsewhere as a 50 m × 100 m submerged footprint using containers up to 60 m long and 5 m in diameter [R2]. No crest-depth figure, sand/rock split, or hydrodynamic wave-height model output was found in any source fetched for this dossier.

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Construction was repeatedly delayed by bad weather and equipment failure [R1], stretched over roughly three years (2005–2008) instead of a single season, and never reached the designed volume: the finished structure held only about 2,800 m³ of sand-filled geotextile against a design target of 5,000–6,000 m³ [R1][R2] — i.e. built at roughly half the intended volume. Construction proceeded in halves: the left/western side began in November 2005, and by 8 March 2006 was already producing "quick, head-high, left-hand barrels" on a small windswell even while incomplete [R2]. The right side was reported "nearly to specifications" by October 2006, but final construction did not finish until June 2008 [R2][R7] — a multi-year gap the sources attribute to weather, funding, and construction snags rather than a single design change [R1][R2]. ASR Ltd, the reef's designer, later went into liquidation; the company was also separately criticised for its Boscombe (Dorset, UK) surf reef, which suffered comparable geotextile-container degradation within about two years of construction according to a peer-reviewed evaluation of that reef [R4][R9].

## Outcome

When it worked, surfers reported the reef producing "quick barreling waves similar to what you might find at a ledge like the Box but smaller," with ride lengths of 20–40 m [R2]; the very first days of surf on the partially built reef (Sunday, per an article dated 21–22 December 2005, i.e. most likely 18 December 2005) were described as short, sharp, chest-to-shoulder-high waves that mostly "disappeared before making the link" to the inner beach, more suited to bodyboarders than standing surfers at that stage [R5]. But usable conditions were rare: long-time local surfer and former surf-shop owner Derek Winter said rideable surf occurred only "three or four times since the surf reef was completed" and called it "pretty pathetic" [R7]. ASR's Dr Shaw Mead conceded the reef was "over-sold and under-delivered" and was "a fickle break" that needed precise swell/tide alignment [R7]. Coastal-protection performance was mixed: one source reports the beach in the reef's lee grew "up to 20 m wider than sections of the beach away from the reef" [R1] (an unreferenced-elsewhere, single-source claim — no measurement methodology or date given). Safety outcomes were negative: the reef caused scouring, "unexpected holes in adjacent sand," and "increased rip currents," raising lifeguard call-out rates and drawing a "dangerous flop" verdict from lifeguards [R2][R3][R4]. The five-year resource consent lapsed in 2010, an independent specialist report assessed status-quo/removal/repair options, and the Bay of Plenty Regional Council opted for (partial) removal, executed 25 September–10 November 2014 at a reported cost of NZ$87,000 [R2] (vs "~NZ$60,000" in two other accounts [R3][R4]), described by council staff as completed "on time and within budget" [R6]. Removal was only partial — large sections of geotextile fabric, "likely" from this reef "given the large size of the geotextile fabric," washed ashore near Sutherland Ave on 18 January 2023, prompting a fresh council clean-up and safety advisory; the council said it expected the fabric "came loose due to the weather and tidal conditions experienced last week" [R12].

## Why it worked / why it failed

The core engineering failure identified across sources is a volume shortfall: the reef was built to roughly half its designed sand volume (2,800 m³ actual vs. 5,000–6,000 m³ designed) [R1][R2], which the sources link to construction delays from weather and equipment failure rather than a documented deliberate redesign [R1]. This under-delivery meant the structure could not reliably reproduce the wave shape the design promised, leaving it "fickle" and dependent on rare, precisely-aligned swell and tide conditions [R7]. On the coastal-process side, the reef altered nearshore currents in ways that were not the intended benefit: it produced localised scour holes and intensified rip currents near the structure, which lifeguard services flagged as an escalating safety hazard rather than an amenity [R2][R3][R4]. The comparable Boscombe reef (same designer, ASR Ltd) showed geotextile containers physically degrading within about two years in a shallow marine setting, pointing to a possible generic durability weakness in ASR's geotextile-container approach beyond Mount Maunganui alone [R9]. No source fetched for this dossier gives a bag-fabric spec, marine-growth data, or storm-event log specific to Mount Maunganui's failure mode, beyond the general "scouring" and "sand leaked from some bags while others were buried in sand" description [R2].

## Unexpected results

The reef's most consequential unexpected effect was safety-negative rather than safety-positive: instead of the hoped-for amenity, it measurably worsened rip-current frequency and intensity near a popular swimming beach, to the point that Bay of Plenty Regional Council deputy chief executive Eddie Grogan stated "the reef's expected positive effects have not been realised. It's also generated some unforeseen effects, including creating a large scour hole which affects waves and currents, increasing the frequency and intensity of rips which pose a serious risk to swimmers in the popular Tay Street area" [R3]. Mount Lifeguard Service and Omanu Surf Lifesaving Club both had to increase monitoring in the area because of this [R6]. Local surf lifesaving bodies ultimately called the project a "white elephant" that caused "more headaches than success stories" [R6]. A second unexpected result was the reef's very slow, incomplete "removal": geotextile fabric attributable to the structure was still surfacing and washing onto the beach nearly a decade after the 2014 removal project was declared complete, in January 2023 [R12] — i.e. the structure was not, in practice, fully taken out of the coastal system even after the council's formal removal programme.

## What could have been done better

**Authors'/officials' view:** Council staff and the independent specialist report that Bay of Plenty Regional Council commissioned before deciding on removal framed the core problem as a structure that could not deliver its promised benefits while actively creating a new hazard (rips), concluding removal was the only responsible option after weighing status-quo, removal, and repair/restoration [R3]. ASR's own Dr Shaw Mead acknowledged the project had been "over-sold and under-delivered" — an implicit admission that expectations (and possibly the original pitch to funders/public) were not matched to what a half-built, under-volume reef could deliver [R7].

**Surfers'/locals' view:** Veteran local surfer Derek Winter framed it as a trust-and-value failure rather than a purely technical one — that the young people and the community "were sold a con or a lemon" [R7] — implying the gap between what was promised (world-class waves, 30x return on cost) and what was delivered (a rideable break "three or four times" since completion) was itself the central failure, independent of the specific engineering cause.

No source fetched gives a specific engineering fix (e.g., "should have used bigger bags," "should have built to full 6,000 m³ before opening," "should have dredged a channel") in Mount Maunganui's own case; the closest is the implicit conclusion, drawn by comparing design (5,000–6,000 m³) to built (2,800 m³), that finishing construction to the full designed volume was the design team's and Trust's own unmet target [R1][R2].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Tauranga/Mount Maunganui is a micro-tidal to low-meso-tidal harbour coast: the tide-table data source shows high tides ranging roughly 1.5–2.1 m and lows ranging roughly −0.1–0.6 m over a monthly spring/neap cycle (max recorded high 2.1 m, min recorded low −0.1 m); the source does not itself label specific days "spring" or "neap" or give a single average range figure, so those descriptors are this dossier's own characterisation of the underlying data, not a quoted figure [R11]. This is a genuinely tidal environment — meaningfully larger than the near-zero, sub-0.5 m Mediterranean tidal range at Haifa/Israel that Lior's own outreach material argues is an advantage for reef stability (see Lior's notes: "Israel is a unique place to build such a reef — the tide conditions here allow high stability and natural long-term maintenance" [R14]). What transfers directly to Israel is not the tide range but the two failure modes documented here: (1) under-building relative to design volume (only ~47–56% of the designed sand volume was actually placed [R1][R2]) as a root cause of underperformance, and (2) a geotextile-container reef's capacity to unintentionally amplify rip currents near a public swimming beach [R2][R3][R4] — a safety risk that would need explicit hydrodynamic modelling before any similar structure is proposed for an Israeli beach. The fact that Mount Maunganui's higher tidal range did *not* save the project from failure (the failure was volume/construction-driven, not tide-driven) is itself a useful data point against overstating Israel's low-tide advantage as sufficient on its own.
