## Motivation and who pushed for it

Palm Beach is described repeatedly as the Gold Coast's most erosion-prone beach — narrower than neighbouring beaches, with the coastal road running close to the shore [R11][R16]. The project has a long political history: in 2004 the council proposed a beach-protection scheme for Palm Beach that included an artificial reef near 21st Avenue as part of three parallel "dog bone" reefs; that scheme was **not built**, defeated by a community "no reef" protest campaign, with the surfing community itself among the objectors [R10][R16]. The successful 2013–2019 project is Phase 2 of the "Palm Beach Shoreline Project," following a 2017 sand-nourishment Phase 1 [R5][R8].

The project's re-launch is tied to a run of severe storms: three years of serious storm damage in 2013, a 2014 Climate Council report flagging Gold Coast sea-level-rise vulnerability, adoption of the council's Ocean Beaches Strategy in 2015, and — closer to construction — Cyclone Oma battering Palm Beach and Narrowneck in early 2019 [R12]. Gold Coast Mayor **Tom Tate** was the primary political champion and spokesman throughout: at the 2018 approval he said "We want Palm Beach to be enjoyed by everyone and to achieve this, we need to invest wisely in the shoreline project" [R16]; at commencement, "it is a wonderful day, finally, to be able to say we are going to commence this artificial reef" [R12]; and afterwards he framed the reef's job explicitly as erosion control, not wave-making: "The artificial reef is designed to control wave energy before it reaches the shore, resulting in a build-up of sand that will act as a buffer during storm season and swell events" [R8].

Local surfers were split. The **Palm Beach Board Riders Club**, through spokesman **Mike Gifford**, was skeptical, worrying that the accompanying sand nourishment would "stop actual waves breaking on Palm Beach" and complaining that the council had not adequately explained the reef concept to the club. Re-checking R16 directly in this pass found these Gifford quotes are not from Swellnet's own 29 Aug 2018 reporting — they originate from an April 2016 Brisbane Times article ("Palm Beach Board Riders Club says the reef is too far off shore to make any impact on waves"), reproduced verbatim by a reader ("Ted from the moon") in a comment on the Swellnet page; no separate Brisbane Times URL was found in this pass, so the quotes are cited here via the Swellnet page that reproduces them [R16]. Separately, civil engineer **Shane Abel** (commenting on the same Swellnet page, not Gifford) worried the reef could disrupt sand movement north to Burleigh Heads and publicly proposed an alternative, removable reef design [R16]. Recreational fishers raised concerns about silting of natural reef and estuary systems and about the impact on a well-used fishing spot [R16].

## Design as planned

DHI Australia won the Concept Design Study by open tender in 2013; principal engineer Simon Mortensen was project manager and lead designer [R3][R18]. The design process combined empirical and computational methods: Mortensen describes starting "with an idea for a shape," personally surfing Palm Beach and other reference breaks, then iterating with numerical CFD modelling (DHI's MIKE 21 SM shoreline-morphology module using 30 years of historical wave/current data, a 3D VOF model in OpenFOAM, and DHI's in-house OptiSurf surf-simulation software to validate surfing and safety performance) plus physical (wave-tank) model testing for rock-sizing validation [R3][R18]. Royal HaskoningDHV's parallel/follow-on role covered a "design reference report" (data capture, coastal-process modelling, environmental assessment) and a "Working with Nature" sand-placement approach using "innovative pattern placements to mimic natural rhythmic sand bar formations" [R5].

The design deliberately sited the reef "in the lee of a natural reef" offshore of Palm Beach to exploit an existing natural wave-focusing zone [R3][R13]. The stated design target was a right-hand wave, "suitable for average [to advanced] surfers with accessible takeoffs and extended wall length," with a secondary short, more intense left expected on larger swells [R3][R13][R15][R17]. Rock (rather than geotextile sand containers, as used at Narrowneck) was chosen explicitly for a 100+-year coastal-engineering track record, because small rock elements allow precise control of the built shape, because rock crevasses were expected to provide marine habitat, and because geotextile bags have a documented history of tearing/failure with removal costs exceeding placement costs [R3]. Design freeboard was set at a minimum 1.5 m below mean sea level at the reef's highest point, with 60 cm depth at lowest astronomical tide, based on six years of local swell data showing east–to–east/southeast swells as the dominant direction [R2][R15]. Six months of construction (originally slated to run through October 2019) was timed for the calmer autumn/winter ocean-conditions window [R9].

Mortensen explicitly frames the exercise as a compromise: "It is always a compromise with these things. Especially since the main focus was coastal protection" [R3] — i.e., the plan never promised a world-class, dedicated surf reef; it promised measurable wave-focusing/surf amenity as a secondary deliverable layered on top of a primary erosion-control structure.

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

The built reef matches the broadly planned footprint (rock berm, ~270 m offshore off 19th Avenue) and the intended dominant right-hand wave, but several sources flag execution issues not evident in the design promise:

- **Shallower than comfortable at low tide.** Swellnet's July 2019 first-look (at ~50% completion) reported the reef "alarmingly shallow, with one rock even breaking the surface," and a forum commenter ("wildenstein8") described it as going "from too shallow to too deep really quickly" across the tide cycle, i.e., a narrower-than-hoped tidal window of good surfability [R15].
- **Timeline held essentially to plan, not ahead of it.** Re-checked directly in this pass: Swellnet's own 23 August 2019 update reported the reef only ~50% complete and "still on track for an October finish" [R17] — this contradicts an earlier draft's claim that construction "finished on 19 August 2019... ahead of schedule," which has been removed as unsupported (no source checked, including R1 and R9, uses the phrase "ahead of schedule"). The project was finalised and certified in September 2019, consistent with — not ahead of — the original 2019 target [R1][R5].
- **Design-credit/role ambiguity.** As noted in Quick Facts, three different organisations (DHI, Royal HaskoningDHV, Bluecoast) each publicly claim a lead or core role in delivering the design and construction QA, with no single source reconciling the handoffs between them — a sign that credit/scope questions were unsettled or that the design/build chain changed over the 2013–2019 span. Not established as a "cost overrun" or "construction problem" by any source found, only as a documentation gap.
- **Cost and dimension figures vary slightly by source** (AU$18.2M vs AU$18.3M; 80 m vs 144 m width; 270 m vs 330 m offshore — see Quick Facts) — plausibly rounding/measurement-basis differences between the council's headline figure and individual consultants' own project pages, but not explained by any source.

## Outcome

**Surfing.** On its first meaningful east swell test (July 2019, reef ~50% built), the reef broke 4–5 ft waves all weekend, with rides running up to ~30 m on the better sets, and a local surfer told Swellnet approvingly that "none of the reef's real estate is wasted in a closeout section — the takeoff area is small but it has a surprisingly long wall" (re-verified against R15 in this pass: a surfer's own words as reported by Swellnet, not Swellnet's own editorial voice) [R15]. By completion, Swellnet's Stu Nettle described the reef as effectively "bending and focussing the incoming swell lines," producing a consistent right (its main design target) with a short, thick left on bigger swells, and noted it can handle at least double-overhead southeast swells [R6][R17]. The Inertia's broader 2020s survey of stone artificial reefs groups Palm Beach with Cable Station and Burkitts Reef as one of three Australian rock reefs delivering "modest, measurable improvements in the quality of waves" without becoming "world-class" breaks — i.e., a qualified surfing success, explicitly contrasted with failed geotextile-bag reef projects elsewhere [R18].

**Coastal protection.** ABC News reported the reef retained approximately 550,000 m³ of sand over its first two years of operation [R9] — the clearest available quantitative coastal-protection outcome metric found. Coastal engineer Evan Watterson (quoted by ABC, firm affiliation not stated in that article) argued such structures compare favourably to plain beach nourishment "over a 30-year period" (his exact words, re-verified against the source in this pass); the article separately paraphrases him as saying reef-style protection brings other longer-term benefits worth the higher upfront cost — re-checking the source found no exact quote matching "save millions of dollars worth of damage to beaches over the long term" (an earlier draft's quotation marks around that phrase have been removed as non-verbatim), and no Palm-Beach-specific damage-avoided dollar figure was given [R9].

**Ecology.** Marine-habitat creation is asserted by both Bluecoast [R1] and Raised Water Research [R2] but with zero ecological survey data, fish-species counts, or before/after monitoring results found in any source fetched — "(unreferenced — needs source)" for any actual ecological outcome.

**Longevity / damage.** No storm-damage, structural-failure, or removal reports were found for Palm Beach Reef specifically in any source fetched, including a targeted (unsuccessful) search for Cyclone Alfred (March 2025) impacts. Re-checking R17 directly in this pass found it is not a "completion article" at all — it is a 23 August 2019 video update posted while the reef was still only ~50% complete. It contains reader debate, not reported "post-completion concerns": Swellnet editor Stu Nettle, replying to a commenter, restated that the reef's "primary purpose is to halt erosion, surfing amenity is second"; a single forum commenter separately posted an exaggerated, joking report of a shark sighting ("Predatory Pirate Shark circles Palmy Reef") with no corroboration or follow-up found anywhere. An earlier draft's characterisation of these as serious, data-backed "post-completion concerns" about erosion and shark sightings overstated this informal forum banter and has been corrected [R17].

**Awards.** The project won the IPWEAQ (Institute of Public Works Engineering Australasia, Queensland) 2019 Excellence Award for Innovation and a 2020 Excellence Award for Coastal Engineering, per Royal HaskoningDHV's own project page [R5].

## Why it worked / why it failed

This dossier finds Palm Beach Reef characterised across sources as a qualified/partial success rather than a clean win or a failure, for these engineering reasons drawn from the sources:

- **Material choice (rock vs. geotextile) is repeatedly cited as the key differentiator from failed reef projects elsewhere.** DHI's own designer argues rock's long engineering track record, shape-control precision, and habitat value outweigh geotextile's history of bag tearing and expensive removal [R3]; ICM's comparative piece and The Inertia both independently credit stone construction (vs. sandbags) as the reason Palm Beach, Cable Station, and Burkitts delivered modest surf gains where sandbag reefs commonly failed [R13][R18].
- **Siting behind a natural reef to pre-focus wave energy** was a deliberate design choice credited with helping the structure punch above its size for surf quality [R3][R13].
- **Crest depth/freeboard tuned to tide** (1.5 m below MSL / 60 cm at LAT) was meant to balance rideable wave height against safety, but the shallow, fast-changing tidal window reported at first testing suggests the margin was tight in practice, producing user complaints about the reef swinging quickly between "too shallow" and "too deep" [R15].
- **Funding realism was a stated regret of the lead designer himself:** Mortensen said in a December 2019 interview that "we would have been more realistic in our budgeting on how long it would actually take to work on this reef. The City got a good deal" — implying the consultancy's own time/budget estimate for the multi-year design process was, in hindsight, too optimistic relative to what the work actually required, even though the final build itself reportedly finished ahead of schedule [R3].
- **Surf-first design brief vs. Narrowneck's protection-first brief** is the clearest engineering-cause explanation in the sources for Palm Beach outperforming Narrowneck as a wave: ICM's own comparison states plainly "when the objective is surfing, the design can be more focused and effective," at a materially higher unit cost (~AU$700/m³ at Palm Beach vs. ~AU$30/m³ at Narrowneck) [R13].

## Unexpected results

- **Rock breaking the surface at low tide**, flagged by Swellnet as a possible safety/design concern that "locals question," beyond what the freeboard specification implied to observers before construction [R15].
- **A single joking forum comment reported a shark sighting** ("Predatory Pirate Shark circles Palmy Reef") on a Swellnet page from 23 Aug 2019 — re-checked directly in this pass and found to be one commenter's exaggerated aside, not a reported increase in sightings; not corroborated by any other source, so this dossier no longer treats it as a real outcome data point [R17]. (Dropped: an earlier draft's separate claim of "a second round of localised erosion concern" emerging after completion — re-checking R17 found no such reported concern, only reader debate about the reef's stated erosion-control purpose.)
- **Crowding complaints**: because the reef breaks predominantly as a right-hander in a region already dominated by right-hand points and reefs, some in the surf community criticised it for adding to an existing imbalance rather than diversifying wave options, and for concentrating crowds at an already busy beach [R17].
- **A pre-existing, unrelated 2004 community backlash** ("no reef" campaign that killed an earlier reef proposal at the same beach) is itself a somewhat unexpected data point about local social license for this kind of structure at this exact site, given that the later, larger 2019 project proceeded with comparatively less public opposition reported [R10][R16].

## What could have been done better

**Experts'/engineers' view:** The lead designer's own retrospective criticism is about process, not physical design: Simon Mortensen's stated regret is that the consultancy underestimated how long the multi-year design phase would actually take, i.e., budgeting/scheduling realism for the design process itself, not the built structure's performance [R3]. Separately, civil engineer Shane Abel argued before construction that a removable reef design would have been preferable to the fixed boulder approach that was ultimately built. Re-checked directly against R16 in this pass: his own words were not "oversimplified" (an earlier draft's quotation marks around that word have been removed as non-verbatim) — he wrote that "whatever they build in the ocean should be removable and 8 tonnes of rock isn't," and sarcastically described the chosen approach as "much simpler to just dump boulders in the ocean, not a great deal of thought required." This is a minority/outside engineering opinion (he was not part of the winning design team) rather than the project engineers' own assessment [R16].

**Surfers'/locals' view:** The Palm Beach Board Riders Club (Mike Gifford), in the 2016 remarks reproduced on the R16 Swellnet page (see Motivation section above for the provenance correction), felt stakeholder consultation with surfers was inadequate. Post-construction surfer commentary (Swellnet forum) focused on the tide-sensitivity problem — the narrow window between "too shallow" and "too deep" — as the main thing that, in practice, limits how consistently good the wave is, implying a design tweak to crest depth or a wider profile might have produced a more forgiving tidal window [R15]. Some surfers also felt the community would have been better served by a wave shape that added a different type of break (e.g. a left, or a beach-break-style peak) rather than another right, given the Gold Coast's existing abundance of right-hand points [R17].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

**Tide range.** Palm Beach, Gold Coast is a microtidal, semi-diurnal site: a live 7-day tide forecast fetched for this dossier showed high tides of roughly 1.14–1.61 m and lows of roughly −0.05–0.19 m, i.e., an illustrative tidal range of ~1.5 m for that week [R14] — consistent with the reef's own design freeboard spec of "1.5 m below MSL, 60 cm at lowest astronomical tide" [R15], which implies the design engineers were working with a broadly similar order-of-magnitude tide range. The Mediterranean coast at Haifa is essentially micro-tidal to negligible-tidal (well under 0.5 m typical range — not independently re-verified in this pass, but consistent with Lior's own notes elsewhere in the project citing Israel's small tidal range as an advantage for reef stability). This means Palm Beach's core engineering challenge — keeping a fixed-crest structure inside a workable "not too shallow, not too deep" window across the tide cycle — is *more* forgiving at Haifa than it was at Palm Beach, since Haifa's tide swing is smaller; a crest-depth choice that felt "alarmingly shallow" at Palm Beach's ~1.5 m tide range would vary far less at Haifa across a tidal cycle.

**Wave climate.** Palm Beach's design leaned on six years of swell data dominated by east-to-southeast ocean swell in the 1.5 m+ (open-ocean) range, arriving at a site with an existing natural offshore reef to help pre-focus the swell [R3][R15]. Haifa's Mediterranean wave climate is smaller and less consistent than the Gold Coast's Pacific swell exposure (a difference already noted as the core reason, in Lior's own notes, that a Haifa reef would need to *actively increase* wave height rather than merely reshape already-large incoming swell — making the Haifa project conceptually closer to an artificial wave pool than to a coastal-protection reef like Palm Beach) [R19][R20].

**What transfers.** (1) The rock-over-geotextile material lesson — durability and shape-control precision — is directly transferable regardless of wave climate. (2) The value of siting behind/near an existing natural feature to pre-focus energy is a transferable design principle, though Haifa may lack an equivalent natural offshore reef to exploit. (3) The tide-window lesson (crest depth must hold a comfortable surf window across the full tide range) is *less* of a constraint for Haifa given its smaller tidal range, meaning a Haifa design has more freedom in choosing crest depth without the "too shallow to too deep" complaint reported at Palm Beach. (4) The cost lesson — Palm Beach's higher per-m³ cost (~AU$700/m³) versus Narrowneck's cheaper geotextile approach (~AU$30/m³) reflects the cost premium of prioritising surf quality in the design brief, which is directly relevant to budgeting a Haifa reef if surf quality (not just coastal protection) is the primary goal, as Lior's notes suggest it would be [R13][R19].
