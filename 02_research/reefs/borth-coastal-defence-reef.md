## Motivation and who pushed for it

Borth sits on a mobile shingle bank in Cardigan Bay, and its 1970s timber groyne and breastwork sea defences were judged inadequate: "ordinary storms affect properties every winter with overtopping flooding through homes and on to the high street. Few homeowners can get insurance," per Borth councillor Ray Quant [R2]. A council feasibility study (referenced in community materials as having found existing defences "within 10 years of failure" with "potentially catastrophic losses" possible) triggered the process, and the council held a 150-resident "visioning meeting" in 2001 to shape the response [R4]. Ceredigion County Council's coast and rivers engineer, Rhodri Llwyd, said the scheme "has been 15 years in the making," and that "the community wanted to retain as much of an open beach as possible; they absolutely didn't want a wall, so the idea of a reef was born" [R1][R2]. Royal Haskoning's project manager, Alice Johnson, said "the design of the scheme has been significantly influenced by the people of Borth, who have strongly held opinions of what they want to see on their doorstep" [R1][R2]. A separate, earlier-dated feasibility study — indexed by ResearchGate under the title "Borth Multi-Purpose reef for Coastal Protection and Amenity" [R10] — was carried out for Ceredigion County Council and completed in May 2004, explicitly to test whether a reef could be folded into the coastal-protection scheme to add surfing amenity and a wider beach on top of its primary protection role. The Borth Community website independently confirms this same study, its May 2004 completion date, and its stated purpose in near-identical wording [R4]. Community consultation events in December 2009 (c.120 attendees) and June 2010 continued to shape the design before construction [R4].

## Design as planned

The preferred option chosen after comparing "Technical Advantages, Economic Benefits, Amenity Value, Environmental Impacts and Safety Considerations" was "Rock Beach Control Structures with a Multi-Purpose Reef and Beach Nourishment" — this exact wording and the underlying evaluation process are confirmed verbatim on the Borth Community website [R4], corroborating the same facts as indexed for the (session-inaccessible) ResearchGate feasibility-study record [R10]. As detailed by Royal Haskoning/Ceredigion County Council, Phase 1 combined: a submerged multi-purpose reef near the Craig y Delyn cliffs at the beach's southern end (chosen there specifically because the community wanted an open beach and unobstructed views), two rock groynes and two rock breakwaters further along the frontage to retain shingle, and beach nourishment (originally planned as 150,000 m³ of imported shingle) [R1][R4]. The design was engineered to a 1-in-100-year storm standard while remaining adaptable to future climate change [R4]. The reef's job was explicitly dual: "waves will break over the reef, reducing energy to protect the beach from erosion, and encouraging the development of a broader beach inshore," while being "shaped to help swell waves break, creating an additional surfing facility" as a secondary benefit layered on top of the coastal-protection design [R1][R2]. Numerous layout options were drawn up before the team settled on "Option 9A" to take forward to outline design [R1]. Detailed geotechnical design and 3D physical wave-basin modelling by HR Wallingford were used to size the rock armour before construction [R1].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Construction went ahead broadly along the planned lines — reef, groynes, breakwaters and beach nourishment — but with two significant, contractor-driven changes made to close a budget gap. First, the client's design already carried roughly £1M of cost overrun after physical-model testing showed extra rock was needed; BAM Nuttall's tender then came in £1M over that already-inflated budget (a combined ~£2M gap against the original number) [R1]. BAM Nuttall closed the gap with two value-engineering changes: (1) instead of importing the full 150,000 m³ of shingle originally specified, the contractor mined and reused the beach's own existing shingle, bulked it up with cheaper imported quarried material underneath, and put the original shingle back on top — "a substantial saving," per site agent Ray Jones [R1]; and (2) instead of building the offshore reef sections with floating marine plant as originally tendered, BAM Nuttall built a **temporary rock causeway** out to the reef site so it could use land-based excavators and dumper trucks instead, removing the need for barge-based construction for that part of the works [R1]. The causeway was itself intended to be removed once the reef was finished [R9]. Construction ran on a tight tidal window — site work from 6am to 9pm with a three-hour stoppage over high tide, since the reefs and much of the breakwaters sit below the high-tide waterline [R1][R2]. About 100 deliveries of rock/quarried material arrived by truck each day (each truck carrying c.30 t), plus barge deliveries of large armour rock from as far as Norway — more than 8,000 deliveries had been made by September 2011, with sea deliveries already complete at that point [R1]. Construction of the sea-defence works began under considerable time pressure: funding terms required at least £5M to be spent within the 2010/11 financial year, forcing a January 2011 start after "just one year to do all the detailed design" [R1].

## Outcome

Coastal protection: residents reported the new defences had already stopped two high tides from flooding their properties by December 2011, while the works were still substantially under construction [R2][R3]. The scheme is on record as intended to give 1-in-100-year storm protection [R4]. An unplanned side effect of the earthworks was the exposure of a prehistoric submerged forest (oak, pine, birch, willow and hazel stumps dated to roughly 1500 BCE, preserved in peat) associated with the Welsh legend of Cantre'r Gwaelod — the works made these visible at low tide in a way they hadn't been before [R6]. Surfing outcome: sources disagree on whether the reef has produced a genuinely rideable, reliable wave. Press coverage at construction time promised the reef would "provide excellent conditions for surfers who would help boost the local economy" [R2]. But the Stormrider Surf Guide — a surf-industry reference resource — still describes Borth, years after the reef's completion, as merely "**a proposed** artificial reef site" where "the coastal geography changes from beaches to cliffs and boulder reefs," rather than crediting it with a functioning, named surf break [R7]. Surf-Forecast.com's break-guide page rates Borth's surf quality at only 3.0/5 on a good day, rates it beginner-difficulty (1/5), and — as of this session's access — carries **no individual surfer reviews or first-hand quotes** about the reef itself [R8]. No wave-height, ride-length or surfable-days-per-year measurement for the Borth reef specifically was found in any source this session (unreferenced — needs source). Longevity/damage events, and any removal or major repair, are: not found in sources located this session.

## Why it worked / why it failed

For coastal protection, the scheme is treated by the sources found as a functioning success at least in its early years — it withstood December-2011 storms without flooding the properties it was built to protect, within months of substantial completion [R2][R3]. Design choices credited with this in the sources: graded, multi-tonne rock armour (up to 10 t pieces) sized via HR Wallingford's 3D physical modelling before construction, rather than off-the-shelf sizing [R1]; a reef+groyne+breakwater system working with beach nourishment rather than a rigid sea wall, explicitly chosen because a wall was community-rejected [R1][R2]; and a construction sequencing (temporary causeway, tidal work windows) that let the contractor place rock accurately below the high-tide line [R1]. For the surfing function, no source found this session explains in engineering terms (crest depth, slope, wave-focusing geometry) why the wave has or hasn't performed — this causal detail is not found. The surf-guide description of Borth as still only "a proposed" reef site, well after construction, is suggestive that the amenity function has not been widely recognised by the surf community as delivering a notable wave, but no direct technical post-mortem (of the kind that exists for Boscombe, e.g.) was located for Borth this session.

## Unexpected results

The exposure of the prehistoric petrified forest and its tie-in to the Cantre'r Gwaelod legend was an unplanned, publicised side effect of the earthworks [R6]. No sources found this session document fishing-pressure changes, ecological studies, rip-current safety incidents, litigation, or measurable tourism-economy impact specifically tied to the Borth reef (unreferenced — needs source; treat as a genuine gap, not confirmed absence of any such effect).

## What could have been done better

No source located this session offers an explicit engineers'/authors' retrospective critique of the Borth scheme's design or execution (unlike, e.g., Boscombe's published Plymouth University post-mortem) — this is a genuine gap in what was found, not a confirmed absence of critique. The one available critical signal is indirect: the Stormrider Surf Guide's continued description of Borth as merely "a proposed artificial reef site" years after completion implies that, from a surfers'-view perspective, the reef has not become a recognised, reliably rideable wave in the way the pre-construction press promised [R7]; Surf-Forecast's low "consistency of surf" and quality scores for Borth point the same way [R8], though neither source explains what design choice (crest depth, shape, sand burial, etc.) is responsible. From a cost-management perspective, the scheme's own delivery already shows an authors'/engineers'-side lesson in the sources found: the construction tender came in over budget on top of a design that had already grown by ~£1M after physical-model testing, and the contractor had to redesign its construction method (a temporary causeway instead of floating plant) and its material sourcing (mining and reusing existing shingle instead of full import) specifically to close that gap — implying the original budget and/or design had under-priced the works as first specified [R1].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

Cardigan Bay/Aberystwyth (immediately adjacent to Borth) is **macrotidal**: spring tidal range is roughly 4.7–4.8 m (high spring tide c.5.4 m, low tide down to c.0.6 m) [R11]. (An earlier draft of this dossier also gave a specific neap-tide range of "3.8–4.1 m," but that figure does not appear anywhere on the cited R11 page — it has been removed as unsupported; the neap range at Aberystwyth is meaningfully smaller than the spring range, but no reliably sourced neap figure was found this session — unreferenced, needs source.) This spring range alone is drastically larger than the Israeli Mediterranean coast (Haifa/Ashkelon/Netanya etc.), which is effectively **microtidal**, with a tidal range on the order of tens of centimetres. That difference matters directly for transferability: a reef crest depth and rock-armour design tuned by HR Wallingford's physical modelling to work across a ~4.7 m tidal swing at Borth [R1] cannot be reused as-is on Israel's coast — the same structure would sit permanently near a single water level in Israel rather than alternately surfacing and submerging by several metres through the tidal cycle, changing both its wave-breaking performance and its exposure/maintenance regime. What does transfer conceptually: the core political lesson that a community explicitly chose a submerged multi-purpose reef over a hard sea wall specifically to keep an open, usable beach and views [R1][R2][R4] — directly analogous to debates Lior's other Israeli-coast files describe around geotubes/breakwaters at Ashkelon and Netanya (see project's Israel-track notes, not re-verified here). The value-engineering lesson (reusing existing shingle instead of full import, to control cost) is also a directly transferable coastal-engineering-management point regardless of tidal regime [R1].
