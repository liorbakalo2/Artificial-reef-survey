## Motivation and who pushed for it

The stated motivation is purely engineering/erosion-control, not tourism, politics, or surfing. Korea's east coast suffers severe winter erosion, with significant wave heights reaching up to 4 m, driven by wave attack, periodic tides, and currents linked to the East Korea Warm Current (EKWC) and North Korea Cold Current (NKCC); the erosion damages "shoreline scenic views and... public property," and the paper states Korea's Ministry of Marine Affairs & Fisheries (MMAF) "must apply shore protection methods every year" along this coast [R1]. The existing protection at the time was a near-shore breakwater built from tetrapod (precast concrete) units, which the authors frame as costly, heavy-equipment-dependent, and harder to build than shore-zone structures — this is the explicit reason a cheaper, faster geotextile-tube alternative was studied and piloted at Young-Jin [R1]. The push therefore reads as government/engineering-research led: a national shore-protection mandate (MMAF) combined with an academic/applied-research feasibility study (Oh at a rural/agricultural-corporation research institute; Shin at the University of Incheon), rather than a local tourism board, surf lobby, or elected official's initiative — no named politician, council, or community campaign appears in any source found [R1]. Lior's own project files do not mention Young-Jin beach at all outside of the single citation captured from the Bar & Drimer 2023 survey paper [R5].

## Design as planned

The design was chosen empirically: the authors ran a 2-D limit-equilibrium stability analysis together with hydraulic (physical) model tests of several design options, and concluded that a **double-lined geotextile tube installed with zero water depth above the crest** (i.e., the tube crest submerged just below the still-water line, about 1.0 m below the surface) was more stable and more effective at wave absorption than the other design plans tested [R1][R2][R3]. The structure was planned as a set of detached breakwaters — not attached to shore — sited in roughly 3.0 m of water, 90–100 m offshore [R1]. Two explicit design goals beyond wave attenuation are stated: keep the structure submerged so it would not "spoil the scenery," and keep it submerged/deep enough to "reduce navigational risks" for boats [R1]. A fabric "apron mat" (scour blanket) was planned under each tube to prevent undermining [R1]. No target wave height, storm-return-period, or design-life figure is given in the source (unreferenced — needs source).

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Construction matched the design as documented: eight tubes were installed as four double-lined "twin" units, each unit's component tube 50.0 m long and reaching 1.8 m effective height after filling, spaced 20.0 m apart along 240 m of the Young-Jin frontage, at the planned ~3.0 m water depth [R1]. Filling was hydraulic: a cutter-suction dredge pumped dredged nearshore sand into each tube via a 20 cm pipe in four sequential filling/dredging steps, with each individual tube reaching its final height in **under one hour** — the paper cites this speed of construction as the technology's most evident practical advantage [R1]. No cost overrun, phasing change, or construction-problem account is reported anywhere in the source; the paper describes a straightforward, on-plan pilot-scale build, and does not discuss whether additional tubes or a longer frontage were originally planned but not built (unreferenced — needs source for any design-vs-built discrepancy beyond what is stated here).

## Outcome

Field monitoring ran for 12 months after construction, tracking shoreline position at benchmark points (BM1–BM5) and water depth offshore [R1]. Results were mixed and location-specific along the same short (240 m) frontage:
- At **BM1**, the shoreline extended seaward by about 5.3 m during the first 3 months, then was **re-eroded** by tidal waves after month 3; the authors attribute this specifically to local changes in tidal-current direction and velocity caused by the geotube installation itself [R1].
- **BM3** and **BM4** showed the same extend-then-partially-re-erode pattern as BM1, but with a smaller magnitude of re-erosion [R1].
- **BM2** showed continuous seaward extension throughout the full 12 months, with no re-erosion recorded [R1].
- Averaged/headline figure reported in both the conference paper and its companion journal papers: the shoreline at Young-Jin beach was extended by about **2.4–7.6 m seaward** overall, and seabed sand gradually accumulated (water depth decreased) in the areas covered by the tubes [R1][R2][R3].
- Ecologically, after one year in service, **seaweed had colonized the submerged tube surfaces**; the authors interpret this as evidence the geotextile polymer is unlikely to harm marine life and could be "environmentally sustainable to the adjacent ecology" [R1].
- The authors explicitly caution that the structure's **long-term effectiveness "cannot be judged until more time has passed,"** noting the site is exposed to a number of severe storms typical of Korea's east coast, and that this makes short-term monitoring an unreliable predictor of durability [R1].

This is a shore-protection structure, not a surf reef, so no surf-quality metric applies; no independent damage-event, storm-survival, or removal record beyond the reported 12-month window was found in this research (unreferenced — needs source for any post-2006 status).

## Why it worked / why it failed

The paper treats "worked" and "failed" as depth/geometry-sensitive rather than binary: the double-lined, zero-water-depth-above-crest configuration was selected specifically because model testing showed it out-performed shallower or single-lined alternatives on stability and wave absorption [R1][R2][R3] — consistent with the broader Geotube literature's general finding (cited in the seed source for this dossier) that undersized tubes or loss of effectiveness from sand burial are the common failure modes for Geotube-based artificial-reef/breakwater projects elsewhere in the world [R4][R5]. The site-specific mixed result — accretion at BM2 versus extend-then-re-erode at BM1/BM3/BM4 — is explained by the authors themselves as a consequence of the structure's own effect on local tidal-current direction and velocity: in other words, even a correctly-designed, correctly-built geotube detached breakwater can locally redirect currents enough to undo its own benefit at some points along a short frontage, while still producing net accretion at others [R1].

As general regional context only (not confirmed to describe Young-Jin itself): later Korean investigative journalism on other east-coast submerged breakwaters (잠제/*jamje*) between Goseong and Samcheok — a different set of 16 named beaches that does not include Yeongjin — reports structures that have cracked, subsided, been buried by sand, or distorted currents so as to accelerate rather than prevent erosion, with coastal engineers there blaming inadequate site-specific modeling of current direction, wave angle, and seafloor topography before construction, plus a funding/maintenance gap between the central government (which builds) and cash-strapped local governments (which must maintain) [R7]. Because Young-Jin's own 1-year study already documents an unexplained local re-erosion event tied to current changes, this general regional critique is at least plausibly relevant to the same technology and coastline, but it is **not proof** of a specific failure at Young-Jin — no source found names Young-Jin in that investigation [R7].

## Unexpected results

- The **self-inflicted local re-erosion at BM1** (and, to a lesser degree, BM3/BM4) — the structure appears to have altered tidal-current direction/velocity enough to undo part of its own benefit at some points along the same 240 m stretch — is flagged by the authors themselves as an unanticipated finding [R1].
- **Ecological colonization**: seaweed established on the submerged tube surfaces within one year, which the authors treat as an unexpected but welcome outcome supporting an "environmentally sustainable" framing [R1].
- No fishing-pressure, safety-incident, litigation, or tourism-effect data was found for this specific site in any source reached (unreferenced — needs source; contrast with, e.g., Narrowneck Reef elsewhere in this project, where fishing pressure and anchor damage are documented).

## What could have been done better

**Authors' own view [R1]:** the paper's conclusion is cautious rather than corrective. The authors do not propose a design change; they simply state that long-term effectiveness cannot be judged from 12 months of data given the site's exposure to severe recurring storms, implicitly calling for longer monitoring rather than a different structure.

**Regional experts' view (general east-coast context, not confirmed specific to Young-Jin) [R7]:** coastal engineers commenting on other *jamje* (submerged breakwater) sites on the same coast argue that failures there stem from insufficient site-specific hydrodynamic and sediment-transport modeling before construction — i.e., stability analysis of the structure alone (which Young-Jin's designers did perform) is not sufficient without also modeling how the structure will change local current direction, wave angle, and seafloor topography. Given that Young-Jin's own data shows an unexplained current-driven re-erosion event at BM1, this critique is plausibly applicable, though it cannot be confirmed as the actual cause without a source that names Young-Jin directly.

**Surfers' view:** not found. Young-Jin/Yeongjin beach is not documented as a surfing location in any source reached in this research, and the structure is explicitly a shore-protection-only design (unlike, e.g., Narrowneck Reef in Australia, which combined coastal protection with a deliberate surf-amenity purpose elsewhere in this project).

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

**Tide range:** Korea's east coast (the "East Sea"/Sea of Japan side, where Young-Jin sits) is a small/diurnal-tide environment, with published amplitude figures of about 0.5 m near the North Korean shore rising to about 1.5 m at the Korean peninsula's southern tip; the tidal pattern on Korea's east shore specifically is diurnal (once-daily), not semi-diurnal [R9]. Young-Jin's own exact local tidal range is not stated in the engineering papers themselves [R1]. Israel's Mediterranean coast is understood within this project to be similarly small-tide, but no independently sourced figure for Israeli/Haifa tidal range was found in this research session — this is a pre-existing, explicitly flagged gap in Lior's own source files [R11]. Lior's own material (a draft outreach email to the Israeli NGO Zalul) argues that Israel's tide conditions are an advantage for reef stability and natural long-term maintenance — this is **Lior's own opinion**, not an independently sourced engineering claim, and is labelled as such here [R11].

**What transfers:** Young-Jin is the clearest example in this project's source material of a geotube used purely as a **detached shore-protection breakwater** — not a surfing/ASR reef — directly analogous in stated purpose to Israel's own 2018 Ashkelon geotube project, which the seed source describes as also aimed at mitigating beach and cliff erosion rather than surfing [R4][R5]. The most transferable engineering lesson from Young-Jin's own 12-month dataset is that submerged-geotube performance can be **spatially uneven over a very short frontage** (accretion at one monitoring point 20–40 m from re-erosion at another) and can be **partly self-undermining** if the structure changes local tidal/current flow — a relevant caution against assuming uniform, predictable accretion behind any similarly short structure proposed for the Haifa coast [R1]. Young-Jin's designers also explicitly chose a fully submerged, near-surface ("zero water depth above crest") geometry partly to avoid "spoiling the scenery" [R1] — directly relevant to the stated Haifa-project goal (elsewhere in Lior's own material) of protecting the coast without blocking the horizon view.
