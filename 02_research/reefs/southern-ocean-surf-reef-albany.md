## Motivation and who pushed for it

Middleton Beach historically produced "unreliable and unsurfable waves for much of the year" — mostly straight-handed closeouts — despite being the closest beach to central Albany [R1][R6]. The push for an artificial reef came from local surfers, not from a coastal-erosion or port-mitigation problem: this is explicitly framed by Swellnet as unusual among artificial surf reefs — "Albany's reef is unique in that its sole and primary purpose is surfing... the success of Albany is judged by wave quality and nothing else," unlike Narrowneck or Palm Beach, Gold Coast, where erosion control was the primary driver and surf was a secondary benefit [R8].

Advocacy ran for roughly three decades. Long-time local surfer Peter Bolt says he returned to Albany in 1990 and began working on a feasibility study "for the artificial surf reef at Middleton Beach... We just felt that's where we needed the juniors to have something decent to surf... in town where they can get access to it" [R6][R8]. The Albany Boardriders club and the now-defunct Southern Ocean Surfers Boardriders Club, plus local politicians, kept the campaign alive through decades of setbacks, initially working from a design based on geotextile sand-bag technology that Bolt himself says would likely have failed: "when they have been used to build reefs or protect shorelines, they failed because they split, they settle, they move... So it was not bad timing in some ways, looking back in hindsight" that funding was not available for the original geotextile design [R6].

The political breakthrough came in 2017 when Greg Stocks was elected Mayor of Albany and both major state parties pledged $5 million each during the WA state election campaign; a City of Albany Steering Group had already commissioned an updated Feasibility Study (completed July 2015) [R3][R6][R2]. Bluecoast Consulting Engineers were then contracted for the investigative/design work. The final funding piece came in 2022 when both major federal parties pledged $5 million each [R8][R2]. A community feasibility survey conducted in **February 2015** (not 2016 — corrected from an earlier draft) recorded strong local support: 732 responses (the largest response of any City of Albany community survey), with 90% support for the reef; a follow-up survey in September 2020 recorded continued 87% support. Directly confirmed by fetching the City of Albany's own May 2023 project FAQ PDF, which states this verbatim — this replaces an earlier draft's low-confidence, WebSearch-only citation [R15].

## Design as planned

The 2015 Feasibility Study's stated design objective was "the creation of a consistent, surfable wave, which maximises available swell conditions" for economic and demographic benefit to Albany [R3]. The subsequent Detailed Design (executive summary completed by 2020) set out specific performance targets: a left-hand break, rides of up to 100 m under average swell conditions, and surfable waves for 41% of the year over the reef itself, with additional surf opportunities on the beach inshore of the structure [R3][R2]. The design was explicitly aimed at beginner-to-intermediate surfers, per the EPA WA referral description: "The ASR will be designed to target beginner-intermediate surfers" [R9].

Design work involved "an iterative process involving thorough numerical, physical and conceptual modelling," with location "optimised based on existing seabed variability, user accessibility, shoreline response, wave climate and local ecology" and shape "optimised to improve wave breaking characteristics, promote user safety, minimise coastal impacts and reduce construction costs" [R3]. A 2018 Environmental Impact Assessment (BMT), cited in a Swellnet forum post by a user identified as "steveb" (a self-described offshore/coastal-structures engineer, not the original preparer of the EIA), reportedly concluded the reef "would not have a significant impact on coastal processes" and that in all shoreline-modelling simulations run, "there appeared to be a widening of the shoreline" — i.e. beach accretion was expected in the lee of the reef [R8]. Business-case modelling built around the design projected a Net Present Value of $57.9 million and a Benefit-Cost Ratio of 6.69, driven mainly by ~10 committed annual surfing events and an estimated uplift of ~3,500 overnight tourist visits per year [R3]. (Note: an earlier, May 2023 City of Albany FAQ cites a different, lower interim figure from the same business case lineage — NPV $19.4 million, BCR 3.25, prepared by Keston Technologies — which the later $57.9M/6.69 figure on the current completed-project page appears to supersede; both are genuine City of Albany figures from different project stages, not a contradiction to resolve [R15][R3].)

Physical modelling was used pre-construction to simulate storm events and test structure stability, with the finished design intended to be "low maintenance," requiring only inspection and monitoring rather than active upkeep [R3].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

The reef as built matches the detailed design specification closely on paper: 165 m long, up to 110 m wide, crest at −1.0 m AHD, 62,800 tonnes of rock in three graded layers (see Quick facts) [R3]. Construction ran from roughly October 2024 (quarry establishment) through March 2025 (survey/practical completion), with the main placement phase in January–March 2025 carried out by a specialist marine engineering crew from New Zealand using precision geospatial equipment to place the three rock layers [R3][R2][R8]. News coverage describes the build period loosely as "six weeks" (Wikipedia, citing the Albany Advertiser's construction-start report) or "close to six months"/"approximately 6 months" counting full marine-plant mobilisation and quarry production (ABC News, Swellnet) — the two descriptions refer to different phases of the same programme rather than being in outright conflict [R1][R2][R8].

Once open, the built reef performed noticeably better than the design's 41%-surfability target — described by multiple outlets as "far higher" than the projected figure, though none of the sources fetched give a precise revised percentage [R2][R6]. Design engineer Evan Watterson, who reportedly surfed the reef himself on opening day, is quoted (via a search-engine synthesis of coverage attributed to the ABC) as saying: "It was pretty much pumping. The brief was to amplify the wave climate as much as we could, limit any erosion, and make it a regular surfing break" [R2 — quote captured via search synthesis of ABC coverage; exact article paragraph not independently confirmed by direct fetch].

The most significant post-opening change to the "as-built" structure came in 2026: the City of Albany spent more than AUD $20,000 repositioning 18 rocks on the reef, in a program described by the Albany Advertiser as a "successful bid to reduce user injuries" (article dated 11 March 2026) [R11 — a direct fetch of the Albany Advertiser page confirmed the headline, byline, "18 rocks," publish date and injury-reduction rationale, but the article body is paywalled; the specific $20,000 cost figure is corroborated only via a search-engine synthesis of that same paywalled article, not an independent second source, so it is flagged accordingly. An earlier draft mis-cited this to R7 (Beachgrit) and "R8-adjacent" — corrected: R7 is an October 2025 article that predates this March 2026 event by five months and does not mention it at all].

## Outcome

**Surfing quality.** Widely and consistently reported as one of the few unambiguous artificial-surf-reef success stories. Swellnet's assessment: "the reef performs far better than expectations" [R8]. The Inertia and ABC both quote Peter Bolt saying the wave is "a bit more challenging than expected... We were aiming for an intermediate wave, which it is when the swell is smaller, but as it gets bigger, it breaks out there and it's fairly shallow. So it's intermediate-to-advanced on most days" [R6][R2]. Local surfer Cameron Warburton is quoted by ABC News: "It's a really high-performance wave," and that the reef is "creating a culture around town that's really good" [R2]. Raised Water Research describes the wave as a left-hand break, average face height 0.8–1.3 m, rides of 100–120 m, "easier open face, good for turns," breaking on the reef proper roughly 40% of the year, with larger swells becoming "notably more challenging" [R10]. A local Swellnet forum poster ("soggydog," an Albany local) reported 7 people in the water on a "solid swell" afternoon session as a sign of ongoing (if modest) crowding: "bit crowded on forecast solid swells... We all know each other" [R8].

However, a persistent, repeated forum question (posed multiple times between August and October 2025 by user "steveb," citing wave-buoy data showing Midds is under 1 m "90% of the time") is whether the reef breaks well on small, everyday swells rather than only on the larger swells shown in most published photos and video — to which local "soggydog" answered on 11 October 2025: "No, but it has created reliable banks either side when it's too small for the reef proper. The reef needs a bit of swell to get it working" [R8]. Steveb followed up arguing that the 2015 Feasibility Study's specific target of "1m peelers 70% of the time" (his citation) "isn't happening," and offered an engineering explanation (see "Why it worked / why it failed" below) [R8].

**Coastal protection / ecology.** Not a stated primary goal for this reef (unlike Narrowneck or Palm Beach). The only ecology/coastal-process claim found is the secondhand citation (via a Swellnet forum post, not independently verified against the primary EIA document) that a 2018 BMT shoreline-modelling report found "no significant impact on coastal processes" and predicted beach widening in the lee of the reef [R8]. No monitoring results, post-construction beach-width surveys, or fisheries/ecology data were found in the sources fetched — flagged (unreferenced — needs source).

**Longevity / damage events.** No storm-damage, rock-scatter, or structural-failure events were found in the sources fetched, ~14 months after opening as of this research. The one confirmed post-opening intervention was the March 2026 repositioning of 18 rocks for user-safety reasons, not storm damage [R11, paywalled Albany Advertiser].

**Injuries / safety.** See "Unexpected results" below — this is the dominant "outcome" theme in the more recent (Aug 2025–Mar 2026) coverage found.

## Why it worked / why it failed

Multiple sources attribute the reef's relative success — as distinct from earlier-generation artificial reefs worldwide — to its use of layered, engineered rock rather than geotextile sand-filled bags. Swellnet's designer interview framing states plainly: "Every example of a geotextile reef has failed due to the bags splitting, shifting, or settling," contrasting this with Albany's rock construction, precision-placed with geospatial equipment in three graded layers (belly/core/armour) [R8][R3]. Peter Bolt makes the same point about the original 1990s geotextile design concept never having been funded: "it was not bad timing in some ways, looking back in hindsight," since geotextile reefs of that era "failed... they split, they settle, they move" [R6][R8].

A structural engineer posting on Swellnet under the handle "steveb" (self-identified as having "spent a lifetime involved in the planning, detail design, construction, and maintenance of public recreation facilities," and the author of a separate concept called "Offshore Surf Reefs") offers the most detailed causal analysis found in the sources, made in direct response to reported injuries and the small-swell performance question:
- Rock-built reefs cannot achieve the same shape accuracy as a sand-formed bank of identical design contours, because "the boulders need to be massive for stability but cannot produce accurate enough contours." Trying to place rock shallow enough to break small swells "compromises user safety."
- Rock structures are permeable, so "small swells will tend to somewhat surge into the reef rather than shoal and break," while larger swells "ride over" bottom imperfections (exposed rocks, steps in the wave face) and shoal/break further up the reef — which is why the reef reportedly performs much better on bigger swells than on the everyday sub-1 m swells that dominate the local wave climate [R8].
- He compares Albany unfavourably to the Palm Beach (Gold Coast) reef, which he says "mitigated the risk of injury by lowering the level of the reef, i.e. deeper water over the rocks" — implying Albany's shallower crest (−1.0 m AHD, chosen partly to "maximise wave breaking") is a direct trade-off between wave quality and user safety [R8][R3].

City of Albany's own framing (via Mayor Greg Stocks, reported by Beachgrit) locates the safety problem less in the engineering and more in surfer behaviour: "inexperienced surfers taking on quite elite waves... the temptation to go there above your skill level is probably contributing to some of that" [R7].

## Unexpected results

**Injuries and the 2025–26 "detuning" episode.** This is the most significant unexpected outcome found in the sources. Within the reef's first weeks of operation, Swellnet forum posts from Albany locals already described injuries:
- "soggydog" (17 July 2025): "It's shallow, shallow enough to catch your fins/board spinning for a wave if you are in the wrong spot... Visitor had a good face injury yesterday. The rocks are sharp, feet are pretty cut up... It's not a beginner wave. There are dangers. But it's pretty fucking epic when you get a good one." [R8]
- "Baldhead" (17 July 2025): "On solid days, long period swells the sets draw a lot of water off the reef as they come in. The take-off is sucky, you're looking down at granite boulders gurgling. Sometimes sticking out of the water... If you get pitched there's a chance you'll be pinballed through granite boulders... There's been a number of injuries — broken bones, gashes, concussion. There's a hardy crew on it." [R8]

By October 2025, Beachgrit reported the reef was being considered for "detuning" — "busting up a couple of the rocks in the shallower part of the wave" — following a "spate of injuries on beginner surfers who can't handle its juice," attributed by Mayor Greg Stocks mainly to "inexperienced surfers taking on quite elite waves" [R7]. By March 2026, the City of Albany had carried out a repositioning of 18 rocks specifically to reduce user injuries, at a cost of more than AUD $20,000 [R11, cost figure via search-engine synthesis of that same article — see caveat above]. Swellnet poster "steveb" (4 August 2025) argued this represented a public-liability and facility-design failure distinct from ordinary "surfing has risks" framing, comparing the reef to a council-managed playground or skate park where "public safety is the number one priority" and arguing the shallow, uncontrolled rock surface created "unacceptable safety hazards" [R8].

**Wave performance exceeding the design target.** Multiple outlets independently report that the built reef's surfability exceeded the 41%-of-the-year design target "by far" [R2][R6], though this is reported qualitatively, not as a precisely re-measured percentage in any source fetched.

**Reliable secondary breaks.** An unplanned/unmodelled benefit reported by a local surfer: the reef "has created reliable banks either side when it's too small for the reef proper," giving surfable options even when the engineered reef itself isn't working [R8].

**Crowd/community effects.** Coverage frames the reef as a community and youth-access success rather than reporting negative crowd/localism outcomes: Bolt describes watching "the kids come down here before school, in the dark, to get a wave before they go to school, and then they zip down here again after school" [R2]. One forum poster reports modest crowding (7 people) on peak swell days but notes a cooperative, familiar line-up rather than conflict [R8].

**Shark risk.** Raised as a live concern by Swellnet commenters given the reef sits further offshore than the existing beach break and Albany's south coast is known great-white-shark habitat, but no confirmed shark-related incident at the reef was found in the sources fetched [R8].

## What could have been done better

**Authors'/engineers' view.** The clearest documented critique comes from "steveb" on Swellnet, an engineer with public-recreation-facility design experience who runs a separate concept site (offshoresurfreefs.com) proposing prefabricated reef forms. His argument: rock reefs like Albany's cannot achieve both (a) accurate enough bottom contours to reliably break small, everyday swells, and (b) a smooth, non-injurious surface, because the armour rock must be large and heavy for stability. His proposed alternative is a fully prefabricated reef structure — built onshore in a shipyard, floated out, and installed by controlled submergence onto pre-installed footings — which he argues could achieve "100% accurate shape and level, no subsidence, no settlement, no sharp edges, no possible entrapment points... faster install... cleaner (no silt plume), and possibly less expensive" than a rock-dumped reef, while citing offshore-construction materials with a "safe working life in excess of 100 years" [R8]. He also explicitly cites Palm Beach (Gold Coast) as an example of a rock reef where the injury risk was mitigated at the design stage by keeping the crest deeper (more water over the rocks), implying Albany's choice to prioritise wave quality via a shallower crest (crest at −1.0 m AHD, "to maximise wave breaking whilst also ensuring adequate user safety" per the official design description) under-weighted the safety trade-off relative to Palm Beach [R8][R3].

**Surfers' view.** Local surfer sentiment in the sources fetched is overwhelmingly positive about the wave itself and does not, in the main, call for the wave to be made easier or safer — several posters push back against "detuning" concerns as inherent to a legitimately world-class wave, and Mayor Stocks reports that "surfers acknowledge that there's risk, and all the surfing community that I've spoken to are delighted with the surf reef and accept that risk when they go there," while noting risk-averse surfers "can go and surf on the inside breaks which have formed" instead [R7]. The main surfer-side complaint found is more about information/consistency than safety: repeated, unanswered forum questions (Aug–Oct 2025) asking whether the reef reliably breaks well on the small, sub-1 m swells that make up most of Middleton Beach's actual wave climate, versus only being demonstrated in photos/video on larger swells [R8].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

**Tide range.** King George Sound (which includes Albany's Middleton Beach) has a mean tidal range of only 0.4 m, with a spring tidal range of 1.1 m [R5]. This is a genuinely micro-tidal environment, which — per Raised Water Research's description of the reef's own wave requirements ("minimal tidal fluctuations... maximises breaking duration") — is treated as a positive design factor at Albany [R10][R5]. The Mediterranean at Haifa is also effectively micro-tidal (tidal range on the order of tens of centimetres, well under 1 m), so on this specific variable the two sites are comparable: both avoid the large intertidal exposure/burial swings that complicate reef design in macro-tidal locations. (No source fetched in this research gives a numeric Haifa/Mediterranean tidal-range figure; this is a qualitative, not sourced-to-a-fetched-URL, comparison — flagged (unreferenced — needs source) for the Haifa figure specifically.)

**Wave climate.** Albany's reef sits on the Southern Ocean coast of Western Australia, exposed to consistent, long-period Southern Ocean groundswell (12–16 second periods cited by Raised Water Research as ideal) [R10] — a fundamentally different, much more energetic wave climate than Haifa's, which per Lior's own field measurements at the Shikmona buoy (Haifa) sees "minimum surfable" conditions defined at only 0.45–0.6 m significant wave height and 6–8 second periods, with a narrow, WNW-dominated (284–294°) swell window and marginal ("borderline") surf conditions common even within that window [R12, Lior's notes]. Albany's problem was "closeouts most of the year" from an ample but poorly-shaped swell; Haifa/Israel's problem, per Lior's own Haifa municipal proposal document, is swell scarcity — "less than 1 day/week" with naturally surfable conditions [R13, Lior's notes]. This means the core engineering lesson that transfers from Albany is about bottom-shape accuracy and safety trade-offs in rock construction (see "Why it worked / failed" above), not about wave-energy input — Albany did not need to concentrate or amplify swell energy the way Lior's Haifa headland-refraction concept proposes; it needed to reshape ample existing energy into a rideable, non-closeout wave.

**What transfers.** (1) The rock-vs-geotextile lesson is directly transferable: build with graded, precision-placed rock, not sand-filled textile bags. (2) The safety/shape-accuracy trade-off documented at Albany (shallow crest = better wave, more injury risk; deeper crest as at Palm Beach = safer, less punchy wave) is a first-order design decision any Haifa project would also face. (3) Albany's ~30-year advocacy-to-completion timeline, and its funding model (spanning federal, state, city, and a $5,000 local-club contribution) illustrate the multi-decade, multi-level-government financing pattern a Haifa project should expect to plan for. (4) Because Haifa's wave climate is swell-starved rather than swell-abundant and closeout-prone, Albany is a better precedent for "how to safely engineer a rock reef" than for "how much swell energy a Haifa reef would need to work with" — for the latter, Lior's own Gold Coast/Narrowneck comparison (erosion-driven design with surf as a byproduct) or a wave-energy-concentrating concept may be more directly analogous [R13, Lior's notes].
