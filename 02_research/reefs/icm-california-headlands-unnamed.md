## Motivation and who pushed for it

Oceanside's beaches have eroded for decades, a problem the U.S. federal government itself has acknowledged causing: jetties built at Camp Pendleton Harbor in 1942 blocked the natural south-to-north longshore sand supply, and in 1953 the federal government formally admitted the harbor construction was the cause of the erosion — Army Corps estimates put the resulting sand loss at 1.4–1.6 million cubic yards since 1942, with some stretches retreating up to 6.6 feet/year [R13]. Congress ordered an Army Corps study in 2000 that was not delivered until June 2026 — 26 years late — and its recommended fix (dredge-and-place nourishment, no headlands/reef) is costed at $243.5 million with sand delivery potentially "another couple decades" away even if funded [R13][R14].

Frustrated by that federal delay, the City of Oceanside ran its own international design competition (RE:BEACH, launched 2023, three finalist teams: Deltares/MVRDV, ICM, SCAPE) specifically to find a faster, self-funded alternative that stabilizes sand rather than just replacing it repeatedly [R8][R13]. A 15-member jury of coastal engineers, marine experts, community representatives and city officials selected ICM's "Living Speed Bumps" design in January 2024, citing ICM's decades of Gold Coast, Australia experience with similar headland/reef combinations as a "proven track record" [R8][R12]. Local advocacy group **Save Oceanside Sand (SOS)**, led by Robert Ashton, has publicly pushed for faster, permanent action, framing the erosion as decades of city inaction: *"We've been losing our beach over decades and the city's really done nothing about it until recently"* [R12]. Neighboring Carlsbad has expressed public skepticism about the project (headline only found — full article text behind a paywall/JS block, not independently verified: "Carlsbad's skepticism remains over Oceanside's beach sand project," San Diego Union-Tribune, ~Aug 2026 — content not accessible, flagged as "(unreferenced — needs source)" for the specific concerns) [R17].

## Design as planned

ICM's winning concept combines two "compact, rounded" onshore headlands (at Tyson Street Park and Wisconsin Avenue) with a single offshore, "butterfly-shaped" artificial reef about 900 ft from shore in 15–20 ft of water, plus targeted beach and nearshore sand nourishment [R4][R9][R12][R15]. The explicit design intent, per Oceanside's Coastal Zone Administrator Jayme Timberlake, is retention rather than replacement: *"Nothing really helps retain the sand, so that's the intent of the artificial reef and the two headlands"* [R15]. The headlands are deliberately NOT built to the length of "traditional groins," which the project's own FAQ says are "shore-perpendicular structures designed to trap sand from within the littoral cell" and fully interrupt longshore drift; instead they are shorter and designed to work together with the reef and placed sand, allowing longshore transport to continue at a reduced rate [R6] — this matches the transcript's characterization of the design philosophy (headlands as an alternative to a full groin field) even though no source confirms an existing physical groin field was removed at this specific site.

The reef shape is also explicitly a surf-amenity feature, not purely protective: Timberlake states *"The reef shape is really about bringing more surfing amenities to the area and also retaining sand"* [R15], and the design was informed by extensive testing — 900+ numerical simulations across 45+ reef configurations, followed by 1:35-scale physical wave-basin testing (thousands of wave cycles) at Oregon State University's O.H. Hinsdale Wave Research Laboratory (a 160 ft × 87 ft directional wave basin), evaluating wave height, swell direction, tidal variation, sand retention and surf peel/transmission [R6][R9][R12]. ICM's promotional material frames the approach as "nature-based engineering," explicitly citing its own Narrowneck Reef, Gold Coast, Australia as the proven precedent [R4][R8].

## What was actually built, and how it differed from the design

Nothing has been physically constructed as of the research date (2026-09-24) — the project is still in the detailed-design/permitting/environmental-review phase, so there is no "as-built vs. as-designed" comparison to report yet [R6][R9][R11]. The timeline itself has already shifted at least once: an early news report (ABC 10News, referencing testing ongoing "now") cites a design report expected in June and a potential construction start "Fall 2027," pending funding and permits [R12]; a later item (Yahoo News, June 2026) gives permitting of "up to 18 months" and a construction start of "late 2027 or early 2028" [R15]; and the CEQA Notice of Preparation for the Draft EIR was only published in October 2025, with the comment period closing Nov 17, 2025 [R11] — meaning as of the most recent sources found, environmental review, not construction, is the current bottleneck. Full construction financing (beyond the $1.8M Coastal Commission monitoring grant) does not appear to be fully secured [R11][R13].

## Relevance to Israel / Haifa

- **Tide range:** Oceanside sits on the Pacific Ocean, which has a mixed, semidiurnal tidal regime with a typical San Diego-area range on the order of ~1.5–2 m (spring range up to ~2.3 m) — general regional knowledge, not sourced to a specific document in this research pass; flagged "(unreferenced — needs source)" if an exact figure is required. This is far larger than the Mediterranean's near-zero (<0.3–0.5 m) tide range at Haifa, which matters directly for headland/reef crest-depth design: a structure sized to work across a ~2 m tidal swing at Oceanside would need very different crest-depth tolerances at microtidal Haifa, where the "no breaking / breaking / min depth" band is much narrower and more sensitive to exact placement (compare Lior's own Haifa design-depth bands in `02_research/00_master_list.md` §3, which specify sub-0.5 m depth-band tolerances).
- **Wave climate:** Oceanside faces the open Pacific with a mixed wind-swell and long-period groundswell climate (the site was chosen and tested partly *because* it already has meaningful, surfable wave energy needing management) — comparable in spirit to Haifa's smaller Mediterranean wind-swell climate, but the Pacific's swell energy is generally much larger, so the "butterfly reef" surf-enhancement KPI at Oceanside is optimizing an already-surfable coast, not creating a wave from a marginal one, unlike Lior's Haifa concept.
- **What transfers:** the core design logic — short, rounded headlands (instead of full groins) plus a reef, sized and tested via combined numerical + physical (scale-model wave-basin) modelling before construction, explicitly justified as preserving longshore drift and horizon views while adding sand retention — is directly relevant methodology for any Haifa proposal, independent of tide range: it is the same "let some transport continue, add retention rather than a full barrier" logic seen in the UAE submerged-reef case in the same ICM talk (`icm-uae-submerged-reef.md`) and in Lior's own pitch. The heavy reliance on physical wave-basin testing before construction (Oregon State University, 1:35 scale) is also a transferable process lesson: this project did not proceed to construction on modelling alone.
