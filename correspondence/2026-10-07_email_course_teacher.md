I am writing to update you on my final project, a survey of artificial surf reefs worldwide, and to ask whether you think I am on the right track.

AIM
The project asks why artificial surf reefs succeeded or failed, in order to inform a possible reef at Haifa, where the tidal range is small. For each reef I document:
- year and location;
- cost and financing;
- motivation;
- design versus as-built structure;
- outcome and its causes;
- lessons and published reviews.
Every fact is linked to a cited source.

WHAT I HAVE BUILT SO FAR
The results are presented as an interactive web page with four sections:
- Gallery: a card and a referenced dossier for each of 13 artificial surf reefs, plus 10 related coastal structures. The reefs were built between 1999 and 2026 in Australia, New Zealand, the United States, the United Kingdom, India and Mexico.
- Map: the locations of all sites.
- Table: a side-by-side comparison of the reefs across the fields above.
- 3D models: reconstructions of six reefs as built (Borth, Boscombe, Bunbury, Mount Maunganui, Palm Beach and Pratte's).
  - Each model includes the surrounding seabed and local tide levels, and is shown next to the photographs and drawings it was built from.
  - The reefs can be compared side by side at the same scale.
  - Where sources disagree, the alternative outlines (survey, design drawing, photograph) can be switched.
  - A model can be aligned with an aerial photograph to check it visually.
  - Each model has its own methods, caveats and references.

SOURCES
The 13 reef dossiers draw on more than 300 distinct sources, including:
- peer-reviewed and conference papers (e.g. Mead, Black & Moores 2007; Mead & Borrero 2011; Bar & Drimer 2023; Corbett et al. 2023), a PhD thesis (Rendle 2016, University of Plymouth) and a technical review (Blacka et al. 2013, UNSW Water Research Laboratory);
- monitoring studies and evaluations (e.g. Jackson et al.'s 11-year monitoring of Narrowneck; Davidson's 2010 interim evaluation of Boscombe);
- official survey data: Welsh Government LiDAR (2022), EMODnet bathymetry and Channel Coastal Observatory beach profiles;
- design drawings, satellite and aerial imagery, nautical charts, council records, news and surf-community reports.
In total, 294 images are archived, each with a full citation and a note of how it was used, and 42 videos are catalogued.

VALIDATION
- A fixed hierarchy of evidence:
  - measured surveys over design drawings over photographs;
  - peer-reviewed or official sources over press and developer claims, which are labelled as such.
- Independent re-verification: every fact and every traced outline was checked a second time, separately, against the original source. An earlier AI-generated survey of the same reefs was used only as a lead, and re-checking it exposed several errors.
- Cross-checks between independent data. For example, Borth's design drawings and the 2022 LiDAR survey agree on crest level to within 0.14 ± 0.28 m, and aerial photographs match the traced outlines to within about 2–3 m.
- Reported versus reconstructed quantities. At Mount Maunganui, the reconstruction reproduces the surveyed as-built volume of about 2,800 m³ (against 6,500 m³ designed; Mead, Black & Moores 2007) only if the seabed lies near −3.5 m chart datum. This is stated as an assumption.
- All levels are converted to local mean sea level with stated equations. Each model carries a confidence rating with its reason, and the unknowns are listed.

PRELIMINARY CONCLUSIONS
1. Material matters most in this sample.
   - Rock reefs (Cables, Burkitts, Albany, Palm Beach) have the best surfing record. Borth's rock reef protects the coast, though its surfing benefit is contested.
   - Most sand-filled geotextile reefs (Pratte's, Opunake, Mount Maunganui, Boscombe, Kovalam) did not deliver the promised waves. Several were damaged, and Pratte's and, in part, Mount Maunganui were removed.
   - The inflatable Bunbury reef failed during installation.
2. Coastal-protection goals were met more reliably than surfing goals. Narrowneck, for example, was judged a protection success by its 11-year monitoring.
3. The as-built structure often differs substantially from the design: Mount Maunganui reached about 43% of its design volume. Performance must therefore be judged against what was actually built.
4. Independent monitoring is rare, and many claims of success come only from developers. Boscombe's independent interim evaluation found it met 4 of its 11 design objectives.
The next stage applies these lessons to the Israeli coast (sediment transport) and to Haifa, where the small tidal range keeps the reef's depth below the water surface nearly constant.

I used AI (Claude) as a research and engineering assistant, within a structured workflow that I set up and directed. Separate AI agents searched for sources, traced reef outlines from images, built the 3D models and re-checked one another's work. They worked under rules I defined:
- every number is traceable to its source;
- every result is independently re-verified;

My own part was to:
- define the research question and the method;
- supply key papers and material;
- make the modelling decisions (for example, which outline is the default and which seabed level to assume);
- review and verify the results, correcting them where needed.
I have studied the material so that I can explain and defend every part of it.

I believe this way of working is itself part of the project's contribution. It produced results that a single student could hardly produce by hand within a course:
